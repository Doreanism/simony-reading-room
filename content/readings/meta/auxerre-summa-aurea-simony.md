William of Auxerre's treatise on simony, occupying the second half of Book III, Tractatus XXI of the *Summa aurea* (c. 1220s). Following the discussion of usury that opens the tractate, Auxerre turns to simony with the question *quid sit symonia*, defining it as "the deliberate will to buy or sell something spiritual or annexed to a spiritual thing." The treatise then proceeds through a series of questions: whether a secular judge selling justice commits simony; whether preaching for temporal gain is simoniacal; the sale of sacraments and the canonical gift-types (*munus a manu, a lingua, ab obsequio*); the conferral of prebends for money; the sale of schools; whether the decretists err on simony; the sale of testimony; and whether the right of patronage may be sold. Throughout, Auxerre weighs Scripture, the *Decretum*, and the Parisian theological tradition, marking an important early-scholastic moment in the transition from canonistic to systematic-theological treatment of simony.
