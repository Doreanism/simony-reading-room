— Nevertheless, it seems that one must say otherwise: that although it is a grave sin, it is not simoniacal; and although something annexed to a spiritual good is given for a temporal thing as for an end or motive, it is not, however, given as for a price, and so it is not simony. But if it be objected that he does not give freely, since he does not give solely for God’s sake, it must be said that he gives freely in this sense, that he gives for no price.

\[Reply to the objections\]: 1. To what is objected first, it must be said that the case is not parallel. In the one case, the conferral of the prebend would be made in consideration of a price, whereas in the case proposed it is not made in consideration of a price that the bishop himself might receive. For when a prebend is conferred on a kinsman so that the kinsman himself may receive the fruits, there is no disorder in the fact that the kinsman receives the fruits after the conferral: for by right they pertain to him.

2. To the second it must be said that, although it is simoniacal to confer a prebend out of love of money, it is not so out of love of flesh. For when it is done out of love of money, the intention is that something pass back in some way to the one conferring it from the one upon whom it is conferred; but it is not so when it is done out of love of flesh — rather, the intention is that the whole remain with the one upon whom it is conferred.

3. To the third it must be said that what Bede says is to be understood of cases where there is, or is expected to be, human recompense on account of “the grace of knowledge or of some other gift”: for in such matters the intention ought to be simple, nor should the recompense of a gift be sought.

4. To the fourth it must be said that what Gregory says is to be understood of cases where tithes are given to lay kinsmen: for then it is said to be graver. For it is more difficult to wrest them back, and on account of the greater proneness \[of laymen to retain them\] the church suffers harm.
