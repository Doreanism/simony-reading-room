To the contrary. a. *Extra*, *De simonia*, *Nemo presbyterorum*, where it is said that not only by the intervention of money or temporal gain, but also “out of regard for the person and for kinship or familiarity,” it “is simoniacal” for “the bishop” or prelate himself to conceal the public sin of his own subject.

\[Solution\]: This must be conceded. Hence, if priests “refuse to make known to the bishop” a subject who sins publicly and is incorrigible, for the reasons stated above, simony is committed. But if it be asked what that spiritual thing is which is set under a price, it must be said that this is the revelation of the sin, to which they were bound by office; for the concealment which is perpetrated is not what is sold, but rather the revelation itself which is omitted.

II. Next the question is asked: if someone gives money or something else to a layman who has no right in the election but is maliciously impeding it, so that he may cease from such impediment, whether this is simony.

And it seems that it is, a. from what is said in *Extra*, *De simonia*, *De hoc autem*, where it is said that “you ought to persuade kings and princes that, if they have received anything from the aforesaid bishop so as to give their assent to his election, they should restore it without diminution to the Church of Osma, since they cannot retain \[it\] without grave peril to their salvation.”

But the contrary seems true, 1. For he gave in order to redeem his vexation; but it is licit to redeem one’s vexation; therefore it was licit to give money so that the other should cease from such impediment.

\[Solution\]: To this the answer is given by a certain distinction: either this is after the right has been acquired by him, and in that case it seems to be licit to redeem his vexation
