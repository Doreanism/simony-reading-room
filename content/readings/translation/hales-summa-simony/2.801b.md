b. To the second, however, it must be said that, although it is possible to buy eternal goods, since they fall under merit — for to buy them is nothing other than to merit them: since to give temporal things out of charity is meritorious — nevertheless first grace, by which spiritual goods are given, as in a sacrament and the like, does not fall under merit; therefore the case is not the same with first grace, which no one can merit *ex condigno*: hence the giving of temporal things, which is outside grace, cannot attain to it.

Hence Ephron is rebuked, who sold to Abraham the field in which was the double cave in which the dead were buried; and as a sign of this rebuke his name was changed; whence, although he was at first called ‘Ephron,’ afterwards “he was called Ephran; in which,” as Jerome says, “it is signified what sort of perfect virtue he must have, who could sell the memorials of the dead.” It therefore seems that he committed the sin of simony.

But to the contrary. 1. All that he sold is temporal; therefore it is neither spiritual nor annexed to a spiritual thing; therefore he did not commit simony.

2. Likewise, if he committed simony, then he sold what was not to be sold; therefore Abraham bought what was not to be bought; therefore he committed simony in buying just as Ephron did in selling.

3. Likewise, Abraham seems to have sinned more than Ephron, because Ephron wished to give him the field freely for the burial of his dead, and Abraham was unwilling to accept it except with payment intervening; therefore he scandalized him, and thus seems to have sinned more than Ephron.

\[Solution\]: 1-3. To this some say that Abraham bought a certain large field in which was that great cave in which the dead were buried; whence he did not sin, because that cave passed along with the whole property, just as, when someone buys an estate to which is annexed the right of patronage of some church, he does not commit simony, because the right of patronage passes along with that whole property.
