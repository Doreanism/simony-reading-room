III. Next the question is asked whether bishops or other prelates, who receive the revenues of vacant churches and afterwards confer them, retaining for themselves a part of the proceeds, commit simony.

\[Solution\]: For understanding this, the following distinction is applied: either he “who gives an ecclesiastical benefice received its proceeds before the donation” or he did not. “If he did not receive them, but obtains them as a consequence of the donation,” it is reckoned simoniacal. “But if he did receive the proceeds before the donation,” either “he established before the donation that they should be retained for a time for a just and necessary cause” or “he agreed with the one receiving the benefice or with a mediator that he would receive the proceeds once the donation was made.” If in the first way, it could be licit; if in the second way, it is reckoned simoniacal, as is held in Extra, Ut ecclesiastica beneficia, Ut nostrum, where this is plainly determined.

IV. Next the question is asked concerning one who offers his goods to the Church simply, “yet with this intention” principally, “that by the temporal goods which he offers he may be able to obtain spiritual ones,” and the monastery would not otherwise admit “him unless it received temporal advantages,” whether this is simony.

\[Solution\]: To this it can be answered that, if the church or monastery is well-provided for, and the intention alone — that is, a deliberate will — makes a man a simoniac, it is reckoned to be simoniacal. But if the monastery has been weighed down by poverty and the intention is not principally directed to the temporal goods themselves, but only secondarily, it is not believed to be simony; but if the intention were principally directed there, it is reckoned as simony.
