Therefore to use temporal goods in this last way is a sin, but in the first four ways it is not a sin. Therefore the signification of the preposition “for” in this statement must be distinguished: when it is said, “the Church chants a mass for temporal goods.” For if it denotes the end of enjoyment, it is false. But if it denotes necessary subject-matter, etc., it is true. For this is true: “the Church chants masses for temporal goods,” understood in this way: in the office of the mass the Church requests temporal goods as necessary, etc., and it does not follow, “Therefore she sets a spiritual good for a temporal price.” For this would only follow if the preposition “for” in the statement denoted the end of enjoyment: which is not to be conceded. “The Church consecrates for temporal goods,” because temporal goods are not the end of the consecration in this way; rather, the body of Christ is the matter of the consecration. Whence this argument is invalid: the Church chants a mass for temporal goods — because in the office of the mass, according to some part of it, temporal goods can be the matter of prayer, but not so in the consecration. The statement could however be conceded per accidens: “the Church consecrates for temporal goods,” with the preposition “for” denoting the cause — not the matter of the consecration itself, but of the intention to consecrate. For on account of the temporal goods received, the priest is bound to consecrate. The solution to the first objection is thus clear. To the second objection we say that the Church praises God as for provocative subject-matter, and thus it is clear that she does not set spiritual goods under a temporal price. Whence the temporal goods do not stand there as a mercenary cause. To the third objection we say that to rise for matins or to hurry to church is not a spiritual thing. Whence it is permissible to do these things for temporal goods. But to chant in church or to praise God for temporal goods as for an end of enjoyment or as mercenary subject-matter is simony. To the fourth objection we say that this statement is twofold: “the laity are bound to give temporal goods for spiritual goods” — and this qualification “for spiritual goods” can qualify either the verb “are bound” or the verb “to give.” If it qualifies the verb “are bound,” it is true, insofar as the preposition “for” denotes efficient cause, since the spiritual goods which are given by the priests are the efficient cause why the laity are bound to give them temporal goods. But this is not to buy spiritual goods. If, however, it qualifies the verb “to give,” it is again true, insofar as the preposition “for” denotes final cause, for the spiritual goods are the end of the temporal goods, but not their price: whence there is no purchase there. Similarly this statement is twofold: “priests are bound to give spiritual goods for temporal goods.” If the qualification “for temporal goods” qualifies the verb “are bound,” it is true, insofar as the preposition “for” denotes motivating efficient cause. But if it qualifies the verb “to give,” it is false, because the preposition “for” denotes either final or material cause, and temporal goods are not the end of spiritual goods. And so the reasoning is not the same for priests as for the laity. It is therefore lawful to give temporal goods for spiritual goods, whether the preposition denotes final or motivating cause. But this is not to be understood of those spiritual goods which are eternal, but of the spiritual goods which the priest gives. There is a doubt whether it is lawful to give temporal goods for those spiritual goods as a final end, or to buy them. And it seems that it is: for at the end of Proverbs \[Prov. 31:16\] it says of the valiant woman: “She considered a field and bought it.” Therefore it is lawful to buy the field of paradise or the heavenly inheritance. Similarly in the gospel \[Matt. 13:45-46\]: “The kingdom of heaven is like a merchant seeking goodly pearls: and having found one precious pearl, he went and sold all that he had and bought it.” Thus, therefore, it is lawful to buy the kingdom of heaven and to give temporal goods for it. Therefore much more is it lawful to give temporal goods for the spiritual goods which the priest gives, which are less spiritual than the kingdom of heaven. Likewise it is lawful to give temporal goods to certain good religious men so that they may pray for us; but prayer is spiritual. Whence \[Matt. 7:7\]: “Ask, and you shall receive” — which is understood of the petition by which the Holy Spirit is requested, for it is not true of the petition for temporal goods: for many request temporal goods which they do not receive and are not going to receive; but he who asks the Holy Spirit from a true heart will receive him. Therefore the Holy Spirit is given through prayer. Therefore prayer is a spiritual thing. Therefore it is lawful to give temporal goods for prayer, which is spiritual.
