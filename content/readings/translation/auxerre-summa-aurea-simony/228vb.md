This the Cantor of Paris and all his followers conceded. Jeremiah: “Cursed is the man who does the work of God fraudulently” \[Jeremiah 48:10\]. On which passage the authority says that he does the work of God fraudulently who does it for a gift.

Now there is a threefold gift \[munus\]: the gift of the heart, namely human favor; the gift of the tongue, namely human praise; and the gift of the hand. But besides these the Cantor posited two others, namely the gift of blood — “woe indeed to those who build Sion in blood” \[Micah 3:10\] — and the gift of fear, when the bishop gives a prebend through the violence and entreaties of princes. If for any of these gifts the bishop gives a prebend, he commits simony, according to the Cantor. Hence he said that this is not the true definition of simony: “Simony is a deliberate will to sell” etc., but it should be defined thus: “Simony is the conferral or acceptance of spiritual things not freely given.”

This solution seems to be true. For suppose that the same bishop gives a prebend to someone for money, and to his kinsman gives another solely on account of kinship: he gives both of those prebends out of the same cupidity, which is proved thus — because out of the same charity I love the highest good for myself and for my neighbor; therefore out of the same cupidity that bishop loves money for himself and for his kinsman; therefore the sin is the same in the giving of each prebend; if in the giving of the prebend for money there is the sin of simony, therefore in the giving solely on account of kinship there is the sin of simony.

But to the contrary: in the Acts of the Apostles, when Simon Magus offered money so that \[Peter\] might give him this power, that on whomever he laid hands \[that person\] might receive the Holy Spirit, Peter said: “May your money be with you unto perdition, because you thought the gift of God could be possessed with money” \[Acts 8:20\]. Simon, then, wished to buy the gift of the Holy Spirit, and on this account he committed the sin of simony. Likewise Gehazi wished to sell the gift of the Holy Spirit to Naaman the Syrian, and on this account he committed the sin of simony. And in Sacred Scripture we have no other examples of simony than these two. But in those two examples the proper object of the evil will is to buy or sell a spiritual thing or something annexed to a spiritual thing; therefore, since wills differ in species by their proper objects, and in the proper simoniacal will the proper object willed is to buy or sell a spiritual thing or something annexed to a spiritual thing — whereas in the will of the bishop who gives a prebend to a kinsman solely on account of kinship the object willed is not to buy or sell, etc. — therefore his will is not of the same species as the simoniacal will; therefore he does not commit simony.

This we concede, insofar as simony is taken properly; he sins mortally nonetheless, because “woe to those who build Sion in blood,” and in him there is a threefold sin. The first is excessive love of his own flesh. The second is cupidity, because he covets temporal things too much for his kinsman. The third is contempt for spiritual things, which he gives solely in view of a temporal thing.

To the first objection we say that this argument is not valid: “He gives something annexed to a spiritual thing for a temporal thing; therefore he commits simony.” Rather, there is a fallacy of equivocation, because in the premise the word “for” denotes a final or efficient cause and matter \[i.e. a material cause\], but one proceeds as if it denoted the cause of a commercial exchange. For such a cause properly constitutes the sin of simony. To the second objection we say that this argument is not valid: “He does not freely love God for God’s own sake alone; therefore he does not freely give a prebend, who does not give it solely for the sake of God.” And the point is: to give a church freely is not to give it solely for the sake of God, but to give it so that nothing be received from it in return; and this sometimes happens solely from natural piety.
