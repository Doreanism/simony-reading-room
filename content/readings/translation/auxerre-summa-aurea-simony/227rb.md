Therefore they are buying the sacraments of the Eucharist; and so they are buying a sacrament of the church; therefore they are committing simony. Likewise, the church that makes a pact concerning the celebration of an anniversary in exchange for certain revenues is selling the sacraments of the church, and so is committing simony. Likewise an objection is raised concerning priests who take a church on lease from scholars who go off to the schools, and enter into a pact with the scholars for a fixed sum of money to serve in the church — to sing masses, to baptize, and to administer the other sacraments of the church to the people. These priests seem to be selling the sacraments of the church, for otherwise they would not sing masses and so forth unless so much money were given to them. Therefore they are committing simony, and therefore the church that tolerates this is sinning. But there is also an objection concerning prelates who receive money from the excommunicated before they absolve them, for they seem to be selling the sacraments of the church to those who had been excommunicated. Therefore they are committing simony. Solution. The church takes money for three reasons. The first is on account of devotion. The second is for the support and stipend of the ministers of the church. The third is for the sake of correction. According to the first reason the first objection is resolved; but to make this solution clear, it must be noted that the Lord said to Moses, “Make for me a tabernacle according to the pattern that was shown to you on the mountain,” by which it is signified that the church militant ought to imitate the church triumphant as much as it can; and thus do the saints who are on earth — they imitate, then, those who are in the fatherland as much as they can. Hence Ecclesiasticus: “the birds gather to their like.” The birds are the saints who are on earth, who fly with the wings of the virtues; and they gather to their like — that is, they imitate the saints who are in the fatherland. For with those who exist in the church triumphant devotion is highly pleasing, and with them it counts for much. For the Lord does not weigh how much, but out of how much, as the gloss says on that passage in Luke, “the poor little woman cast two small coins into the treasury.” For the gift itself, taken in itself, is not pleasing to God; rather, what pleases is the devotion of the giver, and the gift on account of the devotion. Hence, on account of the devotion that Tobias had in burying the dead, the angel offered up his prayer to God. And the Apostle, imitating the church triumphant, said, “I do not seek the gift, but the fruit”; and the fruit is in devotion. On account of devotion, then, which is spiritual, the kingdom of heaven is acquired — not on account of money given taken in itself. To the first objection, then, we say that the church ought to celebrate that man’s anniversary not for the revenues, but for the devotion of the penitent. Yet because the devotion of the penitent is not known to the church militant, it can receive the revenues which he gives either as a sign in memory of his devotion, or as a cause provoking the devotion of the clerics who are present at the anniversary; for the distributions which are made in the choir provoke and stir up devotion in those who receive the pennies. He who wishes his anniversary to be celebrated ought to say it thus: “I ask for the suffrages of your church out of devotion; I ask for the suffrages, and as a sign of devotion I give you so much.” But if he has regard for the money as for the principal cause on account of which the anniversary is to be celebrated, it is simony. We do not, however, say that this is what happens, but only what ought to happen. The second objection is resolved through the second reason. For it is lawful for priests who hold a church on lease to receive a fixed sum of money for their support and stipend, for “the laborer is worthy of his hire,” as the Lord says in the Gospel, and likewise the Apostle: “he who serves the altar should live by the altar,” and again, “he who ploughs ought to plough in hope and not for hope.” Hence for this money, regarded as the principal cause, it is not lawful for the leasing priest to sing masses and the like; but it is lawful to do so for money, provided that it is not designated as the cause — not without \[some legitimate\] cause. And the same must be said of bishops and archbishops who receive procurations, and of poor clerics who read through psalters for God’s sake, and of the poor: take the case of the first abbey to which someone comes asking for fraternity, but the abbey does not have the means by which to support him.
