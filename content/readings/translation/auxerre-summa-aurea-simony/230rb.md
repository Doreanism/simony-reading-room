But the contrary of this is false: for kings confer many benefices, and many knights have the right of patronage in churches, and the Church even approves this. Again, a certain decretal says that if some knight sells a village in which a church has the right of patronage, it passes with the whole property. But according to this it seems that that right is sold: for that right does not pass into the dominion of the buyer of the village except in the way the village passes; but the village does not pass except by sale; therefore neither does the right of patronage except by sale; therefore the right of patronage is sold. Again, this whole \[property\] is sold; therefore every part of that whole \[is sold\]; therefore the right of patronage is sold, at least within that whole; therefore there is simony there. Perhaps one will say that it can be sold within the whole, but not in itself. But on the contrary: it is not lawful to sell a prebend and a village together; therefore neither a right of patronage and a village together. Again, the right of patronage is beyond price in itself, and much more beyond price is it within the whole; just as therefore it ought not to be sold in itself, so it ought not to be sold within the whole. Solution. The right of receiving tithes is not spiritual, but annexed to a spiritual thing; for tithes are to be given to the Levites, as Ezekiel 2 \[and\] 2 Chronicles 22 \[teach\], so that they may be free for the law of God. Hence those alone who are free for the law of the Lord ought to live without bodily labor; for after nature was corrupted it was said to man, “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread” \[Gen. 3:19\]. Hence even kings and princes receive from their subjects, because they are bound to labor for them even bodily — namely by defending them and fighting for them, and punishing evildoers; for the Levites are free for the law of God, and for this reason tithes are owed to them without bodily labor. The right of receiving tithes is annexed to a spiritual thing and beyond price; hence simony is committed if it is sold or bought. And thus the solution to the first two objections is plain. To the third objection we reply that this argument does not hold: laymen ought not to have the right of receiving tithes, therefore they cannot have the right of patronage; for they receive nothing from the right of patronage. Moreover, tithes are instituted for another purpose. To the last objection some reply that the right of patronage is not annexed to anything spiritual, nor is it spiritual. For they say that patronage is nothing other than the power, possessed by a layman, of presenting a fit person whom the bishop ought to receive — and he would similarly be bound to receive him if another were to present a fit person. But since we see that some right is acquired by the cleric who is presented if he is a fit person, for this reason we say that the right of patronage is spiritual; therefore it ought not to be sold or bought. Hence if someone buys a village in which there is a right of patronage, and on this account buys it at a higher price, it is simony; but if he holds that right as if for nothing as regards that contract, then it is said to pass with the whole property, and is not a part of the whole that is sold.

## That the simoniac is irregular.

## Another chapter.

Concerning the third chapter we say nothing further except that the simoniac is irregular according to the institution of the Church. We understand this, however, of simony which is subject to the judgment of the Church — that is, of simony which is in the outward act, not of simony which is only in the will. For the Church has instituted that if they do not abstain from selling spiritual things through the fear of God, at least they may abstain through human fear. Such a simoniac is excommunicated with the lesser excommunication, the excommunication of which does not pass over to another person. Again, he is irregular; hence if he carries out his office he sins mortally. Nevertheless, if he consecrates, the thing is consecrated, and if he ordains, the man is ordained, for he has the character whence he imprints it.
