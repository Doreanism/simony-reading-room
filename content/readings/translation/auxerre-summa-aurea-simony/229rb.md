But if this is true, no bishop will ever be convicted of simony, for he will always say that he gave the prebend principally for God, and secondarily for money. On account of this, others have said that it is not lawful to give a prebend either for lawful service or for unlawful service, provided this preposition “for” denotes a final cause; but if it denotes an inciting cause, it is lawful to give a prebend for lawful service — just as someone goes to church at the sound of the bell, and the sound is not properly the cause of his motion, but nevertheless incites him to go. In the same way service incites the bishop to give a prebend. But by the same reasoning money can incite him to give a prebend, and so, as before, the bishop will not be able to be convicted of simony. Solution. Among lawful services, some are temporal or bodily, such as going to Rome, handling lawsuits, and the like; and some are spiritual, such as preaching and singing Mass and the like. For spiritual services it is lawful to give a prebend secondarily and for God principally, so that there be an end under an end; for this is lawful: that man gives a prebend on account of preaching, if the preaching is done for God’s sake, nor is a spiritual thing then placed under a temporal price. But for temporal service it is not lawful to give a prebend such that that service be the final cause, whether principal or secondary. Yet it is true that temporal service incites one to give to one rather than another. Just as we hold that a father is to be loved with greater charity than a nephew, and a nephew than a household member, and a household member more than a stranger, yet kinship is not the proper matter of love, but is a certain inciting cause of love which makes the order of charity — in the same way temporal service incites one to give a prebend, and makes the order of giving prebends: it brings it about that prebends should rather be given to those who serve than to those who do not serve, other things being equal; but service is not the true cause of this. Hence Pope Alexander admonishes a certain bishop to give a prebend to a certain cleric who had faithfully served him, and this not on account of the service but on account of his fidelity: for this is a lawful prebend — it is given on account of his fidelity. Hence the Cantor used to say that the courts of prelates are the proving-grounds of clerics. And it could happen in some case that the giving of money would lawfully incite a bishop to give a prebend to someone, as happened in the case of Bishop Maurice, who gave a prebend to a certain cleric in view of a temporal benefit which the father of that cleric had conferred upon the bishop in his necessity. For works of piety incited the bishop, not the money itself. It must be known, however, that in the prebend itself there are two things: one spiritual, the other corporeal. The spiritual is the brotherhood, and in this, so far as in it lies, the Holy Spirit is given. For the brethren pray for absent brethren, and it is impossible that the prayers of many should not be heard, as Augustinus says. The temporal is the right of receiving the fruits, which is by necessity annexed to that brotherhood; hence without this the prebend cannot exist. The *Decretum* says: “Whoever sells one without which the other does not come about leaves neither unsold.” The burial of the dead is not only a thing annexed to a spiritual thing, but it is itself spiritual, inasmuch as burial is an office that properly pertains to priests. For it contains spiritual prayers by which the priest prays for the soul of the deceased, by which the punishment of sin is remitted. Hence it is simony to sell burial. And on this account priests ought not to demand anything before burial, because it would be either simony or at the least the appearance of evil. But after burial they can demand \[something\] where there is such a custom — yet not for the burial, but because it is owed to them.
