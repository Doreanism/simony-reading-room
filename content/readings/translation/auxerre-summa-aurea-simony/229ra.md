To the third objection we reply that this is false: “that bishop puts what is beyond price under a price,” because there is no commerce there, since he intends to acquire nothing through that giving. To the fourth objection we reply that this argument does not hold: if he were to give a prebend to someone so that his \[the recipient’s\] father might have its temporal goods, he would commit simony; therefore if he gives it to a kinsman so that the kinsman may have the temporal goods, he commits simony. And this is so because in the first case there is commerce, in the second there is not. To the last objection we reply that this argument does not hold: from that cupidity he gives a prebend for money, and he gives a prebend to a kinsman on account of blood; therefore if there is simony in the one giving, there is simony also in the other. And this is so because from one and the same cupidity diverse vices can arise, for the root of all evils is cupidity. Concerning this an objection is commonly raised about Gehazi. For it is shown that Elisha had given \[something\]; but he had given only bodily health, which is not a spiritual thing; therefore he did not intend to sell a spiritual thing, nor one annexed to a spiritual thing; therefore he did not commit the sin of simony. Furthermore, if he committed the sin of simony, why is that sin named more from Simon than from Gehazi? Solution. We reply that Gehazi did not intend to sell bodily health, but the giving of that health, which giving was spiritual, that is, a work of the Holy Spirit; and on this account he committed the sin of simony. That sin, however, is named more from Simon than from Gehazi because Simon Magus sinned more gravely than Gehazi: for he sinned in the time of revealed grace, when all things were already manifest, and on this account he sinned more gravely. From the last two cases it is clear that the decretists lie when they say that there is no simony except where there is an outward expressed pact, since in the first simony there was no pact, as is plain in Simon Magus and in Gehazi, who are the first read to have committed simony — the one in selling, namely Gehazi, the other in buying, namely Simon. Likewise, let it be posited that some bishop gives a prebend to a certain cleric who has served him, and that he owes him so much for the service which he rendered. The question is whether that giving is simoniacal. It seems that it is not, because “the laborer is worthy of his hire” \[Luke 10:7\]; and the Apostle says, “Those who serve at the altar ought to live from the altar” \[1 Corinthians 9:13\]; and to Timothy he says, “If anyone does not have care of his own, and especially of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an infidel” \[1 Timothy 5:8\]. Therefore it seems lawful for the bishop to give a prebend for service rendered to him; therefore he does not commit simony in that giving. On the contrary: he gives a spiritual thing and receives a temporal one; therefore he places what is beyond price under a temporal price; therefore he commits simony. To this some have said that service is twofold: lawful service and unlawful service. If he gives a prebend for unlawful service, he commits simony; if for lawful service, he does not. But on the contrary: if it is lawful to give a prebend \[for lawful service\], it is lawful to give it for a payment of money, for where the cause is the same, the effect is also the same; but this is false; therefore the first \[is false also\]. Others have said that it is lawful to give a prebend principally for God, and secondarily for lawful service, so that there be an end under an end. But on the contrary: if we wish to know about some act of will, which one says is good because it is referred to the supreme end, although one must seek through many intermediaries whether it is good or not, a resolution must be made into those intermediaries; and if in such resolution some evil act of will is found, the first was not good, nor was it principally referred to God. According to this teaching of Augustinus, let the resolution be made in this way: that bishop willed to give that man the prebend because he willed to reward him for God’s sake. In such a resolution the first act of will is evil; hence it is not true that he refers it principally to God. But if all are lawful, then the first is lawful too.
