Hence, taken properly, this proposition ought not to be conceded: “It is licit to give temporal things for spiritual things.” But in an improper sense — namely, so that the word “for” denotes not the immediate end but, as it were, a consequent end from the side — it can be conceded in this way: “It is licit to give temporal things in view of an obligation which is brought about on account of the giving of spiritual things.” The obligation is the immediate end of giving the temporal things, and the spiritual things are, as it were, a consequent end from the side. And in this way this authoritative text is to be understood: “Meanwhile, do whatever you can so that God may enlighten your heart; give alms for the sake of God, and in this way God will be moved to enlighten the heart.” It is clear, then, that the enlightening of the heart is not the immediate end of giving alms, but a consecutive end. The immediate end, however, is the pleasing of God, or the love of God, or the precept, or something of that sort. Alms are to be given because it is good to give them, or because it is commanded, or because it is pleasing to God, and because there follows from it the enlightening of the heart. For if the one who gives alms intends that the alms themselves be the price of the enlightening of the heart, there the thought is illicit and the intention utterly evil. It is clear, then, that the word “so that” \[ut\] ought not there to denote the immediate end.

But to the contrary: the sons of Israel said to Aaron, “For this reason we offer among the gifts of the Lord rings, bracelets, and anklets, that you may intercede for us with the Lord” \[Numbers 31:50\]. This was their intention: that they should give those temporal things for prayer, which is spiritual. They were giving those temporal things for spiritual things, and they are praised in that text; therefore it is licit to give temporal things for spiritual things.

Likewise, it seems so from what Judas Maccabeus did, who sent twelve drachmas of silver to Jerusalem so that he might have them offered for the sins of the dead — “for unless \[he hoped\] that those who had fallen” etc. \[2 Maccabees 12:43–45\]; by that offering, then, he caused prayer to be made for the dead, and so he gave temporal things for spiritual things, and he is praised in all things; therefore it is licit to give temporal things for spiritual things.

The solution to these is clear from what has already been said. To the first, then, it must be said that the sons of Israel did not intend that the intercession be the immediate end of giving those ornaments; rather, they offered them among the gifts of the Lord because, namely, they were pleasing to God. That offering had its own immediate end besides intercession — namely, the pleasing of the Lord or the love of the Lord — while the intercession was a consecutive end. We say the same about the offering of Maccabeus: for it had three ends, one immediate, namely the goodness of the offering itself or the pleasing of God, and two consecutive ones: one, the prayer of the priests; the other, the remission of sins which God granted.

## On the simony which is committed concerning things annexed to spiritual things. Third chapter.

Next we must speak of the simony which is committed against things annexed to spiritual things. The question, then, is about a bishop who gives a prebend to some kinsman of his solely on account of kinship — whether he commits simony. It seems that he does not, because there is no will to sell a spiritual thing for a temporal thing; therefore he does not commit simony.

But to the contrary: this bishop gives something annexed to a spiritual thing solely for a temporal thing; therefore he commits simony.

Again, in this precept — “Freely you have received, freely give” \[Matthew 10:8\] — simony is prohibited. But this bishop acts against this precept, which is proved thus: because in that case he does not love God, since he does not serve \[God\] for His own sake; and as for “freely”: if he does not give the prebend for the sake of God, he does not give it freely; therefore this bishop commits simony.
