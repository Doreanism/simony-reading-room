To the third objection the solution lies in the third cause: for a prelate ought not to accept money from an excommunicate in exchange for absolution, but for the sake of penalty and correction, lest he despise excommunication thereafter. For some are better corrected by the emptying of the purse than by other means. It is honorable, however, that the prelate not fill his own purse with that money, but give it to the poor, lest he seem to have accepted it out of cupidity. For if he accepts it out of cupidity, he sins mortally. Likewise objection is raised concerning those cases in which simony seems to occur through the sale of a sacrament. It seems that one who chants a mass for temporal goods commits simony: for he chants a mass for the fruits of the earth, and for someone’s health, and for temporal peace. All these are temporal goods. Therefore he sets the mass, which is spiritual, under a temporal price. Therefore he commits simony. Likewise he chants a mass for temporal goods: therefore he consecrates the Eucharist for temporal goods; but the Eucharist is a grace-giving good. Therefore he sets a grace-giving good under a temporal price: therefore he commits simony. Likewise the Church praises God for temporal goods, for after a meal she says: “We give you thanks, almighty God,” etc., which is understood of temporal benefits. But the praise of God is something most spiritual, since it is a part of eternal beatitude. Therefore the Church sets the most spiritual thing under a temporal price. Therefore she commits simony. Likewise objection is raised concerning canons who rise for matins and run to matins: they do these things in order to have the daily distributions. But masses and matins are spiritual things, while those distributions are temporal. Therefore they set spiritual things under a temporal price. Therefore they commit simony. But on the contrary: these men sell in order to receive what is owed to them. Therefore they do not commit simony. Likewise it is commanded in Exodus \[Exod. 23:15\]: “You shall not appear empty in my sight three times a year, at Passover, at Pentecost, at the Feast of Tabernacles.” This precept the Church still observes. For the laity are bound to make offerings to their priests on three feasts: at Easter, at Pentecost, and at the Nativity. And unless they offer, they are compelled by the priests to render the oblations. Therefore the laity are bound to give temporal goods to priests for the spiritual goods which the priests give them. Therefore it is lawful to give temporal goods for spiritual goods: therefore it is lawful to buy spiritual goods for temporal goods. Therefore it is lawful to sell spiritual goods for temporal goods. For the reason for the laity to give temporal goods and for the priests to receive them is the same. Therefore it is lawful to sell the sacraments of the Church for temporal goods. Which is contrary to what has been said. Solution. God gives temporal goods for four causes. The first cause is necessity. Temporal goods are necessary so that the ministers of God may be sustained. Whence Malachi \[Mal. 3:10\]: “Bring every tithe into my barn, that there may be food in my house.” The second cause is the consolation of the weak. Whence at the end of Isaiah \[Isa. 66:13\]: “As a mother caresses her son, so will I console you,” namely by the benefit of temporal goods. For great want causes the little ones to sin or others to despair. Whence in Proverbs \[Prov. 30:8-9\]: “Give me neither beggary nor riches, lest, compelled by want, I steal and forswear the name of my God.” The third cause is spiritual commerce. Whence the gospel \[Luke 19:13\]: “Trade until I come.” For this reason God gives the mammon of iniquity — that is, of inequality or superfluity — so that out of that superfluity the rich may give to the poor, and thus acquire the kingdom of heaven. For this is spiritual commerce. The fourth cause is provocation to the praise of God: for many are provoked to praise on account of spiritual goods conferred upon them.
