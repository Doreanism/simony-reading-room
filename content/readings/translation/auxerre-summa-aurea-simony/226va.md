# On Simony. Tractatus Twenty-One

Having spoken of usury, we must now speak of simony. Concerning it, we must first see what simony is and in what matter it consists. Second, we must consider the spiritual cases in which the sin of simony has its origin; and this chapter is divided into many chapters according to the diversity of the matters in which such cases occur. Third, we must consider the punishment of the simoniac himself.

Simony is described as follows. Simony is a deliberate will to buy or sell something spiritual or something attached to the spiritual. And it is also described thus by some: simony is a pact concerning temporal things for the sake of obtaining spiritual things, or conversely.

Concerning the first definition, it is asked whether a secular judge commits simony by selling justice. It seems that he does, because it is commanded: “Love justice, you who judge the earth.” Since justice is commanded to be loved and seems to be a spiritual thing, when a secular judge sells justice, he sells a spiritual thing, or something attached to the spiritual, and therefore commits simony — or else simony is not a deliberate will, etc.

But on the other hand: on that passage of the Gospel, “He overturned the tables of the money changers and the seats of those who sold,” the gloss says that the sellers of doves are the prelates who sell the Holy Spirit. Therefore the sin of simony is committed in selling only by prelates, whose office alone it is to give. Therefore the sin of simony is not committed in selling by a secular judge. Likewise, it is lawful in a case of necessity to sell the crosses and chalices of the church, and these are spiritual things or attached to the spiritual; therefore either simony is not a sin, or simony is not a deliberate will, etc.

Likewise, the rule is that it is lawful for anyone to redeem his own right. Suppose then that someone has a prebend which in truth is his own, yet it has been brought into court, and suppose that unless he gives money to the judge, the judge will not give sentence in his favor. It is lawful even in this case to give money to the judge so that he may recover his right; and thus he buys the sentence, which is a spiritual thing, or the prebend. Therefore either it is not a sin, or simony is not a deliberate will, etc.

Likewise, the law grants that it is lawful for an advocate to sell the right of pleading; therefore by the same reasoning it is lawful for a judge to sell just judgment. This is proved: for just as to judge justly is a work of justice, so to plead is a work of justice — for it is commanded to defend orphans and widows. Therefore either both may be sold, or neither.

Solution: in this matter the spiritual is usually defined by a distinction in the following way. The spiritual is that which has the Holy Spirit, or that by which He is given, or that by which He is shown to be had: that by which He is had, namely as virtue; that by which He is given, as a sacrament; that by which He can be had or is shown to be had, as the gift of prophecy and the power of working miracles.
