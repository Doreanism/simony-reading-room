Provided he is otherwise virtuous and faithful — for in such worldly affairs one has need of worldly dread and being feared — one may have regard to this in him, in the case that thereby he can govern the town the better. And otherwise one shall not have regard to anything that does not contribute to the matter. And these things one need not have regard to in beguinages of this sort, in admitting persons; rather, the contrary things — poverty and humility, as has been written above. And above all I say: how dreadful and disorderly it would be that one should buy aldermanships or positions which ought to be given according to virtue, according to fitness for the duties, and \[so dreadful\] that one who did not have great sums of money to spare, such as a hundred shields, should be excluded. And I grant that one might at some time, if one saw a certain capable, virtuous man, \[take him in\] because such a man might from time to time be the more useful for governing externally in the convent, in order to govern the common goods with the power of his kindred and with virtues — this one might have regard to at such a time, when one needed it at a particular moment, when there was need on account of the external government. But always in the common reception one shall have regard to virtue first, and in the government one ought to have regard to virtue first. For virtue can profit without kindred. And often in such humble lowly estates as in religious life and among the lower clergy and in convents it is more profitable to be without kindred — often both for the convents themselves and for the persons themselves. And therefore one shall first have regard to virtue and to the good spiritual will; for power of kindred (11b), when it comes without virtue or without a spiritual life, is then wondrously more harmful than if the person were without power. For unrighteousness that has weapons is the worst of all, writes Aristotle. And above all: when there was need to take such a powerful person, one ought not to leave him standing on account of any money, nor take him for money’s sake, but on account of the suitability he had for governing the common goods.
