And so the virtue is made perfect that is called religion, and pure, and its works become purer and higher: such as inward devotion and inward prayer, thanking and praising God, and likewise other outward and inward deeds and works of religion become purer and more spiritual, in proportion as a person, inwardly and outwardly, more and more renounces and burns away the temporal things that are transitory. So the prophet says: “If thou take away the vile from the precious, thou shalt be as my mouth.” And St. Augustine, in the third book of *On the Trinity*, speaks thus: “The beauty that draws all rational souls, that is God, draws them the more ardently the purer they are; and they are the purer in proportion as they rise up and direct themselves more toward spiritual things, as they die more to fleshly things.” These are the words of St. Augustine, who was an advocate of the spirit and not of the flesh—whereas one now finds many masters who are advocates of the world and of the flesh, from which God preserve us. And because a person is often not sufficient to himself in acquiring this virtue of religion and in exercising himself in honor and in the service of God, a person chooses companions and fellowship—either male or female—to help one another in the virtue called religion, and in their exercises and works in the service of God. “Oh, how good and how joyful it is for brethren to dwell together as one!” says the psalm (19b). The help is manifold, spiritual, and wonderfully and divinely profitable, the one to kindle the other, to teach, to reprove, to warn, to comfort, to gladden, in life, in words, and in heart. And the fellowship is so glad: to pray together either for some matter or one for the other, and likewise to live together and to thank God when they are together, or when one is apart from the other; and to feel as one, to taste as one, and to will as one in God! How joyful and spiritual are all these things!
