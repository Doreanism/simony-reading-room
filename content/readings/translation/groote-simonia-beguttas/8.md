All good consciences bear witness that they could not do this deliberately and willingly for the sake of any money, for that would be to set themselves willingly against the love and honor of God, and to weigh love in the balance against temporal goods. Now suppose there are two maidens: the one is humble and well disposed toward God and to good morals, and is needed by your convent, and cannot well manage for herself in the world. You do not take her, solely because she does not have a hundred or fifty old shields \[in gold\], and you take another, who is haughtier and less well disposed and less needed, for the sake of money — one who, according to human estimation, can bring forth or bear no comparable spiritual fruit, nor helps you at all toward spiritual fruit, nor \[do\] you \[help\] her. Do you not then see what fruit, what reward, what honor, what love of God you are willingly taking away, according to estimation and according to what we are able to know — and all this for the sake of small, perishable things? — when, as you say, that poor, good little person must go astray in the world, who would have been so useful and so fruitful, and to whom your state of life would have been so useful, but who is now often hindered? And you say that the rich remain worldly in their souls, or fall behind. If you have no eye for this, then you are surely blind or unfaithful. Oh, how little love you have for God and his honor, you who pay this no heed and willingly neglect it. Who shall absolve you so long as you stand in this great contempt of the honor of God! There is \[no\] power on earth which can absolve \[you\] without \[your\] turning toward the honor of God and turning away from passing goods. What else is the root of all sin than a turning away from the honor of God and a turning toward temporal things? Likewise, do you not also see that you wrongfully hinder the poor person from his nearest way to God with (9ᵃ) injustice? For according to the justice of God, the vacant place is due to him, if no one more suitable comes to you and he is more virtuous \[than the others\]. The justice of God — which in Latin is called *justicia distributiva dei*, the \[justice\] which rewards and punishes all creatures according to their merits — speaks in all pure hearts indeed naturally, as Aristotle shows in the fifth book of *On Good Morals* \[the *Nicomachean Ethics*\].
