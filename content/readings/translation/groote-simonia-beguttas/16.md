Now the children of Adam come up with many words that do not stand up in righteousness, by which they try to shield or defend themselves.

They say in the first place that canons give a little money or a cope at the outset when they are made canons. I say that this is not a parallel case, for no poor person, nor any worthy person to whom the canonry rightfully belongs, is for that reason cast aside or shut out, as you \[beguines\] do. For this gift is so small in comparison with the prebend, and he can easily take it back out of the prebend, and therefore that taking is not so much against the righteousness of God, nor against the worthiness of persons, nor against poverty, as is the practice of the beguines. For no one is shut out on account of poverty \[from a canonry\], and if that did happen anywhere, it would be a great, grievous sin. Outwardly, too, the law stands so: that by right one shall first receive canons without any condition or promise of any money, for absolutely nothing; and if they then afterwards give a cope or a little money, willingly, according to good custom, it is not unfitting that the custom be kept. For no one who is poor is excluded by this; and as for whether, after they have received someone, they might afterwards compel him to keep the custom — the masters of the law speak about that with distinctions concerning which there is no need to speak here. For it can openly be seen that there is no parallel, nor is this an acceptance of persons against worthiness, nor against the honor of God, nor against righteousness; yet sin is often committed therein, when one oversteps the measure of the spiritual (13a) law. In the second place they want to cover themselves with the sins of other people — with the monks and nuns who take money, an agreed-upon sum, or who agree to take the very things the people have, so that they bring them in, knowing well that they are rich. This is in truth manifest simony, as the decretal says. And Pope Urban V has banned them all with his ban — namely, those who give or take anything in monasteries of monks or nuns, whether money or valuables or food, anywhere, whether with conditions or without conditions, for custom’s sake, when someone is received — of which bull we have a copy.
