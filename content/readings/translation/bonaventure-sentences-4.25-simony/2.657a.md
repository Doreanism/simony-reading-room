In the second way one is said to be wrongly ordained because he does not receive orders in the manner in which he ought; and this is either by stealth or by skipping. If by stealth, this is in two ways: either because he receives two orders on the same day, so that he steals one of them — speaking of the sacred orders — and the penalty for all such men is deposition, as is said in \[Decretum, Distinction\] 77, *Subdiaconus*, and in *Extra, de Eo qui furtive, Innotuit*, etc. But if by stealth he receives only one, then either it was forbidden under threat of anathema, and then only the Pope grants a dispensation, \[Extra,\] *de Eo qui furtive*; or, if it was not forbidden, he can be permitted to remain in the minor orders, and if he enters the cloister, the prelate can dispense with him. If, however, he was ordained by skipping, this is in two ways: either out of malice, or out of negligence. If out of malice, although he receives the character, he must nevertheless be deposed. If through ignorance, then with the penalty imposed on him by the bishop, he will be able to be promoted to the lower order, while he is meanwhile suspended from exercising the higher one, \[Decretum, Distinction\] 52, and *Extra, de Clerico ordinato per saltum*.

Note also here that, although one may skip from one order while omitting another, nevertheless if the priesthood has been passed over he cannot be a bishop, *Extra, de Excessibus praelatorum, Ex litteris*, at the end. But if he has omitted the diaconate, he is a bishop, yet he cannot give the order which he omitted, \[Decretum,\] Cause 1, Question 1. It is not so, however, in baptism, because that is the first Sacrament.
