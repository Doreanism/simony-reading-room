## CONCLUSION.

*Bigamists are, by reason of a defect of signification, unfit for the reception of orders, especially sacred orders, yet in such a way that they validly receive the order.*

I respond: For understanding what has been said, it must be noted that the Sacrament of orders surpasses the others in its character of *signifying*, since not only does what is applied externally signify, but the recipient himself is a sign, and the consecrated bishop or ordained presbyter himself signifies Christ Himself, as was touched upon above. And in this property it differs from the other Sacraments, except for matrimony, in which the man himself, who is the subject of the Sacrament, is a sign of Christ Himself, just as the woman is a sign of the Church, and so they concur in signifying the same thing.

Since, therefore, the *primary* signification of orders is that the one ordained signifies Christ as the Spouse of the Church — and the bigamist who has had a widow as wife cannot signify this, since the Church had a single Spouse and Christ a single Spouse — hence such persons are unfit for orders, especially the *principal* and *sacred* ones, in which a man already begins expressly to figure Christ. Hence bigamy renders a man unfit for the diaconate, the subdiaconate, the priesthood, and most of all the episcopate; and therefore in these orders we never read of a dispensation having been granted, nor should one be granted; not because the Sacrament is *essentially deficient* — indeed the bigamist does receive the character — but because the *perfection* of the Sacrament is lacking in him, a perfection which can by no means be supplied or compensated for through man. Nevertheless, in the inferior orders he does receive a dispensation in cases of necessity, as is said in Distinction 34, *Necessitate cogente*; and again, *Lector si viduam*, etc. From these things it is plain that division of the flesh impedes, and why it impedes, and which kind of division.
