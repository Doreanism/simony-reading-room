For a priest’s absolution is either null or it is without pretense; but no one is knowingly absolved by a heretic who lacks pretense. — In another way, however, it can be said that a fourfold power is found in the ministers of the Church. One is founded principally upon order, such as the power of consecrating; another principally upon canonical jurisdiction, such as the power of excommunicating; another upon order together with eminence, such as the power of ordaining; another upon order together with jurisdiction, such as the power of absolving and binding in the forum of penance. And since the character cannot be taken away, the power which follows upon the character cannot in fact be taken away. But since jurisdiction descends in orderly fashion from a higher to a lower level, such that its fullness resides in the Supreme Pontiff, he can take it away, and with it the power that follows upon it. Hence by excommunicating he can remove the power of absolving and of excommunicating, but not that of ordaining.

4. And from this the response is clear to many points, and to the following one as well. For when it is objected that the fullness of power resides in the Supreme Pontiff, this is true insofar as power follows upon jurisdiction — which is greatest and reaches its term in him — but not insofar as it follows upon the character, since that is imprinted immediately by God and is equal in all. Nevertheless, every exercise of power must take place under his governance; and therefore, if he forbids it, such a power cannot lawfully pass into act. Yet because in the order of fact \[the power\] does not depend upon him as regards its reality, the man acts just as any other does. And so the whole matter is clear.
