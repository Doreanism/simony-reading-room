Likewise, he who buys gives a temporal good for a spiritual one, therefore he holds the spiritual dearer than the carnal: therefore he does not turn himself away from the unchangeable good, but rather turns toward it: therefore he does not sin, because “sin is a turning away from the unchangeable good and a turning toward the changeable.”

### Conclusion

A simoniac is not properly a heretic, unless he believes that spiritual things are for sale.

I reply: One must say that neither holy orders nor any of the sacraments can be given for money without grave and enormous sin, since what is spiritual—which on account of its dignity ought to be given out of pure liberality—is treated as merchandise and made cheap; and again, because what was beyond price is placed under a price; and this can in no way be done without insult being offered to the Holy Spirit: and therefore it is evil not only in itself, but indeed according to its very nature.

But if you ask me whether it is a sin in faith or in morals, one must say that he who buys or sells spiritual things of this sort either believes that they can be valued at a price and made equal to the worth of an earthly thing and possessed for money—and this is manifest heresy, and so Simon Magus reckoned. Such a one errs in faith, because he believes that grace is not grace. — Or he who sells and buys does not believe this, that the grace of God is acquired by money, but nevertheless, burning with avarice, he strives to obtain or secure for money something that pertains to human dispensation; and so it is not a sin of faith but of morals, since such a one knows perfectly well and does ill; just as he who fornicates, knowing that fornication is a sin, does not err in faith but in the path of morals. — And so the simoniac is a covetous man, and of the worst species of avarice, and is not properly a heretic, but nevertheless is called a heretic by the saints, either because he acts in the manner of a heretic, or
