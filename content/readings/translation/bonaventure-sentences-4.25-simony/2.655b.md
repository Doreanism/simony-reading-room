—Christ, however, was joined to the Synagogue, though not indissolubly, and therefore not by way of marriage. — And so it is clear that all such statutes are entirely reasonable, since in their establishment what is weighed is not fault but rather fitting signification.

In connection with this it is asked: if a slave has been ordained without his master’s knowledge, whether he ought to be restored to him.

Likewise, if he is to be restored, why are not other consecrated things \[restored as well\]; and again, for how long a time can the master reclaim him.

### CONCLUSION

A slave ought not to be ordained, on account of the honor due to the Sacrament.

I REPLY: For the understanding of what has been said, it must be noted that the Sacrament of orders surpasses the others not only in respect of the *character of sign*—because not only the Sacrament but also the one who receives the Sacrament holds the character of sign—but also surpasses them in respect of the *character of remedy*: for here grace is given not only to heal the subject but also grace which equips him to heal others; whence it places a man not only in a state of health but also in a rank of honor and dignity. And therefore for this Sacrament not just any person is required, but an honorable person, to whom clerical honor and dignity is fitting. And because honor regards many external things, a man is rendered unfit to receive this Sacrament not only by defect of life but also by defect of many other things.

For a person to be honorable, honor must concur in him from the side of origin, name, body, action, condition, and conduct.
