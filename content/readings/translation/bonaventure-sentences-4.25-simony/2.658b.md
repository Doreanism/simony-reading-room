### DUB. VI.

Likewise, concerning what he says — *The simoniacal heresy is threefold* — it is asked: in how many ways is simony contracted? And it is customarily said that it is contracted in five ways, which are contained in these verses:

Nummus, lingua, timor, caro cum fama populari\
Non faciunt gratis spirituale dari.

That is: coin, tongue, fear, flesh, together with popular favor do not allow a spiritual thing to be given freely. Therefore, according to this, it seems that the Magister insufficiently assigns only three modes.

In connection with this, the question is raised concerning the sufficiency of these modes. For since reputation is a “good odor,” like a good example, it does not seem to be a temporal thing. — Likewise, in the Old Law the priesthood was held by right of kinship: therefore, since that was not a sin, it does not seem \[that this is a mode of simony either\], etc.

I REPLY: It must be said that, properly speaking, simony lies in a price, which indeed avarice aims at, especially when a pact intervenes, and this simony is determined here by the Magister. And it can occur in three ways: for the price can be given in such a way that the simoniac himself does not receive it — as when it is given to his associates; or it can be given by the associates of the one being ordained; or the one being ordained can himself give it to the ordainer, and then there is simony in the reception of the order itself — and, properly speaking, simony occurs in these three ways. — Or, otherwise: either *the ordained gives* and *he receives*, and then the man is ordained simoniacally by a simoniac; or *the ordained gives* but *he does not receive*, and then he is ordained simoniacally by a non-simoniac; or *neither does the one give nor the other receive*, but the ordainer was on some other occasion a simoniac, and then the man is ordained non-simoniacally by a simoniac. — On this basis the *Magister’s* distinction is taken, and it is sufficient enough; but in the *common* distinction of the modes, simony is taken more generally, from which few in the clergy are today free, compared with the multitude — as, for instance, when he who confers orders does so from an indirect cause, or another acquires either the order or the benefice from a disordered cause; and these are the *five modes*, whose sufficiency is shown thus.
