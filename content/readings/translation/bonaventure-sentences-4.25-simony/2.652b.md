But still, because those orders are ordered to a certain habituation, where some utility or necessity makes it seem expedient, they can be promoted even to the minor orders before the years of discretion. But that they should be promoted thus indiscriminately, and endowed with ecclesiastical benefices, can scarcely be without contempt of the canons and scandal of men and danger of souls. — One must therefore concede the arguments showing that the use of reason is not necessary for the Sacrament’s *being* or *being received*, but only for its *well-being* and *worthy reception*, speaking of orders properly.

1. To the objection from matrimony, it must be said that the cases are not alike: for in matrimony the use of reason is required not on account of the dignity of the Sacrament, but on account of the signification of the union of God and the soul, which exists only in mutual consent. Moreover, this argument does not conclude generally about the Sacrament of orders, but only about the dignity of the bishop.

2. To the objection that the bishop says “Receive,” it must be said that that offering signifies rather the command and power of the *giver* than the exercise of the *receiver*. Hence it is for him not only to *offer* but to *confer*; and unless there is an obstacle of a contrary will—such that he is altogether unwilling—he receives the Sacrament, in the same way as is declared concerning the Sacrament of confirmation.

3. To the objection from use, it must be said that even if he does not have the use immediately, yet because he is hoped at some time to have it, the Sacrament is not given in vain, just as priesthood is given to one who long afterwards defers celebrating.
