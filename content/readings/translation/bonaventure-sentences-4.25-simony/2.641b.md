—taking the false position, showing that heretics cannot confer orders. In the second section he argues to the true position, at the place: *On the contrary, however, others seem to hold*, etc. In the third he resolves the matter, at the place: *Some, however, determine this as follows*, etc.

*Concerning simoniacs, however, there is no doubt*, etc. This is the second part, in which he treats of the vice which arises in connection with the giving and receiving of orders, namely simony; and this part has two sections. In the first he treats of the fault. In the second he treats of the penalty, at the place: *There is, however, a difference among those who are ordained*, etc.

Second, whether a heretical bishop can confer orders.

Third, whether a simoniac bishop can validly sell any order.

Fourth, whether one who confers orders simoniacally becomes by that very act a heretic.

—chapter twelve, speaking of the office of the bishop, says: “He is named both the highest priest and the supreme pontiff; for he it is who makes priests and levites, he it is who orders all the ecclesiastical grades.”

3. Likewise, the matter seems clear by reason: for it belongs to the same person to establish orders in the Church as to govern the Church; but the governance of the Churches belongs to the bishops, who are the overseers \[superintendents\]: therefore the dispensing of orders also belongs to them.
