For if I am ordained knowingly by a simoniac, I am irregular and am punished as a simoniac. If I do not obey, I sin mortally and give scandal: therefore it seems that in such a case a man is in a perplexed position.

I REPLY: It must be said that in the first case he has the exercise of his office from the law, which on account of such ignorance reckons him excused, while he has the order itself from the simoniac. In the second case, he ought, as far as he can, to evade it; but if he cannot, he suffers rather than acts, and he is then held excused. Hence he must either defer the orders, or go to another bishop; but if he can do nothing else, he must endure it, and since the Church tolerates the simoniac, he too must tolerate him and is excused, just like one who is ignorant.

### DUB. V.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: *Whoever of these sells the one without which the other cannot be had.* For this seems to be wrongly said, because the right of patronage is annexed inseparably to the estate: therefore, since the right of patronage is spiritual and cannot be sold without simony, it is impossible that such an estate be sold without simony.

I REPLY: It must be said that the *Magister* is not speaking of temporal things to which spiritual things are annexed, but rather the reverse — of spiritual things to which temporal things are annexed, such as a prebend; and the *Magister’s* word holds in such cases, not in the first. — Or it must be said that he understands it of that which is so joined to a spiritual thing that it takes the place of a spiritual thing. But an estate or a village to which a right of patronage is annexed truly holds the character of a temporal thing.
