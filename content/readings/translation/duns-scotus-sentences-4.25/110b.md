Concerning this twenty-fifth distinction, I ask two questions about the one receiving Orders. The first is whether canonical punishment impedes the reception and conferral of Orders. That it does not: an excommunicate, or an irregular, if he attempts to consecrate, does in fact consecrate; therefore by parity an excommunicate or irregular bishop, if he attempts to ordain and to do what the Church does, ordains; and the one who receives, if he intends to be ordained, is ordained. The consequence is plain by the topic of similarity, since the cases appear similar on both sides; and also by the topic from the lesser, since what does not impede the carrying out of the more noble act does not seem to impede the carrying out of the less noble act.

Moreover, an irregular is not excluded from the reception of the other sacraments, but, if he intends to receive, he receives them; therefore not from this one either. The consequence is plain by parity. The antecedent is plain, because an irregular can receive the sacrament of penance—otherwise the way of salvation would be closed to him.

Moreover, no canonical punishment excludes from any ecclesiastical grade or act more than heresy does; but heresy does not exclude, as is held in C. 1, q. 1, *Quod quidem*.

The opposite is in C. 1, q. 1, *De caetero*, and in the next chapter: “We altogether condemn simoniacs,” and after: “We have decreed such a reception or conferral to be void”; and the c. *Si qui*: “Those who knowingly have permitted themselves to be consecrated by simoniacs, we have decreed their consecration to be wholly void.”
