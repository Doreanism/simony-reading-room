It is commonly asked whether irregularity arising from fault is a censure. The affirmative is taught by Soto in distinction 22 at the place cited, and by others. From this he infers that, when in the Bull of the Crusade the faculty is given of absolving from censures, irregularity arising from fault is included.

The opposite, however, is to be held, as is clear from the chapter *Quaerenti*, etc. *On the Signification of Words*, where under “censure” are included only excommunication, suspension, and interdict — under which irregularity is not comprehended. Second, because from censures power of absolving is given, whereas irregularity is not removed by absolution but by dispensation, as is plain. Third, because in the Bull of the Crusade and other similar grants, the concession or faculty of absolving from censures and reserved sins is expressed as distinct from the faculty of dispensing in vows and irregularities, for which a new confession is required.

You will say: the favors of a prince are to be interpreted most amply (*l. de beneficio*, *de constit. Princip.*); privileges are to be extended (chapter *Olim*, etc. *On the Signification of Words*). The reply is that privileges are limited by the form of the words, by their proper rigor, and by the subject matter. Irregularity does not come under the name of “censure,” and its removal does not come under the appellation of “absolution” but of “dispensation.” Therefore, just as the power of absolving from a censure cannot be extended to what is not a censure, and just as the power of absolving cannot be exercised except by absolution — which is in another form and manner, different from dispensation, by which irregularity is removed — so too the power of absolving from censure does not include irregularity, in which,
