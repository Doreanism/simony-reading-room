(l) *The third penalty is irregularity*, etc. This definition is to be understood as follows: irregularity, of itself and directly, is an inability to receive orders — even the first tonsure — and renders a person, by ecclesiastical law, unfit for the clerical state and for orders. This inability affects the person directly and *per se* with regard to orders, whether for receiving an order or for ministering in one already received. From this we may gather the difference between this impediment and others, first from deposition and even perpetual suspension from an order — with which irregularity has more in common — because irregularity regards the person first and *per se*, in such a way that it is incidental whether one is a layman or a cleric; it renders the person positively incapable of an order and of ministry, as directly as the Church can manage. Real and verbal deposition, however, like even perpetual suspension by way of privation, operate by taking away the grade and its use (deposition does this), or only the use (as suspension does), without establishing a positive impediment by way of inability through which the subject would be rendered positively incapable, but only privation of the grade he had, or at least of its use. Therefore, just as in physics it is one thing for a subject to be made incapable of a form — which denotes a defect of the proximate potency for receiving it — and quite another for the subject merely to be deprived of a form which it has, so too in moral matters it is one thing to render a subject positively incapable by taking away the very capacity for the form, and another for a subject already informed to be deprived of the form it has, whether by privation as to its being or as to its use.
