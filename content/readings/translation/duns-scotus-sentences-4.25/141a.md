To the second: perhaps in Greece the wife of a priest can be called a “presbyteress,” where priests licitly make use of a marriage previously contracted. But among us Latins, where chastity—not conjugal but simply such—is annexed to the order, a “presbyteress” might be called some good matron, a widow or one perfect among other women, or perhaps in a community the one who presides over all the others, as an abbess among nuns. But she does not by this hold a higher grade of order, indeed not even any preeminence with respect to any man. In like manner one may answer the point about a “deaconess”: that the woman to whom it belongs by appointment of an abbess or of a community to read the homily at matins may be called a deaconess; but that is not the act of any order.

To the other point, one may say, as Gratian says in dist. 4, that “by the contrary practice of those who use them, certain laws have been abrogated”—as he afterwards proves there by many examples, and especially when the contrary practices rest on a new reason. So it is here. In the primitive Church boys were not at once instructed in the things pertaining to divine worship or office, and so even fully grown adults were quite rude in such matters.
