The first is proved as follows: to the reception of a sacred order there is annexed a vow of continence, as is held in the *Extra*; but a child, and one not yet possessing discretion, cannot make this vow either tacitly or expressly. The second is proved because no prohibition is found that bars children from receiving the non-sacred orders.

Concerning (b) the third sense of “cannot,” I say that this is not found in a child, and this with respect to any order whatsoever, even a sacred one. For the power of carrying out some act, or the grade by which a person is able to exercise that act, can precede the act itself, or the proximate power to that act, in duration. An order is nothing but a grade that disposes one to minister in a determinate ecclesiastical grade, as was said in the preceding distinction; therefore that grade can belong to someone before, with the impediments removed, he is in proximate potency to exercising that act. Nor in this case is consent required for the imprinting of the character, just as it is not in the minor orders.

### Commentary.

(a) *Here I briefly say*, etc. He distinguishes a threefold manner of receiving orders. The first concerns reception with due reverence and disposition, that is, honestly and fittingly. The second manner is receiving orders licitly and according to the laws. The third manner is receiving them validly and as to their effect.

The Doctor’s first conclusion is that a child cannot receive an order in the first manner before the use of reason. The second is that he likewise cannot receive a sacred order in the second manner, though he can receive a non-sacred order. The third is that he can receive an order in the third manner.
