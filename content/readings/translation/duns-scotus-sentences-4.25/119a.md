Irregularity, then, is a positive inability, as I said, for an order and the use of it, in general or in particular, according to the nature of the order, as it is prescribed by law with respect to one or to many.

(m) *This is frequently incurred by law, although*, etc. He divides this penalty, in relation to its efficient cause, into that which is inflicted by law and that which can sometimes be inflicted by a judge. About the first member there is no controversy. About the second — whether it can be imposed by man — there is doubt. The negative is held by Soto on book 4, distinction 22, question 3, article 1; Ledesma, question 26, article 2; our Castro in book 2 *On Penal Law*, last chapter; Navarrus in his *Summa*, chapter 27; Covarruvias on the *Clementine* unique *On Homicide*, in part 1; Suárez, disputation 40, section 4, doubt 2. In the same opinion may also be cited the Doctor, as we explained there at distinction 13, question 3, § *It remains therefore to be seen* at the end, where he says that the penalty of irregularity is not incurred by a concubinary celebrating, by a usurer, by an infamous person who has repented, or by one who celebrates before making restitution — that such a one does not become irregular, because such a penalty is not expressed by law and consequently is not to be extended. Holding this opinion, then — which is more common among the jurists, and is founded on the chapter *Is qui*, *On the Sentence of Excommunication*, in the *Sext*, and on the usage of the Church — the Doctor understands that, as far as the nature of the penalty goes, there is no absolute repugnance to its being imposed by a judge according to the laws, since no law prohibits it; but the cited chapter can be understood as referring to irregularity incurred by law, about which alone the question was inquiring — namely, whether one celebrating in a polluted church became irregular.
