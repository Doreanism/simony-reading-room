### Scholium.

That women are by divine law incapable of orders. St. Thomas here, q. 2, art. 1; Bonaventure, art. 2, q. 1; Soto, q. 1, art. 1. And the Fathers report the opposite as a heresy: Ambrose on 1 Tim. 3; Epiphanius, *Haer.* 49, attributes it to the Pepuzians and Quintillianists; Augustine, *Haer.* 27. Those, however, who hold that the minor orders are instituted only by the Church would not deny that they can be given to women—on which see Vasquez, third part, disp. 245, c. 2, and disp. 237, c. 2.

This (c) “cannot” in the third sense, however, in the case of a woman, is not to be held as something determined precisely by the Church, but is had from Christ. For the Church would not have presumed to deprive the whole female sex, without any fault of its own, of an act that could licitly belong to it, an act ordered to the salvation of the woman and of others in the Church through her. To do so would seem to be a matter of the greatest injustice, not only against the whole sex but also against the few individuals concerned. But as it now stands, if by divine law an ecclesiastical order could licitly belong to a woman, it could be ordered to the salvation both of women and of others through them. And what the Apostle says in 1 Tim. 2, *I do not permit a woman to teach*—understanding this of public teaching in the Church—is not said by the Apostle as one laying down a statute, but “I do not permit” because Christ did not permit it either.

The evident proof of this is taken from the fact that he did not place even his own Mother in any grade of order in the Church—she to whom no other woman could or shall be able to be compared in holiness. Natural reason, moreover, agrees with this
