Against this (d): where the agent and the patient are the same and of the same species, there the effect is the same; but the bishop is the same agent, and I posit that he intends in the same way here and there, and a man and a woman, and the soul of a man and the soul of a woman, are patients of the same species; therefore if, when the bishop does some act, the soul of a man receives the character, it follows that, when he does the same act with regard to a woman, she will receive the same.

I reply: the major is true of an agent that induces a form, and of a natural agent. But if it acts voluntarily and contingently, it is not true that it does the same; rather, it could fail to act. If, moreover, it does not induce the form but only performs a certain act on which another \[agent\] induces it—an agent that voluntarily introduces \[the form\] into that patient into which it proposes to introduce it—the major is not true; and so it is in the case at hand. And this argument is good for proving that the minister performs no necessary act on which the effect of the sacrament necessarily follows, but only contingently—as for the most part, by divine pact.
