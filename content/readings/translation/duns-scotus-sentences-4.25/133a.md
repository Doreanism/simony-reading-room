That Regulars are not included under the name of “clergy” in odious matters is taught by Sylvester, *Interdictum* 5. But on this point no general rule can be assigned; for the form of the sentence must be examined, as in ch. *Si sententia*, *De sententia excommunicationis* in book 6, where under the name of “clergy” Regulars are included. But that case pertained to the whole community of a town, whose burdens they were bound to share; but when the cause is particular to the secular clergy, then Regulars do not seem to be included under the name of “clergy,” unless it is otherwise clear from the sentence itself, or unless they certainly share in the cause.

A mixed interdict is when not only a place is interdicted, but also equally specifically a person, determinately or indeterminately, or a community; or certainly when a person is primarily interdicted, such as a prince, and a place wherever he comes; this interdict is called by some “ambulatory” or “perambulatory.” Mention of it is made in ch. *Non est vobis* etc. *De sponsalibus*; ch. *Dilectis filiis* etc. *De appellationibus*. A general local interdict does not include a general personal interdict, nor *vice versa*, speaking *per se*, although they can be conjoined by the judge, as is plain from the cited ch. *Si sententia*.
