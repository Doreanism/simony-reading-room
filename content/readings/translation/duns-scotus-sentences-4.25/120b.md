The Doctor can be understood in another way, namely that he does not here mean irregularity to be imposed by a judge’s sentence except in a broad sense — that is, in a doubtful case, by way of a declarative sentence, by which the judge determines that in this or that doubtful case irregularity intervenes according to the laws. This interpretation agrees both with the Doctor himself in the place cited above and with the common opinion and practice, and therefore is to be held. Hence those words of his — *”although it could sometimes be inflicted by a judge according to the laws”* — import only this. For since the laws do not introduce, nor make mention of, irregularity to be incurred but only of irregularity incurred by a sentence already passed (as the interpreters commonly take the cited chapter), so a judge would not sometimes impose this penalty according to the laws, nor could he according to the laws, since it is neither arbitrary, nor do the laws place it under the judge’s discretion, as they do other penalties which can be imposed by man. Hence his saying *only sometimes* and *according to the laws* must be referred to this alone — that in a doubtful case his declarative sentence is required.

It could also, thirdly, be explained that he here understands perpetual suspension, which is equivalent to irregularity, since when treating of suspension he seems to hold that it lasts only for a time; whereas irregularity is by its nature perpetual, and difficult to dispense.
