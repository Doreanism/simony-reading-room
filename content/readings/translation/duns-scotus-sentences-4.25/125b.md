When, however, neither of these is brought about, irregularity is not incurred. Canon 22 of the Apostles, where it is said that eunuchs made so without their fault are not irregular; chapters 1 and 2 *de corpore vitiatis*, where it is defined that those defects which do not bring about an impediment to the ministry, or a deformity, do not bring about irregularity. Likewise, chapter *Si partem*, chapter *Lator*, chapter *Praecepta*, distinction 55. An impediment to the use of an order and a deformity in a layman bring about irregularity simply; in a cleric, so that he may not ascend to the higher orders; in one already ordained, with respect to use, if it supervenes, only with respect to the use of that order to which he is impeded, or which he cannot exercise without scandal. If, however, he can exercise some act of the order without scandal, he does not become irregular for that act — for example, if a priest, lacking hands, cannot celebrate, he can nevertheless hear confessions. If he is deaf, he cannot do the latter, but can the former; he can solemnly exercise the office of deacon and subdeacon, unless he is impeded for these.

An incurable disease likewise induces irregularity, when it has attached to it a perpetual impediment or deformity. This is clear from the canons cited *de corpore vitiatis* and *de clerico aegroto*, distinction 55. It is required that it be incurable, in order that it may bring about this irregularity, and that the impediment or deformity be perpetual. As for the irregularity which is incurred from a bodily defect, the dispensation belongs to the Pope alone, as the common opinion holds.
