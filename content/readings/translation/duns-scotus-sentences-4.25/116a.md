Second, he distinguishes the infamous person from infamy itself; for one can be defamed by being accused of some crime, yet not for that reason be infamous — that is, one does not incur the penalty of infamy — unless the sin itself is public and notoriously committed, and beyond this it is required that it be such a sin to which a penalty of this kind is annexed by law or by a judge. Thus, as the Doctor says, every infamous person is publicly marked for some crime which the law punishes with the penalty of infamy; for even if someone commits some notorious crime to which by law such a penalty is not annexed, he will indeed in the opinion of men be unworthy of offices and dignities by the nature of the thing, yet not through the canonical or civil penalty of infamy; and without the assistance of a law inflicting a penalty on account of such a deed, the conferral of office and dignity upon him will be valid.

Third, he says that infamy is sometimes incurred by the law itself, sometimes by the sentence of a judge, sometimes by both — where he touches on the common division of infamy into that which is of fact and that which is of law. Concerning the former one finds the chapter *Licet, etc., de testibus*, and the chapter *Licet, etc., de probationibus*. Concerning the infamy of law one finds c. *Infames*, C. 3 q. 7, in books 1, 2, 3 *ff. de his qui notantur infamia*. What is called infamy of fact is called by the Doctor infamy of law, because it is incurred by the sentence of the law without the sentence of a judge or judicial order.
