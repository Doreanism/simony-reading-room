If, however, the ignorance is not invincible, nor is there total uncertainty — as when notice of the excommunication has come to him by common report or public rumor — he is irregular, although he is dealt with more mildly. This is proved by *Extra, de translatione Episcoporum, Inter corporalia*. So too if the penalty of interdict is not observed, as is had in *Extra, de excessibus praelatorum et subditorum, Tanta*; though that chapter could well be expounded otherwise. The same follows for one who does not observe a suspension, as is had in *Extra, de sententia et re judicata, Cum aeterni*, and *Extra, de sententia excommunicationis, Cum medicinalis*.

### Commentary.

(p) *A simoniac, however, in a benefice*, etc. Here he deals with one upon whom a benefice has been conferred simoniacally, without his knowledge; he says that, as soon as he knows, he is bound to resign the benefice and to make restitution of the fruits, since he did not make them his own, the conferment having been by the law itself null. See the Scholium and the authors whom it cites on *Extrav. Detestabile*. The rest of this passage is clear from the canons alleged. Some, holding the higher opinion, deny that one who, while bound by a censure, receives orders becomes irregular properly speaking, but only suspended — thus Suarez, disputation 42, *de censuris*, section 3, case 7. But the laws dispose otherwise and are to be interpreted as the common opinion of the doctors and the gloss understand them.
