For although this tonsure is by the institution of the Church, and is not an Order, nevertheless it is a disposition toward an Order, and accordingly, as such, it ought to be performed upon a capable subject. Therefore, just as the Church cannot exorcize or bless a font of wine, for example, as the matter of Baptism — because it is not capable of that blessing which disposes for the Sacrament — so likewise neither can it bestow the clerical tonsure, which is a disposition presupposed by the rite for the Orders, except upon a subject capable of the Order, because a prior disposition must be made upon a subject capable of the form.

(b) *Against this, where the same agent*, etc. The answer to this objection, and the difference between a voluntary or free agent and a natural one, is clear from the text: when he says that upon the action of the minister the effect of the Sacrament does not necessarily follow, he excludes natural necessity and inevitable necessity, not necessity from covenant, supposing the requisites on the part of the subject.

To the first argument the answer is clear from the text.

To the second: the nomenclature derived from the order which the husbands had assumed is clear from the Council of Tours, canons 13 and 20; from the Council of Auxerre, canon 21; and from Gregory, book 4 of the *Dialogues*, chapter 22. In the Latin Church, after the death of their husbands, it was not permitted to a deaconess, subdeaconess, presbytera, or episcopa to pass to second nuptials, because they were bound by a vow of chastity at the time they gave their husbands license to receive Orders, as is clear from the Council of Auxerre and from the Roman Council under Gregory. Of these \[women\] Peter Lombard, and after him St. Thomas, understand the words of the Apostle in 1 Timothy 3: “The women in like manner chaste,” etc. Chrysostom, Theodoret, Theophylact, and Oecumenius \[understand the passage\] of deaconesses, whose use was found in the Church.
