The first conclusion is self-evident. The second, as to its first part, is the common view—St. Bonaventure on this distinction, art. 2, q. 2; Richard, art. 4, q. 2, ad 3; Major, dist. 24, q. 2; Paludanus on this distinction, q. 3, and others. The Doctor’s argument is clear: the vow of continence which is annexed to a sacred order can be emitted neither tacitly nor expressly before the use of reason and liberty, since it must be a promise made to God in one of those two modes, emitted by the very one who is obligated through his own act. See Vasquez, disp. 246, c. 3, tom. 3, on the third part. Some, however, teach not improbably that, if upon attaining the use of reason a man ratifies the ordination performed and exercises an act of the order, he is bound to continence, because that ratification and exercise involve consent to the order received together with the burden attached to it by law, whether that law be divine or human—and this holds especially if it should happen at puberty.

The second part of the conclusion is clear, because by the ancient law a boy of seven could receive the first tonsure (*cap. Nullus Episcopus, de tempore ordinandorum* in 6), and thereafter he could likewise be marked with the minor orders (*cap. De iis*, etc., dist. 28).

(b) *Concerning the third sense of “cannot,”* etc. Here he establishes the third conclusion, namely, that little ones can be validly ordained before the use of reason. This is the common view. Some of the older writers, however, denied that an infant is capable of an order—in St. Thomas at the place cited in the *scholium*—and Durandus afterwards followed them on this distinction, q. 2. Peter de Soto, in his lecture on the sacrament of orders, favors this view, but only with respect to the major orders. Paludanus requires the consent of parents or guardians, without which even baptism conferred on an infant would be null—a view which we have refuted above in the same way as we refuted him on the baptism of little ones.
