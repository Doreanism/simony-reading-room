Moreover, the Church renders persons illegitimate for contracting marriage, in such a way that if they attempt it they accomplish nothing; therefore much more can she render a bishop unfit for a spiritual act, in such a way that if he attempts it he accomplishes nothing. The consequence is proved thus: those things which belong to positive law appear to be more subject to the Church than those which belong to natural and divine law. But every ecclesiastical ordering by way of these dignities of the episcopate and the like seems to pertain to the positive law of the Church; at any rate it does not appear to belong to natural and divine law in the way that marriage does.

### Scholium.

He defines canonical punishment, and divides it into seven species: deposition, degradation, infamy, irregularity, excommunication, interdict, and suspension; and he sets out each one precisely, in the brevity he aims at.

In (a) this question, the first matter to be considered concerns the canonical punishments about which the question inquires. The second is how they exclude from the conferral or reception of Orders. As to the first (b): canonical punishment, as we understand it in the present matter, is a penalty inflicted through the Canons, prohibiting or restraining the one punished from some ecclesiastical grade which would otherwise be lawful to him. This last clause is added because, if it would not otherwise be lawful to him, it is not a punishment—for it is no punishment to a layman
