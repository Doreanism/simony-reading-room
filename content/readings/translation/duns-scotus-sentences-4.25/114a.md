Nevertheless deposition renders a person perpetually unfit by the very nature of this penalty, and it can be removed only by restoring the person to the former rank, although a lawfully deposed person is never or only rarely restored; suspension, however, does not render a person unfit absolutely, and can be removed by dispensation. For this reason, then, the Doctor says that this is the greatest penalty, both because in extension it takes away every ecclesiastical rank, and also, when it is complete, the clerical state itself, and because by its nature it is perpetual and irrevocable by the usage of the canons and of the Church, although absolutely it could be revoked by the Pontiff through a restoration of the person to integral standing.

He says further that deposition and degradation, when they extend to every ecclesiastical rank and privilege, are one and the same; but when degradation is from one rank only — as deposition from the priesthood while the remaining ranks abide — degradation is a partial deposition. In the first sense of degradation he speaks in the sense and manner of the canonists and of certain canons, which sometimes take both terms as synonyms, so that degradation accordingly signifies deposition from any rank whatever, whether it is properly an order, or an order taken broadly for any eminent rank in the Church, even of office and dignity, as is clear from chapter 2 *De poenis* in the Sixth, the chapter *Tuae, de poenis*, as Abbas \[Panormitanus\] there \[notes\] on the chapter *Ne Clerici, vel voventes*.
