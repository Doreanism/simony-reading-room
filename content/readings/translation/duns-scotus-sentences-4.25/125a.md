(s) *There follows also the third*, etc. Irregularity from mutilation is sometimes with fault, sometimes without fault. It is with fault even through indirect voluntariness, as when the man himself gave cause for the mutilation of a part or of a whole member, even covertly — for example, if he cut off for himself a part, some member or part of a member; then he is irregular, as is clear from chapter *Qui partem*, distinction 55. As to the cutting off of a part that is not notable, direct volition is required, and indirect volition does not suffice, as Navarrus teaches, chapter 27. So that if in a duel some part of a finger has been cut off by another, this does not make him irregular, because it is far more atrocious that someone should turn the iron against himself than that he be cut by another. Direct volition, therefore, insofar as it has its act, is what is punished in that text. But if he gave cause, and a member — even a hidden one — is entirely cut off, as when one is made a eunuch as a penalty for a crime, he becomes irregular, according to the same author, who cites Hostiensis and Joannes Andreae, and the common opinion. And it is clear from chapter *Ex parte*, etc., *de corpore vitiatis*, etc.

The opposite of this is also probable, if the rigor of the canons and their proper sense be considered, which always seem to speak of direct volition, as indicating a certain ferocity against oneself, as is clear from canons 22 and 23 of the Apostles, chapter *Hi qui*, distinction 55, and chapter *Maritum*, distinction 33, etc., chapter *Qui partem*. The former opinion, since it is the common one, is safer. For irregularity from direct culpable volition it suffices that a part of a member be cut off. For irregularity from indirect volition, the whole member must be cut off. If the mutilation occurs without fault, irregularity is incurred when, from the lack of a member, a notable deformity and indecency is brought about, or certainly an unfitness for the sacred ministry.
