The third saying of the Doctor is held by Felinus in the chapter *Per tuas*, *de Simonia*, who asserts that it is the common opinion. And it is evident from the argument of the chapter *Nobis fuit*, etc., *de Simonia*, which the Doctor cites in the gloss on the chapter *de Simoniace*, under the same title. The contrary view is taught by some on the basis of the foundation alleged above, namely because that penalty is not expressed in law; thus Navarrus in his *Summa*, chapter 25, and other later authors. But, on the contrary, although he himself does not contract irregularity, yet because simony intervenes through the fault of others, these things seem to have been expressly enacted in odium of it, as is contained equivalently in the title *de Simonia* in various chapters concerning benefices and offices in which simony intervenes through the fault of others without the promoted person’s knowledge, which offices must be relinquished and resigned when the simony is established. Now these laws are not new decrees, but a declaration of the ancient ones, and they likewise comprehend, or rather *a fortiori*, simony committed in an order in any manner whatsoever, unless it is done in fraud of the one ordained, and they declare the ordination itself invalid. See the Gloss with its addition on chapter *At si Clerici*, etc., *de judiciis*, where it understands those penalties as referring to irregularity.

### Scholium.

That one who receives a benefice simoniacally is bound to make restitution \[is established by\] *Extra. Cum detestabile, de Simonia*, chapter *Si quis* 1, question 1. He is not, however, bound to this by divine law; both because otherwise, if simony were committed without his knowledge, he would be bound to it; and also because by other sins a man is not rendered, by divine law, incapable of a benefice. Thus Navarrus, chapter 23, numbers 103, 104; Covarruvias, *reg. Peccatum*, part 2, § 9. Other spiritual things accepted simoniacally, however, one is not bound to relinquish before sentence, since this is not had by law. Lessius, book 2, chapter 35, dubia 29, 30. The Doctor teaches whether irregularity is incurred in the surreptitious reception of orders, and in administering in an order not received, or in a censure, and \[he proves this\] by clear and express laws.
