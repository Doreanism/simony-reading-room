I said *according to the Canons*, because whether it is inflicted by a Canon or by a judge—and not by a Canon—still, in order that it be just, it must be inflicted according to the Canons. I said *prohibiting* or *restraining*, because the punishment of which we speak is either understood to restrain only as a prohibition, or, if in some further mode (which is spoken of in the second article), this mode is contained under “to restrain” or “to constrict.”

In particular there are seven such canonical punishments, namely deposition, degradation, infamy, irregularity, excommunication, interdict, and suspension. Of these we must speak in order: each one in itself, how each is incurred, and by what remedy each is removed; and we must compare them with one another according to their gravity and permanence.

The first (c), namely deposition, is the greatest, because it is total removal from the clerical state. If degradation be a removal from every clerical grade, then deposition and degradation are the same. But if degradation be deposition only from a determinate grade, with another grade nevertheless reserved—as when, from the priestly grade, the clerical grade remains—then degradation is a kind of partial punishment in relation to deposition.
