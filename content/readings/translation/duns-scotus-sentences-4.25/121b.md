(o) *From four crimes pertaining to orders*, etc. The first crime is simony in an order, knowingly committed. That this entails irregularity he proves from causa 1, question 1, chapter *De caetero*, and from the places cited in the argument to the opposite, under the same title. Several chapters carry the same penalty. Next, he says that dispensation belongs to the Pontiff alone, from the chapter *Nobis*, *On Simony*; thus the Doctor’s allegation is to be sorted out. Third, he says that if simony has been committed by another, in the candidate’s favor and unknown to him, who gave the price, the candidate is not made irregular — unless after the error has been established \[he continues to act\], because in that case he remains suspended from his order until he is released. But if in that state he performs an act of his order, he remains irregular.

As to the first point: those who hold, from the chapter *Is qui*, *On the Sentence of Excommunication*, in the *Sext*, that irregularity is not incurred unless the penalty is expressly stated by law — and likewise that consequently it cannot be imposed by man — deny that those laws comprehend irregularity, but \[say\] only the penalty of deposition, or degradation, or suspension. And this consistently, since, as is clear from that title, several of these contain a sentence even of a judge, and the same form of words expresses the mode of the penalty; otherwise it would have to be said that this penalty can sometimes come from a judge. But from this an inconvenience arises: namely, that the penalty of irregularity can scarcely be proven to have been in use in the ancient decrees, since they make no mention of it under its proper name but use the words deposition, suspension, ejection, voiding, and the like.
