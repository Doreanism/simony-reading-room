Since, then, the judge is a living law, and his power is not limited by the canons with respect to this penalty more than with respect to any other, and since he does not otherwise exceed his ordinary power unless a limitation or reservation of this power is shown — which is not found among the canons — it follows from this head that it can be imposed by a judge according to the laws, that is, in a similar crime in which the law imposes such a penalty. And at the very least there is no doubt that the Roman Pontiff can impose this penalty by way of sentence for this or that offense of a private person. The only thing that stands in the way is that by usage and custom this penalty is inflicted by the canon alone. Whether this usage carries the force of law is uncertain, although in practice it is to be followed, and it now seems that this penalty should be reserved to the Roman Pontiff alone. Nor do I see why some of the aforesaid Doctors should so strongly oppose this opinion — those who say that some irregularity is properly a censure, like Soto and Córdoba and certain other more recent authors. For whether it be called medicinal or truly vindictive, it does not seem to exceed the power of a judge, no more than do other censures, such as even perpetual suspension from an order. Whatever may also be said about irregularity for the reception of orders, irregularity from the use of orders does not seem, in its character as a penalty, to exceed perpetual suspension or removal (*dispositio*) from an order, which can be inflicted by man.
