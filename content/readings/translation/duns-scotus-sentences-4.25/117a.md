Because, however, to these is annexed the actual possession of good name, which no one is bound by himself to renounce before he is convicted by the notoriety of the fact or by a sentence, with the judicial order observed, he is not bound to execute the penalty of the law to the prejudice of the actual possession of his good name, but he is bound, so far as he can, to abstain from legitimate acts and to provide for himself by procuring restoration.

Our \[author\] Castro, book 2 *De lege poenali*, c. 15, although in conclusion 4 he holds that a civil penalty pronounced *ipso jure* against a crime, even an occult one, holds, makes an exception at the end for the penalty of infamy, on the ground that infamy depends on common opinion, which remains intact for him whose crime is not notorious but occult. But infamy, as it is taken here as a penalty depriving of offices and dignity and rendering one unfit to acquire them, differs greatly from infamy by which one is defamed before others for a crime committed, as the Doctor rightly notes at the beginning of this text. The crime perpetrated, therefore, consummates the cause of the penalty through the sentence pronounced of the law itself, and the public knowledge of it that subsequently arises has this effect: that it takes away also the actual possession of good name, as far as the legitimate acts of which such a person is deprived.

Infamy of law is when a sentence and a judicial process intervene; moreover a definitive sentence is required to incur it, nor does an interlocutory one suffice, *l. Verbum, c. Ex quibus causis*, and the final chapter *de temporibus ordinandorum*, where the gloss and commonly the doctors \[so hold\], and on c. *Vestra* and the last chapter *de cohabitatione clericorum et mulierum*, c. 1 *de exceptionibus*, etc. For infamy as a canonical penalty a judicial process is required, from the chapter cited *de temporibus ordinandorum*: *If the crimes proposed*, it says, *are proved by judicial order*, etc.; a judicial order, however, requires accusation.
