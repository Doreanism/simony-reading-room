The Doctor distinguishes three kinds of homicide, namely simply voluntary, voluntary in a qualified sense, and accidentally voluntary. Simply voluntary homicide occurs when the killing is intended in itself, or in its proximate and inevitable cause of death — for example, when someone directly intends to kill another, or even if he does not directly conceive of inflicting death, he nevertheless intends an action which of itself inevitably brings about death, such as striking someone with a sword, and as forcefully as he can, and wherever he can, or hurling him from a tower, or administering a poison lethal of itself; for he who wills the proximate cause also wills the effect, if it follows so far as is in his power. In voluntary homicide, however, in the style of the Law, the Doctors distinguish homicide as it is against justice, and homicide lawful, or at any rate unlawful as being against charity — for example, when a judge kills a guilty person according to justice, when such a penalty is owed by law, it is lawful homicide. But when he kills someone out of hatred, yet according to justice and because of demerit, he sins against charity and not against justice, because such a penalty is owed by law. Voluntary homicide in either mode, however, induces irregularity, as the Doctor says, and is clear from the laws cited.

But with respect to irregularity and its reservation, “voluntary homicide” is understood as homicide unjustly committed, which is reserved to the Pope alone, as appears from the terms of the Jubilee, the Bull of Crusade, and the Privileges, in which the faculty of dispensing in this kind of homicide is reserved to the Pope; and from the Council of Trent, session 24, ch. 6, even though the homicide be occult.
