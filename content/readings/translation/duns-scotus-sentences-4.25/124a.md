### Scholium.

He explains exactly how slaves, illegitimates, and those shamefully mutilated or infirm are irregular, by express laws. See the Summists, *v. Irregularitas*; Henriquez, book 14, chapter 8; Suarez, tome 5, disputation 49, and the two following.

There follow (q) also irregularities from three non-crimes, namely servitude, distinction 54, as in *Extra, de servis non ordinandis*, throughout. The reason is that a slave is the property of his master, and therefore ought not to be taken from him against his will. When, however, he is ordained, he becomes incapable of certain servile acts, because if he was in fact ordained without his master’s knowledge, and without his subsequent consent, he must be returned to him; nevertheless, the master ought not to apply him to acts unbecoming his order, but he ought to serve his master in acts becoming his state.

There follows (r) also illegitimacy, *Extra, de filiis Presbyterorum non ordinandis*, chapters 1 and 2. The reason is that they are presumed imitators of their fathers’ incontinence; such men are also presumed to be ill-mannered, since ill brought up; nevertheless, dispensations are granted in their case with sufficient ease. Hence Benedict XI, in the case of a certain such man who had been ordained without dispensation and was ministering in orders, afterward dispensed easily — as I myself saw the bull of dispensation.

There follows (s) also a third non-crime, namely shameful or enormous mutilation, or infirmity, as is had in *Extra, de corpore vitiatis non ordinandis*, chapter *Exposuisti*, and distinction 55, *Si Evangelica*, and the preceding chapter, and *Extra, de Clerico debilitato*.
