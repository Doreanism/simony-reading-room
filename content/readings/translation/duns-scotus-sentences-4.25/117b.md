*sitionis, etc., de accusationibus* says that a sentence given by way of inquisition without judicial order is not valid. There is required, therefore, for incurring this infamy, a deed marked by the law with such infamy, and a sentence inflicting the penalty, so that it is actually incurred. This, however, happens either when the delict is not notorious except through a judicial process and a declarative sentence; or when the law does not impose the penalty *ipso facto*, but prescribes that it be imposed by the sentence of a judge.

From this is clear the threefold manner of incurring this penalty, which the Doctor prescribes, namely by the law itself, or by a judge, or by both together. By the law itself, when the law itself draws and applies the penalty *ipso facto* without the sentence of a judge; by a judge, however, when the law does not comprehend a pronounced sentence but one to be pronounced by a judge. In both ways, when the delict is occult, from which the penalty is incurred *ipso facto*, and is proved by judicial process, and is made notorious by a public and declarative sentence — because then good name is completely lost as to lordship and even factual possession, so that without injustice anyone may speak of the crime in public, which otherwise would not be lawful so long as the crime had not been proved and a sentence pronounced, although against charity one could be offended in this in certain circumstances.

(h) *But what is the cause on account of which?* etc. This text is clear from the law alleged; he cites moreover two texts, c. *Infames*, and c. *Omnes vero infames*, etc., which are found in C. 6 q. 1, and c. *Beatus Petrus*, in the same \[cause\]. It is to be noted, with the gloss on c. *Omnes*, that not every infamy induced by civil law is received by canon law, such as that which a widow incurs by marrying within the period of mourning.
