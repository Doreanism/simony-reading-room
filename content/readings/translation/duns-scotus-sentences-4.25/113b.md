### Commentary.

In this distinction the Doctor treats of the impediments or unfitness by which a person is excluded from receiving Orders, or from ministering in those received. It is divided into two questions, the first of which contains unfitness arising from canonical punishment, while the second treats of impediment arising from sex and age.

(a) *In this question*, etc. He divides the question into two articles. In the first he treats of the definition and division of canonical punishment, briefly and clearly comprehending the foundations of the laws and their resolution. In the second article he treats of the effect of canonical punishment as regards impediments to conferring Orders, or even to ministering in them—which he further explains in his reply to the principal arguments, which touch on the same matter.

(b) *As to the first, canonical punishment*, etc. Here he takes “punishment” broadly, so as to include both censures properly so called—such as excommunication, suspension, and interdict—and also impediments or privations introduced by the Canons, which apply to spiritual acts or to acts of Orders; and these are called punishments in the broad sense, whether they are incurred for a fault or on account of disobedience. Punishment thus taken is a genus in relation to censure; and so too the word *the one punished* in the definition is to be understood by correlation to punishment broadly taken. From these things the definition is plain. There are seven punishments taken in this mode, as is plain in the text.

(c) *The first, namely deposition, is the greatest*, etc. This punishment is found properly in ecclesiastical persons, inasmuch as it is spiritual; for it is the privation of their grade and function—from their Order, from their office, and consequently from their benefice (which is given on account of the office); and when the deposition is total, it also removes their state, that is, the clerical privilege.
