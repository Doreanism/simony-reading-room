But an infant is capable of baptism, the Eucharist, and confirmation independently of his own consent or that of his parents, as the common view holds; therefore he is also capable of ordination. And, as has been said, the instance drawn from the capacity for minor orders proves that no such consent is required for receiving the power of the character; therefore on no count is it required. The same is to be said of the episcopate, as has been said, since it too can be conferred by consecration on someone to whom no church is conferred as a title—although by this he does not receive proximate power over the mystical body. Furthermore, the power that a priest receives of binding and loosing is a remote power, and according to St. Thomas it is one of dignity and not of order. If, therefore, it can be conferred on an infant, then so too can the episcopal power, even if it were of dignity alone; but we have said above that it pertains to order, and therefore the reasoning is the same concerning it and concerning the others.

Durandus objects that it is absurd for an infant to be made a priest or a bishop.

I reply that it is absurd in the sense of being illicit, but not absurd in the sense of being invalidly done.

He objects, second, that consent is required.

I reply that from what has been said the contrary is clear, as appears in ordination to the minor orders. Therefore, neither from the nature of this particular power nor from the nature of power in general is such consent required, when the conferral of the power takes place by command and independently of the will of the recipient.
