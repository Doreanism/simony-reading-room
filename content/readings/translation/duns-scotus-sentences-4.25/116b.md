But if the delict becomes public and notorious, then the penalty of the law is incurred, which is also drawn back to the time of the commission of the delict as regards the effect of nullity of acts, because the law annuls them, as being done by an infamous person. There are excepted the acts of public administration, which hold by reputed title, lest the commonwealth, or others, suffer harm through the fraud of a private person; particular acts, however, which concern personal favor, are declared void, as ordination already received, as to its use, D. 81 c. *Tantis*, *Daniel*, etc., \[the chapter\] *Cadit \[a\] dignitatibus, de excessibus Praelatorum*, c. *Inter dilectos*, the rule *Infamibus, de regulis Juris* in the Sixth, and C. 3 q. 7.

But what is to be done in such a case, when the crime is occult? The answer is given according to Panormitanus on c. *Ut si Clerici, etc., de Judiciis*: that a prelate ought to renounce \[his office\] in order to escape the sentence of infamy. This indeed is well, if he can renounce without betrayal and disgrace of himself; he is bound to abstain so far as he can from those acts for which by the incurred penalty he is rendered unfit *ipso facto*. Nevertheless no one is bound to execute this penalty of the law against himself with such grave detriment; he ought therefore to seek his restoration prudently, in a way which provides for himself and for his conscience.
