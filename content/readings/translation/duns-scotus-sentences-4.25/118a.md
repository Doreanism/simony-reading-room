\[this effect\]. Moreover, there are other crimes on account of which the canons themselves directly inflict infamy, and these are enumerated in the chapter *Infames*. From this arises the well-known division of infamy into civil and canonical, etc.

(i) *What is the remedy against this penalty?* etc. The common resolution is this: canonical purgation removes infamy arising from suspicion of a crime, which is called popular suspicion and is not legitimately proven. Infamy arising from a public sin is removed through penance, provided the sin is not otherwise specially marked with the penalty of infamy. This too is canonical, in accordance with the said chapter, so long as the person perseveres in that state. Civil infamy can be removed directly by the prince, and when it ceases the canonical impediment ceases indirectly as well — unless the sin on account of which it is incurred is otherwise specifically marked with infamy by the canon; in such case penance does not suffice to remove the infamy that is a special penalty by law, but full restitution (*restitutio in integrum*) is required, as the Doctor and the common opinion rightly hold. This penalty is greater than deposition as regards civil life, says the Doctor, unless the deposition itself includes infamy. Real deposition, indeed, on account of a crime, and verbal deposition indirectly, include infamy or carry it with them *ipso jure* — though not every deposition from some grade entails infamy. For instance, if someone is wrongly promoted in good faith on his own part, even though a defect creeps in from elsewhere — e.g., if one suspended from orders ordains a man ignorant of his suspension. It can also be understood in another way: namely, that deposition, taken directly and in the intent of the law that imposes such a penalty (speaking of complete deposition), indirectly entails the infamy which the concurrent law of infamy directly inflicts. But if the deposition is not complete, it does not necessarily entail this penalty, unless the same cause of deposition and of infamy concurs by law or by fact. On this material the Doctors may be consulted at the texts cited, and in the matters of accusation, witnesses, election, exceptions, infamy and the irregularity flowing from it, and censures, where Suárez treats this material at length in disputation 48.
