Thus the Doctor rightly establishes this difference in a moral sense, for the other features that belong to suspension also belong, wholly or in part, to the other penalties, apart from the difference noted.

(b) *This [suspension] is manifold, etc.* — from office, from benefice, from entry into the church, from the exercise of an order received from one who could not confer its exercise, and likewise from an order knowingly received from a simoniac. The Doctor does not mention suspension from the order itself, because this is included in suspension from office laid down absolutely, which deprives one of all use of order, jurisdiction, and ecclesiastical office, as is commonly taught: *cap. Cum dilectus, de consuetud.*, Panormitanus, *Clement. Capientes*, *Caveant. de sentent. excomunic. in 6.*, *cap. ultim. de cleric. excommun. injuste*. One suspended from office, and likewise one suspended from order, who exercises an act of order becomes irregular: *cap. Cum aeterni, de sententia et re judicata in 6.* This suspension from office is understood to be from order and jurisdiction, but not from benefice — as the gloss on *Clement. Capientes* says. This is clear from practice, since suspension from office and benefice together is often imposed, an addition that would be superfluous if suspension from office already included benefice. One suspended from benefice is not for that reason suspended from office: gloss on *cap. Unitoniensis, de elect.*, Panormitanus on *cap. Cum dilectus, de consuetud.* Navarrus qualifies this: when jurisdiction does not belong only by reason of the benefice (*cap. 27 in Summa*). One suspended from a benefice is suspended from receiving its fruits. When suspension is imposed simply, the greater is understood — that is, suspension from all things — because nothing limits it, and it must have some determinate effect; otherwise it would be ineffectual.
