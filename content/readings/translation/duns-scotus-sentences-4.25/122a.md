Either, then, this penalty is not included in the ancient decrees, or it is incurred on account of the sin of simony. Likewise, *Extra de Simonia*, chapter *Nobis*, etc., near the end, where a simoniacal election is said to be rejected, and that the bishop cannot dispense with the one elected; but dispensation presupposes irregularity. Likewise, the chapter *Per tuas*, etc., decrees that a deacon ordained simoniacally is not to ascend to the higher orders, nor is he to minister in those already received, unless he has been dispensed; the chapter *Si quis ordinaverit*, etc., near the end: one ordained simoniacally remains suspended from the order until he has been dispensed; and by that suspension irregularity can be understood, since a suspension is not properly dispensed with, as a censure is. From which it is clear that one ordained simoniacally incurs this penalty on account of simony itself, and not only on account of the censure annexed to it.

In the new law, however, as in the Extravagans of Paul II, *Cum detestabile*, real simony is punished by excommunication and by the other censures and penalties imposed against simoniacs in any manner whatsoever. The Council of Trent, session 21, chapter 1, established the same penalties against those who ordain simoniacally. Likewise, by the decrees of Sixtus V against those who promote and are promoted wrongly, which, although Clement VIII reduced others to the terms of the common law, nevertheless as to the penalty against those promoted and promoting simoniacally, he left it in force. Through these laws, therefore, the penalty of irregularity is revived, if it ever was imposed against simoniacs in an order. It seems, therefore, that one must say that the suspension by way of penalty imposed in the ancient law against those wrongly promoted by simony, and which is by some called perpetual, does not differ from irregularity, nor is it properly a censure; rather, that medicinal and temporary suspension which presupposes contumacy is a censure.
