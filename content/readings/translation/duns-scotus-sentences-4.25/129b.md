Now who is the minister of the Law in inflicting this penalty has been said above in dist. 19, in the same place: because for simply excommunicating, no one suffices except him to whom all the faithful are subject; therefore no one else either, except as a commissioner by his authority.

How (r) is it relaxed? I reply: by absolution given by him who excommunicated, if it is excommunication by a judge or by his superior. If, however, it is by Law, by the absolution of him who enacted it — and this not only, as many say, but by the absolution of any priest whatsoever, when the legislator has not reserved that absolution to himself; and they prove this by the chapter *Nuper* of the *Extra*, *De sententia excommunicationis*. But that proof has little force, because it does not follow: “he does not reserve, therefore he conceded” — speaking of the form of consequence, and even according to the Laws — because the power of absolving falls under this rule: “What is not granted is to be considered prohibited,” because it does not belong to anyone to absolve from a sentence imposed by a superior unless it has been granted to him.

Comparing, however, this penalty with the preceding, this is simply more grave in respect of its intention, because it excludes from every otherwise lawful communion, whereas the other excludes only from those things which pertain to Orders; but this is reckoned lighter, because it is more easily remitted. But this turns to the opposite \[conclusion\]: for it is more easily remitted precisely because it is most grave, and excludes from more things.
