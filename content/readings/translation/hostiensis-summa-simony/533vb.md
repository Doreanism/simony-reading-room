## What Simony Is

What simony is. It is indeed a deliberate will or desire to buy or sell spiritual things, or things annexed to spiritual things, according to Damasus and the other ancient doctors. But this description does not seem good, because it implies that simony is committed by intention alone. This, however, is false as regards the Church, though true as regards God, as at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], *Tua nos* at the end. For if someone, drawn by such an intention, enters a monastery and is received on condition that he would give something, yet enters with no pact interposed, even though he afterwards gives something, he is not compelled to renounce the benefice or share thus acquired, nor is the recipient compelled to return what he received. Rather, such simony is wiped away by penance, because here he does not offend the Church but God alone, as at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], cap. ult.: “Because intention judges the man,” at C. 22 q. 5 *Quamplura* and following. But the Church does not judge of hidden things, but God alone, as at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], cap. *Sicut* at the end, and C. 32 q. 5 *Christiana*. For if all that is committed in this world were punished by the Church, divine judgments would have no place, as at C. 1 q. 4 *Si omnia*.

But contrast \[Extra\] *de usuris*, *Consuluit*, and C. 14 q. 3 cap. 1, where usury is committed by intention alone without a pact. And if it is said that this is false in the judicial forum, as at \[Extra\] *de usuris*, *In civitate*, yet in the forum of conscience the man is held a usurer such that he must restore what he has thus received, as the decretal *Consuluit* says. Why then is it not the same here, so that in the forum of conscience the abbot is bound to restore the price, and the monk to renounce the benefice or fellowship thus acquired? It can be said that usury is condemned by both laws indifferently, and therefore is prohibited in the New Testament because it was condemned in the Old.

The same therefore holds in things prohibited, since the simoniacal is like the things forbidden as sacred. Whence the Lord also cast the sellers of doves out of the temple, as at C. 1 q. 1 *Cum multis*; and so can be understood C. 1 q. 1 *Qui studet* and cap. *Reperiuntur* and q. 3 *Non solum*, and C. 1 q. 1 *Consequens*. But the decretal *Hic*, cited at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], cap. ult., speaks of simoniacs, because the buying \[of a benefice\], although in the Old Testament it was not prohibited, is today simoniacal — because it is prohibited; and so notes Vincentius at \[Extra\] *de officio delegati*, *Ex parte* I. Whence today too it is simoniacal to buy a vicarage, a stewardship, an advocacy, and such like, as at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], *Ad nostram*, C. 1 q. 1 *Si quis episcopus*, and q. 3 *Salvator*. And in these things the will alone does not constitute the offense, but an act is required, according to Goffredus. This is true as regards the Church, as I have said.

Or it can be said that the reason it is otherwise at \[Extra\] eodem \[titulo\], cap. fin. — namely, that he is not bound to restore to him what he gave — is because a monk can have nothing of his own; whence, however much one should restore the price to him, it would always remain the monastery’s, as at \[Extra\] *de statu monachorum*, *Cum ad monasterium*. But he who gives money to a usurer would have it restored, because he has it as his own.

From this it appears that if a bishop, drawn by such hope, ordains a secular cleric and afterwards receives the money which he had hoped for, in the forum of conscience he is bound to return the money. And to the cleric it must be said that he had the same intention, so that he must not exercise his office until it has been dispensed with him. But what of celebrating in the church?
