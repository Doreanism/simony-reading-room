## But with those who have been received this side of the council

But with those who have been received this side of the council, some are dispensed by the pope; and this extends from monks even to abbots — but monks are not compelled to renounce spiritual things, nor abbots to renounce temporalities, which were thereby acquired with nothing antecedent but the intention alone. Say this as noted above eodem § *quid sit*. You have it below eodem c. ult., where it says that the palium alone abolishes such a pact. And so gather that an injury done to God is more easily and safely remitted than one done to man: by which is plain the reason which Iohannes seeks above eodem § rh., verse *suspensus*; or see \[further\].

## But what is the reason

But what is the reason why a secular convicted by way of inquisition is degraded, but not a regular? I answer: because in the inquisition of a secular the subtlety of law is required, and therefore in these excepted crimes judgment is pronounced by strict law; but in the inquisition of a regular one proceeds summarily, and therefore he would be punished summarily — as is plain from what is noted above eodem § *quid de accusatione*, and under the title *de accusationibus*, in the rubric *de inquisitionibus*. Likewise, because the rule is approved that while one is burdened in one respect, he is relieved in another: as below in the next, verse *sed et episcopus*, and the following.

## What if someone offers his goods to the church

What if someone offers his goods to the church simply, without a pact, but with this intention: that for the temporal goods which he offers he may obtain spiritual ones — not otherwise being about to give them, nor would the clerks otherwise have received him? To this the decree says that nevertheless both that man and these clerks are judged guilty before the strict judge who is the searcher of hearts: below eodem, *tua* § *sed aut*. Whence, since it does not say “they are judged simoniacs,” they are to be punished on account of the depraved intention. There have been those who said that they are not simoniacs, but nevertheless are to be punished as simoniacs on account of the depraved intention together with the work that followed: argument above *de bigamis non ordinandis* in fi., c. c. *a nobis*.

## You say that they are simoniacs

You say that they are simoniacs: because simony is committed by the will alone — which say as noted above c. verse *si vero scienter*, and above eodem § 1.

## What if the monastery does not abound

What if the monastery does not abound — indeed is meagre and pressed by great poverty? Can money be given and received without the vice of simony? Not very well; nevertheless, it can be conceded that these words may be spoken by those of the convent to one wishing to enter: “We will freely and gratis receive you into our spiritual fellowship; but since our goods do not suffice for us and for you, bring with you the means whereby you may live”; or thus: “provide for yourself so that you may live.” For then if the one entering brings any temporal goods, simony is not committed. Nor is it an objection that below eodem *quoniam* it says that some wish to palliate this vice under the pretext of poverty; for that \[passage\] is to be understood when one does not proceed thus cautiously — rather, those receiving make a pact and a fixed forum, and when they have so acted, they cannot excuse themselves under pretext of poverty.

## But this pretext does not absolve the delinquent

But this pretext does not absolve the delinquent. ff. *de ritu nuptiarum*, *palam* § *non est ignoscendum*; above *de divortiis*, *ex literis*. For it would be more holy to die in reputation, C. 32 q. 4, *sicut san*\[ctius\]
