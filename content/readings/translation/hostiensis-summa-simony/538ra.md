For this \[suspension\] continues to bar the execution of his order even after the penance has been carried out: §. *de renunciatione*, *nisi cum pridem*; D. 50, *de his*; \[Extra\] *de temporibus ordinationum*, c. fi. Or, \[the point is that\] although such a sinner — that is, one not cut off but tolerated and penitent — can celebrate, nevertheless a simoniac cannot: as below, eodem, *tanta*. But on the second view, below eodem, *tue*, Iacobus says that this is speaking of the occult case. But Petrus understands *tanta* of the notorious case: for he is suspended, C. 1 q. 1, *reperiatur*; C. 32 q. 2 §. *verum*.

## And understand this with respect to others

For with respect to himself he is suspended, however occult he may be, just like any sinner: §. *de cohabitatione clericorum et mulierum*, c. vl.; C. 1 q. 1, *si sacerdotes*. Or say that if he is a simoniac in respect of orders and occult, he is suspended with respect to all, since he does not have the character — understand \[this\] \[with respect to execution\], C. 1 q. 1 c. 1 — and so the chapter *tanta* speaks accordingly.

## And the church

\[Extra\] eodem, *ex tue*, says that it can prohibit him from execution: understand, in fact. That is to say, in fact it can carry out \[the prohibition\], even though the crime is occult, provided you yourself know him as such. But why can you not prohibit him? — see \[Extra\] *de temporibus ordinationum*, c. *contra*, *nulla*, and *de officio ordinarii*, *si sacerdos*. Nevertheless, understand this according to Sina in the case of one who has received a benefice simoniacally which required a certain order, as is read \[Extra\] *de etate et qualitate*, c. 1; and in the case of a homicide, as below, *de accusationibus*, *inquisitionis*; \[Extra\] *de temporibus ordinationum*, *qualitum*. But he who confers the order is not suspended except with respect to himself; nor is the simoniac in religion: as §. eodem, *veniens*, and c. *dilectus filius*.

## Whence he who confers the order

Since he is not suspended, he gives execution to others by the law itself. But he who has received \[orders\] simoniacally cannot give it, because he does not have it: above, q. 5, *Daibertum*. And therefore he cannot celebrate, C. 1 q. 1, *sicut eunuchi*, and c. *quibusdam narrantibus*. But others say this with respect to the power of office, not the power of merit: as below, eodem, *tanta*. For with respect to the power of office a heretic can celebrate, as above, q. 1, *quidam*.

## But Iohannes said

That whoever has once conferred an order simoniacally will not thereafter be able to give execution, however occult it be, however ignorant the recipient: as above, q. 1, *si quis a simoniacis*; and therefore the gloss *misericorditer*; and eodem q., *reperiuntur*, and c. *quibusdam*. But this seems to be refuted below, c. *plus*, §. *p qua*. Whence at home if I have received an order from one whom I did not know to be a simoniac, I have execution, even if I have heard this from some, unless my conscience reproved me: as you have, \[Extra\] *de sententia excommunicationis*, *cum illorum*, rh. 1, and q. si., where in the same case the secular is deposed, but the regular is only suspended. And some say also that a simoniac, however occult, is suspended with respect to all: as I noted above, and \[as in the case of\] the notorious fornicator, D. 32 §. *verum*, and c. *nullus*, and c. *preter hoc*, and c. *si queras*. Why do the canon laws so prosecute these two crimes alike? Answer: simony is a cancerous disease, and so the red-hot iron is needed; it is also a vice that ought to be avoided, C. 1 q. 1, *reperiuntur*. Likewise, fornication is a common vice, D. 50, *quod tua sanctitas* in fi.; and so in both the penalty ought to be sharpened, ff. *de penis*, *aut facta*, §. fin.
