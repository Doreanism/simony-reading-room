ged: C. 14 q. 6, *si res*. But this I do not believe: indeed, the pope can dispense with such a one, since he can voluntarily offer himself for deposition: D. 21, *nunc autem*. If he can renounce it as far as in him lies, and if the pope dispenses with him, the vice of orders will be purged: C. 1 q. 5, *vitium*. For the vice of theft too is purged by the stolen property remaining with the thief, the owner being willing: ff. *de usucapionibus*, *si fur*; C. 1 q. 1, *quidam*, verse *si forte*. The same in a violent detainer: ff. *de precario*, *certe*, c. *Iulianus*. And this is confirmed by \[Extra\] eodem, *per tuas*, and q. 2, c. pen. Much more so if he is not a simoniac — although simoniacally promoted, that is, because the money was given without his knowing — and so perhaps in this case, if it is hidden, the bishops will be able to dispense: argument from \[Extra\] eodem, *simoniace*.

But what if it concerns one simoniacally promoted to some religious order? Say as above pri. §. *si vero*. A dispensation can be applied by the bishop, if the money was given without his knowledge. Others say it should be referred for discussion to a general council: \[Extra\] eodem, *quoniam*. But if someone has simoniacally obtained another benefice, if it was a simple one, after his free resignation of it he can recover that benefice by dispensation of the bishop — or even of the chapter, the bishop consenting: above *de electione*, *si alicui*, at the end; \[Extra\] eodem, *ex insinuatione*. But if he has obtained a prelacy or dignity, if he comes to repentance without any accusation, and of his own motion has renounced, humbly petitioning that his renunciation be admitted, then once the renunciation is made he can recover that prelacy by dispensation of his bishop — who in this has not transgressed — provided the utility or necessity of the church requires it: as C. 1 q. 5, *vitium*, r. c. *quibus*. But if he has contested in court, or being summoned has confessed for fear of the proofs, the bishop cannot dispense with him on that occasion: as above *de electione*, *si alicuius*, et c.; \[Extra\] eodem, *nobis fuit* §. 1. According to this, understand C. 1 q. 1, *erga*; C. 7 q. 1, *vitium*; above *de electione*, *super eo*, r.; C. 1 q. 1, c. 1, and c. ult. Or more briefly say that in a simple benefice the bishop dispenses with the chapter — with or without cause — provided that the prerogative of merits requires it: thus understand above *de electione*, *si alicuius*, at the end; below eodem, *ex insinuatione*.

In a double \[benefice\] he is able to dispense, even on this occasion, if the necessity of the church requires it: as in c. *vitium*. Otherwise the bishop cannot dispense: as in the said c. *nobis fuit* §. *verum est*, on that occasion; but the see \[Apostolic\] is able to dispense: as in c. *si alicuius* at the beginning. And so the ancient decretum of Stephanus determines, *si alicui*; Bernardus determines, with Innocentius, *nobis fuit*: ff. *de legibus*, *non est nouum*, and l. *sequenti*. What I said — that the bishop can dispense — is to be understood when the money was given not to him but to one of his associates without his knowledge; otherwise, if he himself had transgressed, he would be unworthy of the power of dispensing: argument from above *de clericis coniugatis*, *diuersis*. This is intimated in C. 1 q. 5, *vitium*, where it says that money was given not to the bishop, but to one of his princes.
