When the wife of Zebedee, Jesus’s aunt, prayed to him that her two children, Saint James and Saint John, the cousins of our Lord, might be seated, the one on his right hand and the other on his left hand, his answer was: *Nescitis quid petatis.* They were unworthy to be placed near him, and so God said to her, “You do not know what you are asking. You may indeed endure suffering as I do, but to set you on my right hand is not mine to give you: *sed quibus paratum est a patre meo*”—that is, to those who are worthy.

Fourthly, those who make a pact to give a certain sum in order to be received into a monastery so well endowed that it is sufficient to support and provide both for those already there and for those they will receive—these are simoniacs. And likewise those who carry out such a reception, or who give counsel or consent to it, where there is no custom to the contrary that might excuse the sin.

Fifthly, the clergy of the Church should administer the sacraments and the burials without making any pact or demand for money; otherwise there is simony. But once they have done their duty, the laity should compensate them according to the ancient customs; otherwise one may have them brought before the bishop according to the law. *Extra De symonia ad apostolicam.* The sacraments of the Church should not be sold, for that would be simony.
