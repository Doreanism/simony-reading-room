On account of the goodness that he sees and knows in him, he should grant him a benefice in that church, and this is not simony but rather well done. For in the courts of prelates one ought to put the clerks to the test. Likewise, in directing one’s charity, one should give first to one’s own household before strangers, provided they are equally worthy.

People say that those who give, or procure, or improperly make prayers and petitions in order to obtain benefices for their kinsmen, servants, or other friends are simoniacs and should be excommunicated, according to what Saint Thomas says, and likewise the synod: *Si quis avaritia ductus amore servitio vel precibus episcopalem vel sacerdotalem acceperit dignitatem: et in vitam reliquerit eumque in asperam penitentiam mors non invenerit in eternum peribit. i.q.i.ca. Si quis.* That is: if anyone has obtained an episcopal or sacerdotal dignity led by avarice, by love, by service, or by entreaties, and has not relinquished it during his life, and at death has not found himself in sharp penance, he shall pay for it everlastingly. In granting a benefice to someone of one’s own blood, even though there be no simony, since there is no sale, there is nevertheless sin, which may be reckoned in three ways: either because he loves his own flesh too tenderly, or because he covets to give a thing improperly to his kinsman, or because he squanders the spiritual thing by giving it to one who is unworthy. And it would seem that he would be guilty of simony in this if he had regard to how his kinsman gains temporal profit through the spiritual office, inasmuch as he is a kinsman, for he would then receive something that should not be due to him.
