Second, a clerk who makes a pact with a churchman or another in order to obtain his title, promising that he will never demand anything inasmuch as it suffices him that the matter may go through, is deprived of the orders he receives, in such a way that no one may dispense him but the \[pope\], according to the law, *Extra de simonia, Per tuas*.

Third, two beneficed persons who agree together in this form—”let us exchange benefices, but inasmuch as your benefice is better than mine, I shall give you such a sum of money”—commit simony here, even though the one may grant a pension to the other without simony. And if they make the said exchange simply, without either demanding anything of the other, but they make gifts or promises to the patron in order to obtain his good will toward it, this too is simony in the same way; for since we may not make the exchange together without the consent of the bishop, let us both labor to obtain his consent. Such a pact would not be simoniacal if it were made simply, without any gift, and the said consent were not obtained by way of simony. Likewise, to renounce any benefice on such a condition and pact that it should be given to a particular person is simony, when the renunciation is not made to a person who is worthy to have it, simply and purely for the love of God.
