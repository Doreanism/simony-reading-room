For example, suppose someone gives something to the bishop’s attendants in order to move the bishop to grant some benefice. The bishop, who knows nothing of the gift, would by this means make over the benefice, yet such a grant would still be simoniacal. But only the person who made the gift to the bishop’s attendants would be in mortal sin and a simoniac in that respect. And the man beneficed in this way, without his own knowledge, would be bound—once it came to his knowledge—to renounce the said benefice and the other present revenues belonging to it; but he is not bound to restore the revenues already spent during the time of his ignorance. To know clearly when a man commits simony in granting benefices, or when someone renounces his benefice, or when someone gives a benefice, or when someone receives it, one must look at and consider the conditions, the circumstances, and the causes, and whether there are gifts, promises, or entreaties from lords or friends, and so forth. Moreover, one must above all consider the final end at which the man intends to arrive; for if the conditions and the purpose are entirely for the love of God, he has rightly entered the benefice by the door. But if a man enters a benefice otherwise than through God, simony is to be feared, for he has not entered by the door.
