\[from good works\] one seeks praise, since the speaker has said, as above. Thus \[the rule is\] not to seek temporal praise. Likewise, one nevertheless does a good work for the sake of earthly goods — yet not for praise or glory under the heading of seeking the good — and this I do not do thus; this is not for “a gift from the hand,” that is, to obtain earthly things — is it? But if other earthly goods, \[then\] to do for “a gift from the hand.” For, as we say, only let it not be so: \[one is bound\] to those things to which one is bound not for a reward, and to St. James \[i.e., on pilgrimage\], and one is not bound in this not for a reward; one is not bound in that action for a reward. Likewise, only not to promise was — he did not say “nonetheless” — already in act, already because he does not merit this, even if it is permitted.

Likewise, concerning the master who teaches for the sake of pence or the like, it is asked whether he falls under the curse. Likewise, not so if he acts out of avarice or vainglory; but of theologians it is said otherwise, since they are *publice persone* — public persons — and their lecturing bears witness to the truth. Therefore, since they are *doctores verbi dei* — teachers of the word of God — it is not lawful for them to teach for a reward, lest they be found *venditores verbi dei* — vendors of the word of God. And they hold no privilege of office that allows one who is publicly bound to do God’s \[work\] of teaching theology to do it for “a gift from the hand.” Whence the Lord says in Micah \[3:11\]: “because they were taking a gift” — but is it not \[also written\]: “their priests taught for hire” (*sacerdotes eorum in mercede docebant*)? But if a chancellor or a schoolmaster of boys holds his post for “a gift from the hand,” we shall say that — provided no scandal arises — he does so not in the labor of teaching but in \[teaching\] an art, since if he holds a necessary art, he does not earn ill, just as, with respect to St. James \[i.e., the pilgrimage case\], one is not to go from that case; therefore he does not earn ill in what he does. Likewise, the case is that he does the work of God negligently and defrauds him who gives without distinction, since on the prior intention he seems to hold a necessary art — for he ought \[to teach freely\] where another draws away, but does not. \[So\] I am not making my point about this art. Indeed, it is permitted to hold an art, and one is not approved if he does not earn his keep. If this one thing he does not give, in him he is not approved if he is not. But it is more \[meritorious\] to accept one donation than another, since one does not receive it on account of necessity. But if he holds an art as he holds his king \[i.e., his rightful claim\] — I do not approve; this is not what the Apostle holds, who has \[the right\] intention. Therefore he does not lose his merit, if it be on account of him who is in the field: in doing a good work, much more \[is this so\]. For he who is in the field acts on account of the Lord and not on his own account; therefore he does not earn ill. Because the other is a hireling — his status, his \[hope of\] reward gives him either pain or glory, as in the parable of the prodigal son: a hireling, who in hope of the desire for the kingdom of heaven and the head of heaven \[acts\] — such a hireling \[the Lord\] does not himself give mercy, and is not the Lord on account of those things. So that \[one ought to act\] not \[for the sake of\] God \[as a means\]; rather, that He alone may be known to you, not for another’s sake, give yourself, but that you may live unto eternity. Likewise: “if you eat or drink, do all in the glory of God” — likewise to the Colossians: “Whatever you do, in word or in deed, do all in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Likewise this precept, that every action be \[so directed\], therefore if in mortal \[sin\], he who fulfills all the precepts \[is to be reckoned\]. For just as another has the intention to give a hundred shillings and, while giving, dispenses the tenth coin lawfully with a fellow on the boundary which you commit without intention to commit — and \[then\] one committing it, properly proceeding far, by intention by the very thing has revoked it — he cannot return; and yet because, just as one without intention does not now revoke him or did not in intention revoke \[him\] — but this is not the same in management as in action. Likewise it is then asked: he who was in a meritorious act, his intention being interrupted, the remainder \[of the act\] in them not being meritorious — therefore if he is in the field or at home and speaks with his fellow, that he is joined to him is not a meritorious action with respect to this; for that meritorious action existed only \[as long as\] in that act without speaking far with his fellow or with him only, and in habit it is no longer meritorious; he does not recover by intention back to him, but not because he is compelled \[to it\] in another by a meritorious work — this he says at the end if \[…\]
