# On the Work of God

He calls “the work of God” any work whatsoever that is done either out of charity or by precept. If one acts out of charity by grace, or by precept by grace, the curse falls upon such a person \[only when the work is corrupted\]. By this distinction the Apostle calls a work of piety a work of God when it is done by grace; and such a work, when done either of itself or through negligence, falls under the malediction whenever it is performed in such a way that the doer is already cursed in the doing. But it must be asked why one who acts in this way out of charity and grace incurs the curse — for example, in the matter of his prayer — since no one ought to chant in such a way as to lack grace. To this question the answer is that he who acts thus by precept and by grace, even if of himself he is feeble, must nevertheless take care diligently to avoid sloth (*accidia*). The Apostle calls this sloth “negligence,” for if he who is so reproached is reproached not for the work of God itself but as one who is, so to speak, reproved for slipping into negligence. The Apostle therefore calls it “to do the work of God gratuitously”; but, according to the division given in the chapter on maledictions, if a man performs the work choosing to attend diligently, or by habit with good will to render the duty owed to God, then under such conditions he does not fall under the curse — for the work of God exceeds, as the Apostle says. The Apostle further says, in the fifth place, that whoever commits this kind of negligence \[falls under the curse\] — and the negligence is mortal or venial according as it touches what is vital. I say truly that when such negligence is mortal, then the malediction holds: as for instance if a man, fleeing his duty, falls into shameful usury contrary to precept, holding back what is owed, or who, when he ought to go forth to feed the poor, would rather perish of hunger himself. The same applies to him who ought to feed the poor and fails through such mortal negligence; mortal sloth, as in the case of one going to market \[instead of to the office\], the curse there falls. As to the chapter “*In dies*” concerning him who is accustomed to recite matins out of mere routine without devotion, or who, beginning matins with devotion, lets thought slip away in the end, that sloth is venial in the recitation. But you ask whether one ought to break off there: I say that if you study, if you know yourself to be turning away in various ways from divine grace and not preserving it, but rather, as it were, despising grace if in truth, or if then you give it secretly — then in this you sin, since one ought, you say, to proceed by good reason and devotion. If devotion does not follow from this reason, do not for that reason go on, unless out of devotion or by today’s reason or change of devotion you persist; for the same sloth attacks the choir of hours over the master, and yet by good intention — by which I grant indeed to the right intention what was found — that one who at the beginning of the hours says “*Intende in adiutorium meum*” and at the end “*intende*,” from beginning to end intends \[as it were\] in his innermost being, it stands that even our \[recitation\], though often interrupted, is brought to perfection. So too it is said that he is dispersed in good devotion when he recites \[and yet completes\]. And he who does not pray there \[as he ought\] — whence above on lengthy prayers, “in which you put your trust by reason of length” — it is said with him \[Romatus?\] that he said “if I knew myself” \[to recite\] in himself without devotion, in scripture, where he held to weakness lest in truth, when he did not know — and over this I say that no one is in the like situation, and the test, where I have seen him, makes prolongation through sacrifice; where indeed it is recited with good intention, sacred fact and \[a remedy for\] sleep — even the sacrament, even if without devotion, or though many a learned man may be found in words at the end without devotion, I grant that he goes under that curse, “Cursed.”
