## On a gift consisting in service.

I ask whether one who serves a prelate in order to obtain spiritual benefices, or \[serves\] a monastery in order to be received into it, is in this way a simoniac. \[Look at\] *c. cum eterne, eo. ti.* The gloss there holds that he is not — when he serves without any agreement, and with the intention of obtaining the aforesaid things secondarily, not as the primary aim, provided he is suitable, and provided the prelate or the monastery does not directly intend, on account of this service, to provide him with a benefice or to receive him; for if it were so, it would be a gift consisting in service, and simony, when \[done\] principally and directly: de \[i.e. on\] *c. ordinationes*. Johannes says that if an agreement enters into the service, it is always simony: *c. cum eterne, eo. ti.* If no agreement enters in: if he serves principally without charge, but secondarily in order to obtain something spiritual, it is not simony, according to Hostiensis; and thus the laws that seem to contradict one another are reconciled. Hostiensis nevertheless also held that, if the bodily service is honorable and worthy, \[and\] is rendered as worthy service to a worthy person, one may lawfully serve even principally \[for this end\], provided no agreement enters in — as if someone serves a bishop by going to Rome \[for him\], by managing his affairs: this is the contrary sense of *c. fuit non nulli, 1. q. 1.* But in the case of unworthy service, or service for an unworthy person, it will be simony. Likewise say, regarding spiritual service, that if an agreement enters in it is simony, unless \[such service\] is annexed to that benefice.

## Wherefore note.

That not only do the aforesaid things induce simony in those who give or make agreements, but also in those who consent or act as intermediaries, etc., *per inquisitiones, de electione, 2. c. 2., de professis*; and also in those who make an agreement to give or do something for the ministers, and even in the ministers themselves, or in the household servants of those administering — just as we say of Gehazi, who was the servant of Elisha: *1. q. 1.*, in the *Summa*. Ricardus says, however, that even if a bishop has made an agreement or promise to give some benefice, and afterwards, forgetting such an agreement, simply offers that benefice to the church, he is not a simoniac; nor is the one who received such a benefice, if he was ignorant of this agreement, and therefore he is not bound to resign it — note this well.
