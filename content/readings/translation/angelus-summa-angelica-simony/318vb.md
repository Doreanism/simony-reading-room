But does \[the pope\] by that very fact seem to dispense, from the fact that he knows with certain knowledge and tolerates \[it\]? Johannes de Lignano replies, *eo. ti. c. 1*, saying that he does — so that he does not commit simony; which I believe to be true, when simony is committed together with the pope, as, for instance, when someone has obtained a benefice by means of money from the pope, the pope knowing this and consenting to it: because then the knowledge of the prince purges this fault, and so such a person would not incur the penalties of the law, whether the simony be of divine law or of positive law; nor would he be bound to resign, if the simony is of positive law. But concerning other things given to the pope, even though he himself knows that the \[curial\] lords and courtiers demand and tolerate them, he is not on that account seen to dispense, and therefore they are involved in the penalties. \[This view is\] supported by §. *Dispensatio*, §. 12.

## Simony, namely with respect to penalty.

It must be known that the first \[penalty\] is fourfold suspension. It is asked whether every simoniac, in receiving an order, is suspended by the law itself. As I gather from Innocentius and Panormitanus, on *c. tanta* and the chapter *per tuas* and 2., *eo. ti.*, \[the answer is\] yes — both with respect to himself and with respect to others; and not only with respect to the order so received, but \[suspended\] from all other orders also lawfully received, on account of being removed from the administration of the temporal goods of his benefices. The gloss supports this: *1. q. 1. repiunt*. And see the gloss on *Clem. cupientes, de penis*. Nor can he be dispensed with except by the pope: *1. q. 1. gratia*.

## Whether a simoniac in conferring an order is likewise suspended.

Goffredus replies, holding that he is, for a three-year period. But Innocentius, on *c. presbiterum, eo. ti.*, \[and\] Panormitanus, on the said *c. per tuas*, hold that he is suspended in perpetuity; and this seems truer, by reason of this text together with the gloss on *c. si quis a simoniacis*, and *1. q. 1.*, and §. *Verum*, *32. di.*

## Whether a simoniac in a benefice is suspended.

Panormitanus, in the said chapters cited above, \[says\] that he is. But if he is hidden, he is suspended with respect to himself, not with respect to others, but ought to be suspended: see the argument of *1. q. 3. c. 1 and 2*. Hence, if he renounces the benefice so acquired, he can use his order after \[making\] the agreed-upon penances, and lawfully hold other benefices; but while retaining that benefice in which the simony was committed, he cannot perform the penances, as in *c. inquisiti*
