## Whether someone worthy to be ordained

or beneficed, who gives by agreement, or who is exacted from by a doorkeeper so that the latter will let him in to the bishop to seek a benefice or order or the confirmation of an election, is a simoniac. Reply, according to Rodon., that he is not, if he does not give with a corrupt intention — namely, in order to obtain the advancement of the one to whom he gives — since if he gave with such an intention he would be a simoniac.

## Whether someone may be induced

to enter religion by money given to him. Reply that he may not; the chapter *dilectus*, \[and\] the same in the second \[chapter\] of the same title. But as for inducing someone to be converted to the faith, the gloss in the *Distinctio*, the chapter *dilectus*, says that money may be given. Hostiensis adds that the same may be said in the case of necessary sacred things, but not in voluntary ones. But I hold, with Pa., in the *Distinctio*, the chapter *dilectus*, that to give by agreement so that someone be converted, enter religion, or receive sacred things is not lawful, but that it is quite lawful without an agreement, so that he may be enticed to take up the aforesaid things.

## Concerning confraternities

constituted in such a way that no one is received into them unless he promises or swears that he will pay ten denarii annually. Reply, according to Ray., that if it is done for an honorable cause — for instance, to feed the poor, to ransom captives, and the like — there is no simony there; indeed, it is done lawfully, and whoever despises such confraternities is to be anathematized; \[see\] the forty-second *Distinctio*, *si quis despicit*, and the argument at the sixteenth question, seventh \[part\], *sane*. Rodon., however, says that if the statute is framed in negative form it is not good, because it excludes piety; but in affirmative form it is lawful — namely, that whoever is received shall give a certain amount — but he does not approve that an oath be taken, because of the danger of perjury.

## Whether to give money

or its equivalent for the doing of justice is simony. Ricar., in the fourth \[book\], the twenty-fifth *Distinctio*, third article, second question, holds that it is, speaking of ecclesiastical justice — as when one gives to a bishop or others for rendering judgment over spiritual matters; in this the gloss on the chapter *vendentes*, first question, third \[part\], agrees. Rodon. holds the same, and \[likewise\] over temporal matters if it is an ecclesiastical person who must render judgment by reason of ecclesiastical jurisdiction; it is otherwise if he is a secular or lay judge, although in that case he sins gravely on other grounds. Alexander de Ales, however, holds that even in the case of a secular judge to sell a just judgment is simony, since this is an act of virtue and so in a certain respect is something spiritual; but for advocacy or representation, even in a spiritual case, it is lawful to accept something without simony. But the gloss on the *Distinctio*, the chapter *vendentes*, and Ricar. in the place cited above, and perhaps more \[authorities\], hold the contrary — and \[hold\] that it is only a grave sin to accept payment for justice in temporal matters and from one who is a \[secular\] judge.

## Whether it is simoniacal

to conceal someone’s sin for money when it pertains to one’s office to reveal it. Reply that it is; the chapter *nemo*, \[*Extra,*\] the same title. Indeed, it is also \[simoniacal\] to conceal it out of regard for a relationship of consanguinity or affinity, as is said there. But Ricar., in the place cited above, qualifies this as true if such matters bear on a spiritual case; otherwise it is not \[simony\], but it is a grave sin.
