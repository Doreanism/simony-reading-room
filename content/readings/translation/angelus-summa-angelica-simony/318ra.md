\[Whether one who enters a monastery\] under a pact concerning the giving of spiritual things becomes a simoniac. The answer is: yes, by the chapter *In tantum* and the chapter *Veniens* of the same title. Yet Hostiensis, Gofredus, Raymundus, and Bernardus, and on the chapter *Cum simoniaca* of the same title, say that if a monastery were so poor that it could say to one wishing to enter, “If you wish to live with us, bring something with you, for although we would gladly receive you, our goods do not suffice” — \[then it is permitted\]. But hold rather, as a matter of conscience, the opinion of Thomas in his *Summa*, who says that for entry into a monastery it is not permitted to receive or demand anything as though for a price; but it is permitted to receive something toward the maintenance of the person to be received, if the monastery’s resources do not suffice for this. Likewise it is lawful for a bishop \[to consider\] the devotion that someone shows to a monastery by giving generous alms to it, so that he may be the more readily received, just as it is lawful — and conversely, that to compel someone, on account of spiritual benefits, to devotion toward a monastery, it is lawful for him to give or receive something by pact so that he may be received \[as in\] the chapter *Pro* \[Causa\] 1, question 2, \[per\] Rodonensis. And likewise that, when a monastery is poor and needy, it is not simoniacal to make a pact in such matters, provided it is not done for spiritual things to be conferred but for expenses to be made or recompensed. Hence it is permitted to express the names and the quantity in figures, and this pleases me as regards conscience, and as to the chapter *Qui videntur*, especially the said chapter *Qui simoniaca*: I say that they speak as regards the perilous \[external\] forum, on account of frauds, and that those \[authorities\] ground themselves on a presumption that ceases in the forum of the soul; or they speak so that nothing \[be given\] at entry, but they do not speak of advancing a sum when the monastery cannot otherwise \[manage\].

## Simony

Concerning the things that are forbidden: to give or to bring into a pact for anything spiritual, or annexed \[to the spiritual\], or dependent on it. And first.

Whether there is simony only when a pact concerning money intervenes. The answer is: no; rather I say that there is a threefold gift that induces simony in spiritual matters, if it is brought into a pact. The first is a gift of the hand, such as money, \[so called\] from “taking” inasmuch as one receives it, \[as in\] Causa 1, question 3, the whole \[causa\], for every spiritual thing. The second gift is of the tongue. The third is of service, as in Causa 1, question 1, *Sit nulli*. By some a fourth is added: the gift of blood, which is when someone confers a church or a prebend on another out of regard for blood-kinship or friendship. For according to the gloss on Causa 1, chapter 1, *Vt ecclesia*, distinction \[?\], if he confers it solely on account of consanguinity or friendship, and would not otherwise prefer that person, he is a simoniac. But Alexander and others, and Thomas in the *Secunda secundae* and in the \[Commentary on the\] Fourth \[Book of Sentences\] and in the *Quodlibets*, qualify this as true when from this he intends some good redounding to himself — for instance, that it may magnify or ennoble him, or that he may make for himself some recompense and the like. It is otherwise if he intends only the spiritual good of the one to whom it is given and not of another: for then, although it be a grave sin, nevertheless there is no simony in this fourth gift, because he gives or receives nothing, and \[this opinion\] pleases more.
