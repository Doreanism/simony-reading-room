If, however, such an office has been given to someone freely, and it is the custom of the place that he may appoint another in his stead, and may lease it out and sell it for the lifetime of the one granting it, this is not simony, according to Rodericus. But note that in 1. q. 2. c. 1. it is said that to close the doors of a church is not a sacred office, yet it is something annexed or united to one. And the gloss there says that it is then simony to buy some administration or office of the church when it is annexed to something spiritual, or when it has customarily been exercised by a cleric alone. And so hold that it is simony to sell an office, but not so to sell its revenues.

## What of those who give something so that they may \[preach\] in a parish or diocese

\[that is,\] who seek alms or beg for funds. The reply, according to Thomas in his \[Quodlibet\], is that priests and prelates who receive \[such payment\] are simoniacs, since the persuasion of a priest is spiritual and is believed because it comes from prelates or priests; thus it is simony with respect to the prelate or priest who grants permission, but with respect to the giver it is sacrilege, because there is a defrauding of alms against the intention of the givers, and both parties are bound to restitution. If such collectors of funds are wicked men, as the charlatans are, then as to restitution see “Restitution,” first part, under “shamefully,” §1.

## And whether it is permitted to give or receive money for prayer, preaching, the praise of God, or a procession

The reply, according to Ricardus as above and Astesanus, book 6, title \[on Simony\], is that it is not. For preaching and the like are called spiritual inasmuch as they proceed from grace and dispose toward grace, although they are performed through the ministry of men; and therefore to sell that which is spiritual in them is simony. And so one who preaches chiefly in order to obtain human praise or favor or gain is a simoniac, at least mentally; see 1. q. 2. “Non solum.” Note, however, that those who give alms to the poor or to religious in order to obtain the support of their prayers do not give because they intend to buy prayers, but in order to move them by gratuitous beneficence to pray for them freely and out of charity; and so they do not sin.

## What of those who decree in some church that a procession shall not take place at any funeral

unless one has bequeathed a certain sum of money to the church. The reply, according to Raymundus and Thomas, is that they are simoniacs; argument from \[the canon\] “Sicut” below, or rather from the first part of Thomas. Secondly, if one were to decree that for those bequeathing such a sum a certain honor or procession would be performed, the argument from the same source, the canon “Cum fine,” shows a diversity: because there the price is exacted, since it presupposes that for a lesser sum it would not be done; whereas in the former case there is a hope of gratuitous recompense, since it is not denied that it be done even for those who make bequests; argument from the law below, the canon “Sicut,” first part.

## And whether it is permitted to give money for celebrating an anniversary, and the like

The reply, according to Ricardus on book 4, distinction 20, is that one may not make a pact about money to be given for this purpose, but if by reason of a debt of devotion the church ought to celebrate \[it may do so\].
