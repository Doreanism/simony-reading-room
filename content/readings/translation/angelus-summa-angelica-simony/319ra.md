Restrict all of the foregoing to cases where the simony was committed knowingly. It is otherwise if he was ignorant of it — for instance, \[where it was committed\] by a father or by relatives — for then he is not suspended either with respect to others or with respect to himself, except as regards the order and the benefice thus obtained, but not as regards other orders received or benefices held or to be held, \[as in\] the chapter *de simoniace*, same title, where Innocentius held this.

## But I ask

who can dispense a simoniac as regards an order or a benefice. Answer: the gloss and Calderinus, on the chapter *Quia propter*, *de translatione* \[episcopi or rei\], and lord Antonius and Panormitanus, on the chapter *de simoniace*, hold through many \[arguments\] that, in the case of a simoniac as regards an order, only the pope \[can dispense\] when \[the simony was committed\] knowingly. But when the simony was committed in ignorance of it, then the bishop can dispense, \[as in\] *1. q. 1. presentius*; and a bishop can dispense with such a person, provided that, when he knew of this, he did not renounce but ministered in the order he had received, or proceeds to other \[orders\], according to Rodoricus. But in the case of a simoniac as regards a benefice committed knowingly, only the pope can dispense, according to Bernardus, Raymundus, and Gofredus — whether in that benefice or in any other whatsoever, according to the gloss and Johannes Andreae on the said chapter *simoniace*. But Hostiensis holds that the bishop can dispense only in a simple benefice. Panormitanus, however, on the chapter *Ex diligenti*, same title, \[holds\] that he can dispense even as regards a benefice with cure of souls, as he proves from *1. q. 1. c. 1.*; nor does the penultimate chapter *de electione* stand in the way, according to the same Panormitanus, for he says it speaks of a dispensation \[to retain\] that benefice in which the simony was committed, so that he may hold it — for this cannot be done except by the pope. Likewise, in a cathedral church one cannot, by a bishop’s dispensation, hold anything except a simple benefice, and with the exercise of the minor orders only. But in the case of one promoted in ignorance to a benefice, only the pope can \[allow him\] to hold it; whereas in other cases the bishop can, as is noted in the said chapter *patientia*, and what is noted in the chapter *futuram* and the chapter *nobis*, same title. But Tancredus, on the chapter *Sicut nobis*, same title, holds that the bishop can dispense as regards that benefice in which he was promoted, \[provided\] the bishop himself is not at fault; and this is acceptable.

## But might

the judgment be the same concerning intermediaries, as regards suspension and as regards dispensation? \[Answer:\] yes, by the rule “accessory things,” *de regulis iuris*, book 6.
