## But does an advocate likewise commit simony

if he pleads a case for money? Answer that he does not, because pleading and counsel assist a case only externally, whereas testimony belongs to the substance of the case; and therefore witnesses are compelled, but advocates are not, according to Alexander.

## Concerning renunciation and remission

beyond what you have above under *“Renuntiatio.”* I ask whether someone who renounces a lawsuit over a benefice still to be obtained is a simoniac. Answer with David in the chapter *“Ad audientiam,”* *de rescriptis*, that he is, if it is done by agreement to receive something temporal, or for the sake of a fixed pension to be received. The gloss on the said chapter *“Ad audientiam”* holds that it is indeed simony when one has obtained the benefice, but that where one has not yet obtained the benefice it is not simony. But you should hold that it is simony in this case as well, if something is given by agreement of the parties; it is otherwise if, by the office of the judge or through the mediation of good men, the renunciation is made purely on the one side, and on the other side something temporal is given. And this is gathered from the chapter *“Sane,”* *de renuntiatione*, and *3. q. 6.* *“Pac.”* \[and\] the chapter *“Cum prede.”* Likewise it is not simony if someone renounces such lawsuits because something spiritual is given to him—say, a benefice—according to Iohannes and Hostiensis, *de rescriptis*, the chapter *“Ad audientiam”*; but limit this to the case where there is an exchange, if the conditions concur which I stated above under *“Permutatio,”* §3.

## Whether someone who gives something temporal to a prelate so that he will not remove him from a prelacy or benefice is a simoniac

Answer with Rodon. that he is, if there is a necessity or advantage that he be removed; otherwise not, provided the intention is not corrupt. Likewise he is a simoniac who gives something spiritual in order to obtain a temporal due that is his own, according to Thomas in book 4, dist. 25—about which I am in doubt, because of what I said above under *“Simonia,”* 2, §17 and §18, and I rather believe that it is not simony, by the argument of the chapter 1, *de usu*.

## Whether someone who renounces a benefice so that it may be given to another, a certain person, is a simoniac

Answer that he is not, unless it is brought into an agreement, the chapter *“Dilectio,”* *de prebendis*. For to have a hope of this is not simony, the chapter *“Cum prede,”* *de pactis*, \[and\] *1. q. 1.* *“Ordinationes.”*
