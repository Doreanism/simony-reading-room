Third, the spiritual in the first sense \[i.e., by comparison with the Holy Spirit\] is taken with respect to its effects, namely the things that come to someone from some spiritual gift or office, such as to prophesy, to work miracles, to celebrate the divine offices, and the like; and such spiritual things cannot be sold, redeemed, or resigned for money, as in the chapter “Querela,” in the same title, and \[Decretum\] 1. q. 1. c. “Nullus episcopus,” and “Eligendi” 2, “Nisi forte”—see above in the section on “Dica” \[i.e., what is to be said\]. The spiritual in the second sense \[i.e., by comparison with the human spirit\], taken with respect to dispositions of the human spirit, is said to be twofold. First, by essence, as are all the branches of knowledge that are acquired through human investigation and teaching; and these can be sold neither by law nor in fact, as is clear from Proverbs 17: “What does it profit a fool to have riches, since he cannot buy wisdom?” Second, something is called spiritual by reason of its cause, such as the teaching of the aforesaid branches of knowledge, which can sometimes be sold both by law and in fact, but sometimes not without simony, as will appear below.

What is annexed to the spiritual taken in the first sense, that is, by relation to the Holy Spirit? I answer: that by means of which the spiritual is administered, such as the sacred vessels, the church, the cemetery, the dwelling of bishops, the priestly vestment insofar as it is sacred, and things of this kind.

What is it to depend upon or follow from the spiritual taken in the first sense? I answer that it is everything which depends upon or follows from the spiritual, such as to hold a church or an ecclesiastical dignity, a canonry or prebend, or any other ecclesiastical benefice, or the right of receiving tithes, first-fruits, or offerings, and whatever belongs to clerics by reason of their order, such as to ring the bells, to close the doors of the church, and the like; for all these are annexed to the clerical order and pertain to clerics alone. And this thing annexed to or dependent upon the spiritual cannot be sold, as in the chapter “Ad aures” and the chapter “Ad nostra,” in the same title; yet simony is committed in this case in a different way. And therefore the administrators of ecclesiastical property—such as the provost, the steward, and likewise the vicedominus, who is the manager of the bishop’s property; and likewise the defenders of these, such as the advocate of a corporation, the syndic of a church, who manages the church’s lawsuits; and the castaldus or major-domo, who has charge of the bishop’s external affairs, and the bishop’s judge; and likewise those subject to a rule, such as the archdeacon, archpriest, canon, monk, and anyone bound to an ecclesiastical office—if such persons ordain anyone for money, they are judged simoniacs, according to Augustinus and Archidiaconus \[Guido de Baysio\], \[Decretum\] 1. q. 3. c. “Salvator.” But Raymundus restricts this as true when such an administration has something of spirituality annexed to it.

What is the ordinary antecedent to the spiritual taken in the first sense? I answer: the right of patronage, which is the power of presenting someone to an ecclesiastical office, which of itself cannot be sold without simony, as in c. 1 \[Extra\] de iure patronatus; but it passes along with a temporal estate, as in the chapter “Ex litteris,” de iure patronatus.

What are the things annexed to the spiritual taken in the second sense, that is, by relation to the human spirit, or dependent upon it? I answer that they are schools, books, and the like; and such things can be sold and bought, unless the church of the place has assigned the school to a teacher.
