And the same Panormitanus likewise says, on the final chapter ne presbiteri, vi. su., that the revenues of a church may be leased to a layperson, provided he puts a cleric in his own place for collecting them, lest he presumptuously approach the altar where the oblations are: argument from chapters 1 and 2, ne clerici vel monachi.

## Whether one who gives money

for the administration of ecclesiastical property commits simony. Panormitanus replies, on the chapter Consulere in the same title, that he does; and the text is there. I limit this, following the gloss on the chapter Salvator, 1. q. 3., to the case when such an administration has some spiritual thing annexed to it; otherwise not — since one could grant temporal jurisdiction under an annual pension. And say the same — namely, that it is simoniacal — if it is given for the administration of the poor in hospitals: 1. q. 7. si quis oblationem, in the gloss; and in the Authentica de sanctissimis episcopis, § sed neque, according to Petrus de Pusio in his treatise on the canonical portion of the bishop, c. 4 — whether the hospitals were built by the authority of the bishop or not. It is otherwise if one gives something in order to be received merely to serve, without acquiring any right thereby. And understand in this way the single chapter, ut ecclesiastica beneficia, namely that a church — that is, a prelacy — cannot be granted under an annual rent: de simonia, c. non sat.; 11. q. 2. precipimus. Hence one who buys a provostship, a stewardship, a vice-domain, an advocacy, a castaldy, and the like, is a simoniac: 1. q. 1. si quis episcopus — subject, however, to the limitation stated above.

## Whether one who gives something

in order to serve as a vicar in a church is a simoniac. Panormitanus replies, on the chapter Ad nostram in the same title, that he is. See the good text together with the gloss on the chapter Licet, de presbiteris.

## Whether one who sells a villa and the right of patronage

which he has in it is a simoniac. Panormitanus replies, on the chapter Arellani, de simonia, that he is, since a conjunctive expression brings in the conjoined thing as a principal object and not as an accessory. It is otherwise if one says “I sell the villa with the right of patronage” — and understand Johannes Andreae in this way, on the chapter primum, de iure patronatus in the Sext. For my part, as regards conscience, I would have recourse to the intention, if he had one — namely, the deliberate purpose of selling the right of patronage specifically — and in that case he becomes a simoniac, which is evident when he sells the villa for more; otherwise not.

## Eleventh case

This is when a bishop receives a cleric to render him spiritual and temporal service, with a promise that he will pay him annually a certain salary until he provides him with a benefice. For although this is permitted, according to Hostiensis — provided he has not specifically bound himself to render temporal service — he does not commit simony by afterwards remitting the salary when the benefice is conferred, since the salary was conditional, and once the conferral of the benefice is made the condition expires: Institutes, de verborum obligationibus, § omnis. It is otherwise if the promise of the salary had been absolute and perpetual: argument from the chapter Veniens in the same title. This indeed Panormitanus lays down on the chapter Cum essent in the same title — namely that it is permitted when the prelate is not principally moved by the service, but because the man deserves it; otherwise, if the final cause is the service, it is not permitted: argument from the chapter Grave, de prebendis. It is otherwise if it is an impelling cause.
