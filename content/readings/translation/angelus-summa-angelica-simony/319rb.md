## What of a prelate

who promised a benefice to someone for money, and then, the matter being still intact, revokes the agreement? Answer: according to Ricardus, as above, that such a man has sinned gravely in making the pact, but because he did not proceed to carry the pact into effect, he does not incur the penalties of suspension and irregularity.

## Whether one ordained

by a bishop whom he heard \[committing\] simony may minister. Answer: that he can minister in the order he received, but he will not be able to receive other \[orders\] from the same bishop unless he sets aside his knowledge, \[as in\] the chapter Per tuas, second \[chapter\] of the same title. There Hostiensis notes some notable points. First, that from various authorities, prejudice \[of conscience\] in some matters is not a properly formed conscience. Second, that a conscience thus formed is to be set aside at the counsel of a prelate. Third, that if it cannot be set aside, it must by all means be followed. Fourth, that it is useful, since a scrupulous conscience is good when it is informed for the better, and yet for that reason it is a weakness of mind and excessive simplicity. Fifth, that in indifferent matters anyone can form his conscience as he pleases, which is well noted; and therefore such a man ought to minister and set aside his conscience.

## The second penalty

against simoniacs is deposition and infamy, and this is imposed if \[the offense\] has been established against him in court, \[as in\] 5. q. 3. sane, de accusationibus, in glossae.

## The third penalty

is papal excommunication. For Martinus, in the Council of Constance, in the extravagant that begins Bullae, speaks thus: with the approval of the sacred council, we declare that those ordained simoniacally are suspended by the law itself from the exercise of their orders. Moreover, elections, postulations, confirmations, and any simoniacal provisions whatsoever of churches, monasteries, dignities, personates, offices, and benefices of whatever kind, made henceforth, are null by the law itself, nor is any right acquired for anyone through them; nor do those promoted, confirmed, or provided make the fruits their own, but rather are bound to the restitution of those \[fruits\] as of things unjustly taken away — establishing further that the giver and the procurer, by that very act, incur the sentence of excommunication, even if he be resplendent with pontifical or cardinalatial dignity. In another extravagant, made in the second year of his pontificate, given at Mantua, beginning Damnabile, he confirms all excommunications, suspensions, deprivations, interdicts, and other censures pronounced by the supreme pontiffs against simoniacs — which he wills that every simoniac incur by that very act, whoever commits simony, occult or manifest, anywhere, of whatever rank he be, even if he be resplendent with episcopal or cardinalatial dignity or any other ecclesiastical or imperial dignity — reserving to himself the absolution from the said excommunications, suspensions, and penalties, except at the point of death; nor does he will that by virtue of any grant or any faculty of absolving whatsoever, granted or to be granted by the Apostolic See, they can be absolved, unless \[the absolvers\] specifically possess the authority of absolving in the matter of simony.
