I answer, according to Hostiensis and Bernardus on the last chapter of the same title below, that it does not; and they argue this from the chapter “Tua nos” at the end, and from the said final chapter of the same title, and the chapter “Ut nostrum” \[at the title\] “Ut ecclesiastica beneficia.” But I draw a distinction with Hostiensis, that with respect to venial sin and mortal sin it is committed by the will alone. I say that as regards venial sin: a man says Mass, hears confessions, administers the sacraments and the like principally with this intention, that he may receive money or some other temporal thing for it—he sins mortally and simoniacally as regards the intention with which he gives. But if he were to take it for the support of God’s minister, doing so with this as his principal purpose, or out of reverence for the sacred thing, or in keeping with a praiseworthy custom of the place, then it is allowed, as in \[Decretum\] 1. q. 2. “Si episcopum,” and the chapter “Quid pro pecunia,” \[Decretum\] 1. q. 1. “Ecclesiarum non,” and as in the chapter “Tua nos” in the same title; and therefore it is by the intention alone. But as to whether restitution is otherwise owed, see the final chapter of the same title; and indeed Dominicus restricts this as true in the case where simony is only a matter of positive law—indeed, he says that in such a case one does not even sin. But where simony is a matter of divine law, he says that such a person ought to be compelled to restore whatever he received, just as usurers must restore the money; and this is the view that pleases Panormitanus on the said final chapter. But this much I hold as regards sin: that without doubt he sins mortally if he has a deliberate will to give spiritual things for temporal things. And this is true—that unless the effect follows, namely that the spiritual thing is actually given for the temporal, he will never be a simoniac, as is noted in the chapter “Nemo deinceps” of the same title. As to restitution, see below, “Simony” 7, at the beginning. As to the other penalties imposed by law against simoniacs, I firmly hold that a pact is required, as I said in the definition above—”a preceding pact.”

But what if neither the one administering the spiritual thing nor the one receiving it gives or receives anything, nor makes any agreement about giving or receiving, but, without their knowledge, friends of the one receiving the spiritual thing give something to the one administering it, so as to induce him to administer it? I answer that, since most authorities hold that the one who gives by a pact in an advisory capacity, the one administering the spiritual thing, and the one receiving it are not simoniacs—because the latter knew nothing—yet those friends themselves, who may be called advisers, are simoniacs, \[Decretum\] 1. q. 1. “Statuimus,” and the chapter “Liga simoniacis.”

## Simony 2

In what cases does a pact of promise, of a deadline, of a condition, of a manner, of a form, of wine, of provisions, and of temporal things of this kind, given for a spiritual thing or for something annexed to or dependent upon the spiritual, not bring about simony? I answer: in 17 cases.

First, when a possession is given with the pact that a church be built upon it, as in the chapter “Ecclesia” \[?\] de restitutione spoliatorum. Hence permission may also be given to build the church itself under a certain pact, as in the chapter “Sane” \[?\] de censibus, and in the chapter “Ex insinuatione” in the same title, because such pacts are reckoned as spiritual—as also in the chapter “Significavit” de praebendis, where a prebend is given with the pact that the recipient celebrate daily in a particular church and that he not be permitted to relinquish it; and this is allowed, because in handing over one’s own property one may in some way attach whatever pact one pleases, as in the chapter “Verum” de conditionibus appositis. Whether a layman, in giving a temporal thing to a church, may attach a spiritual condition—for instance, that an anniversary be kept, or that so many Masses be said—see below, “Simony” 3, § 2.
