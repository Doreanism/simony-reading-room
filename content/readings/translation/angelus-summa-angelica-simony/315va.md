## Eighth case

This is when there is a custom that something be exacted for spiritual things. But concerning this, see what is said above at Consuetudo, § 1. And note that this holds where something is owed by laypersons to clerics. But where the custom is that something be exacted from clerics, then say, following Panormitanus on the chapter Jacobus \[Extra, de simonia\], that for the private advantage of clerics it is not valid; but it is indeed valid in favor of divine worship, provided that the exaction did not precede the bestowal of the sacred things — understand it of the time before the rendering of the sacraments. And for this reason Innocentius and Johannes de Lignano say, on the chapter Ad apostolicam \[1. d. c. ad apostolicam\], that a custom by which something is given before the reception of the sacraments is a corrupt practice, and it is not valid however long-standing it may be, even when no exaction preceded. Why, then, is it valid for laypersons and not for clerics? The reason for the difference is that laypersons seem to have bound themselves under the name of a certain oblation. This is supported by what is noted at the chapter Novum genus, de decimis.

## Ninth case

This is when someone, for the sake of an exchange, gives something in compensation for a greater benefice. Concerning this, see what is said above at Permutatio, § 6. And note that a transaction of exchange between priests over benefices does not induce simony, as said above at Permutatio, § 4. Indeed, as Cardinalis says on the chapter Unica, de rerum permutatione \[Clementines\], in a resignation it can be expressly stipulated that one resigns for the sake of an exchange without simony, since this is permitted by law: de electione, c. significasti; and de rerum permutatione, c. universorum, and the single chapter de rerum permutatione in the Sext; and the said Clementine chapter Unica.

## Tenth case

This is when sacred vessels are sold. Although they are annexed to spiritual things, nevertheless they can be sold without simony — provided no regard is had to the form of consecration — according to Ricardus in Quodlibet 2, q. 12, yet in such a way that they are sold to another church in the same form. But it is not permitted to sell them to a secular person, unless they have been reduced again to their original mass of metal. And I say the same about sacred vestments, that they can be sold to another church.

## But surely

Is it permitted to exact something for chrism, with regard to the liquid? Panormitanus replies, on the chapter Ea que, de avaricia in the same title, that this is not possible without simony, since chrism, insofar as it is a sacrament, is not only spiritual, but even insofar as it is a liquid it is spiritual: 1. q. 1. placuit, or the first chapter. Likewise neither blessed bread nor blessed candles.

## Eleventh case

This is when there is sold something dependent on, or consequent to, that which depends upon a spiritual thing, according to Ricardus as above — such as the levying of tithes and the fruits of benefices, the bread of oblation or wax, grain, and the like which are offered in the church. For insofar as a thing is precisely spiritual it cannot be sold; but the fruits indeed can. And therefore Panormitanus says, on the chapter primum, ne presbiteri, vi. su., that a prelate may well sell the revenues of jurisdiction as fruits, according to the gloss there. Yet, according to Innocentius, they ought not to be sold to one who exercises that jurisdiction, and rightly so, since on account of the connection he would seem to be selling the jurisdiction itself: 1. q. 3. c. si quis obiecerit.
