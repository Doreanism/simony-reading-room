Yet we can say that even then he is a simoniac, when a prelate \[gives\] another an ecclesiastical benefice to be given to someone with a pact or intention that the latter should provide for his own blood-relatives.

But does just any gift of the hand induce simony in spiritual matters? The answer is: yes, if a pact has gone before. But if it has not gone before, then, if it be so small that it ought not to sway the mind of the one promoting — the quality \[of the thing\] and the quality of the persons being considered — then it does not constitute simony, \[as in\] the chapter *Etsi quaestiones* of the same title. For it is very inhuman to be unwilling to receive anything from anyone; but it is most base to receive everything from everyone, \[Digest,\] *De officio proconsulis*, law *Solet*, final paragraph. And this is true as regards the Church; but as regards God, whatever is given or received with a corrupt intention — I say that as to the spiritual thing the mind is simoniacal; yet it is purged by penance alone without resignation of the benefice, and so understand the chapter *Nemo* of the same title, according to Gofredus. Note, however, that when something is given without a pact, not as a debt but as customarily given, it is not simony, according to Rodonensis.

What if what is given is from the goods of the Church? The answer is: it will nonetheless be simony, namely according to Thomas.

What if a gift be given not of the hand but of the tongue, for a spiritual good? The answer is: it is simony. Note, however, that “of the tongue” \[a *manus a lingua*\] is understood otherwise: praise itself, pertaining to human favor and falling under a price. Other prayers are those by which human favor is sought or hatred avoided. And therefore if someone aims principally at this, he commits simony. And he must be aiming principally \[at it\] when it is given or received for the admitting of an unworthy person. But concerning a worthy person Gofredus and Hostiensis distinguish: either someone advances himself by his own prayers — and so, if he seeks a certain prelacy through election, then it is simony, \[as in\] Causa 1, question 6, *Sicut id quod*. But if it is not given through election, if it be a simple benefice, then it is permitted, if he is in need. If it is not simple but one carrying a dignity or the cure of souls, then it is not permitted, by the argument of Causa 1, question 1, *Ordinationes*, and Causa 8, question 1, *In scripturis* and *Et simonia*. But according to Raymundus and Hostiensis, Thomas in the *Secunda secundae*, and the *Directorium*, book 3, by the very fact that he seeks it he is unworthy. But Rodonensis believes that it is permitted even in a dignity and a cure, \[citing\] the chapter *Tua nos* of the same title. And yet I believe it true if he is in need — for instance, that he has nothing elsewhere from which to live; otherwise not, \[as in\] the chapter *Ad aures* of the same title — provided only that he is worthy and intends to be of profit and not to preside. But if he is advanced by the prayers of others: if for an unworthy person, then according to all it is simony, \[as in\] Causa 1, question 1, *De ordinationibus*. But if for a worthy person, then, if they are spiritual \[prayers\], it is not simony, \[as in\] Causa 1, question 1, *Latorem*.
