Concerning entry into religion and promotion to ecclesiastical dignity, many things are simoniacal according to the doctors, which they understand either presumptively in the contentious forum, or in conscience if the regard is not to the judged matter but to the entry itself or to elevation to clerical status. So first, if no pact is made but there is a tacit understanding that something must be given to him, according to Hostiensis *Extra eod. Sicut* and *Pro certo*. Second, if a pact is made about observing custom, *Extra eod. Veniens*. Third, if he receives him gratis and afterwards compels him to observe the custom, *Extra eod. Iacobus*. Fourth, if someone serves the religious on account of a promise made to him concerning his reception, according to \[???\] *quapio*. But it is clear that all the aforesaid canons speak of the reception itself or of the promise, not of sustenance. If, however, such a person had simply said this without intending by temporal means to obtain entry into religion, but rather expressed his intention, namely of transferring along with his goods, and the receivers did not principally regard the temporal goods, the Archdeacon does not clearly see how it would be simony in the forum of penance. Second, when monks give something to the abbot or bishop for priorates to be obtained or not to be lost when held, according to \[???\]: if in those there is something spiritual, even though he speaks of appraisal or only of the first and principal element, then it is simony; indeed, it is simony even if only temporal jurisdiction is involved, as is clear from what was said above in the fourth and fifth parts.

### Sixth

If he admits someone with the condition that reception be after two years, on condition that in the meantime he learns such an art or remains in the schools, according to \[???\] and the *Vide.*, it is not simony. If someone said, “We will clothe you if in the meantime you make yourself fit,” the Archdeacon thought this would be simony if he had an intention directed to the profit of that art. But this is not correct, because such profit can only be intended mediately, through the fitness of the person, which can indeed be intended; otherwise good religious would daily commit simony in receiving any whom they hope will be useful to the religion.

### Seventh

If someone, upon entry into a confraternity, promises or swears by statute to pay something annually, it is not simony, because that fraternity is not a religion but a congregation for doing good works. Indeed, it is praiseworthy if such an obligation is imposed for alms to be given or for expenses incurred when they meet to deliberate about the salvation of souls.
