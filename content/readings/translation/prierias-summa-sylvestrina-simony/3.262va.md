And therefore in the case set forth a distinction must be made. If one receives such distributions as the principally intended end of his work, he commits simony and so sins mortally. But if his principal end is to serve in such an act, and he has regard to such distributions only secondarily — not as an end, but as something necessary for his sustenance — it is clear that he is not selling a spiritual act and so does not commit simony nor sin. For in this way the receiving of the distributions will not be the cause of his going to church, but properly the determining factor of why he goes now and not on another occasion. From which, together with what was said in the preceding paragraph and section, and the section following, I infer that one who is not bound to a spiritual act on the side of the principle by reason of salary or office, being in need either for necessary sustenance or for honest well-being, may lawfully intend it in such an act — not as a price, but as a charitable sustenance — with an intention without which it would be simony. Yet if such intention were brought into a pact, tacit or express, it would be simony as regards the Church, which does not consider intention.

### Third

For the celebration of Mass or other divine offices, or processions, and the like, it is never lawful to receive or give anything as a price. But if a priest is bound to celebrate Mass, or to perform funerals or such offices, then according to Raymundus to demand anything is simony (*f. q. f. Vendentes*). But if he is not so bound and he lacks means, he may receive (*j. q. f. Duces*); for he can rent out his labor either by the day or by the year (*X ne pla. vi. su. c. fi.*). But if he has the means and is not bound to these things, he ought either to do them gratis or to desist; otherwise he seems to act out of avarice. And although, when he is not bound, this perhaps is not simony — because he need not serve at his own expense (*xv. q. ii. Charitatem; X eod. Cum sit*) — yet it is base gain when done from avarice; or if he takes something for his expenses, even if he is wealthy and does not act from avarice, it at least has the appearance of evil, as has been said. § But note nevertheless, that in such matters no one can lawfully rent out his labor properly speaking, according to Saint Thomas and the gloss on the said *c. ult.* And this is clear because in spiritual matters, in which all pact and agreement should cease (*X de pac. c. fi.*), there is no place for letting or hiring. Hence, according to the gloss to that text, just as a spiritual office cannot be sold, neither can the labor in that office be properly sold or rented; nor do the cited authorities prove this, nor does Raymundus himself intend this. Rather, he only means that one may accept such things offered for spiritual acts, and with the intention of not proceeding unless they give such things as would sustain him, provided that nothing is agreed upon or demanded before the spiritual acts are performed.
