### Third

The gift “from the tongue,” according to St. Thomas in II-II and in IV, is twofold: namely, that which consists in an act of the tongue, such as praise pertaining to human favor, which falls under a price; and that which takes its origin from an act of the tongue, as when one expects favor from the fact that he complies with someone’s prayers. Hence the gift “from the tongue” is also called the entreaties by which human favor is acquired, or even by which danger is avoided, as when they are armed prayers—since to be free of an evil is itself a kind of good (*Ethics* V). And thus this gift, according to his mind, consists wholly, whether mediately or immediately, in human favor. To give “on account of entreaties” here is to give on account of the favor hoped for from the one entreating. This gift undoubtedly induces simony, as he says, if a spiritual thing is given on account of it, because it is ordered to a temporal utility measurable by money; or if the giver principally intends this, it is simony—which, he says, is what one does who grants entreaties for an unworthy person. Or—if we go with him—the doctors distinguish thus concerning entreaties. If one offers entreaties for himself to obtain the sacraments and other spiritual things apart from benefices, it is not found to be forbidden, provided he is worthy, except in the case of a religious who has no will of his own, and especially he ought not to be promoted to orders except by the will of the prelate (*de tempore ordi., Ad aures*). But if he offers entreaties for himself to obtain a benefice or church or dignity with the cure of souls, it is simony, according to Huguccio, Raymundus, Durandus, and St. Thomas, even if he were otherwise worthy, because by thrusting himself forward he is presumptuous and ambitious, and consequently unworthy. According to St. Thomas in the *Quodlibet*, without the grace of God—which no one knows he has—no one can worthily exercise the cure of souls, since he sins mortally by unworthily doing those things which pertain to the cure: see above, *Episcopus* § iv. And it is much more simony if he is otherwise unworthy, for example because he is illiterate or a notorious fornicator. But if he offers entreaties for himself to obtain a simple benefice, it is not simony, nor does he sin, if he is in need and not unworthy on account of some impediment, according to Raymundus, St. Thomas, and others (*de prebe. c. ij.*); but if he is not in need, he sins, according to Raymundus (*j. q. ij. clericos*), and much more so if he were unworthy—indeed, then it appears to be simony. Some, however, such as the gloss, Raymundus, and Vide., say that this opinion is harsh, because one promoted to sacred orders—since such promotion requires an ecclesiastical title—may, if he is worthy, without sin seek an ecclesiastical benefice even if he abounds in temporal goods, since no one is compelled to serve at his own expense (after the example of Isaiah saying in chapter 6, “Behold, here am I; send me”); for which the toleration of the pope also stands, since otherwise nearly the whole world would be in a state of damnation. And the *Summa Rosella* follows this opinion, ascribing it to Innocent (in *c. Tua nos, eo. ti.*). But this opinion, as to the words “even if he abounds in temporal goods,” is overturned by the laws (*de rescrip. Ad aures; j. q. ij. clericos and c. pastor; iij. q. j. illud autem*).
