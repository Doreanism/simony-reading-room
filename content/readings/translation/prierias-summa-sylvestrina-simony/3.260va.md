### Fifth rule, set forth by

Saint Thomas in Quodlibet viii. q. v. ubi supra, namely: that if an act is spiritual on its principal part — that is, belongs to someone on account of something spiritual in him, as it belongs to a deacon to read the Gospel — it is simony if anyone intends to sell his act; but not so if it is spiritual only on the part of its end, as in teaching the liberal arts, whose truth is something spiritual; though it is simony if the end which is spiritual, that is, the truth, is sold. From which it is also plain that one who hires out his labors sells the act.

## Fourth question: whether simony can fall upon anyone, even the pope and the Supreme Pontiff dealing with the pope

And I say that as to the pope, a certain opinion says no; but according to Saint Thomas and other theologians and canonists commonly, if the pope receives money for any spiritual thing or for what is annexed to it, he commits simony — even if that money is from the revenues of some church, because, although the things of the Church are his to dispense as principal dispensator, this is not as their owner. But Saint Thomas speaks of those things which are simoniacal by the definition of simony or by their very nature; hence this opinion, namely that simony can fall upon the pope, which is the common one, Antonius de Butrio (following Thomas) rightly limits to be true with respect to things that are prohibited because they are simoniacal, namely by their nature, but not with respect to things that are simoniacal because they are prohibited, namely by positive law, since such a constitution does not bind those who promulgate it. Petrus de Palude also limits the proposition to be true as to sin, not as to the penalties imposed by law on simoniacs, since the pope always escapes these.

### As regards those dealing with the pope, I say that according to

some, they are excused from the penalty of simony. Hence Peter says that one who buys a benefice or order simoniacally from the pope does not incur the penalties of law, because, just as in penalties already incurred, so also that they should not be incurred, the pope can dispense — just as he could grant someone a privilege that, by striking a cleric, he should not be excommunicated. But when the pope confers a benefice through money, this is as if he dispenses that it be lawful to retain it. Hence one who has obtained a benefice in the Curia through simony, if he is conscientiously aware that this proceeded from the pope’s knowledge in conferring it, by repenting of the offense is not bound to resign but may retain it. So far from him. Indeed, according to some, they are excused from the precept against simony in the case of simoniacal acts that are such because prohibited, since Johannes de Lignano (extra eod. c. f.) says that neither the pope nor others present in the Curia commit simony if the pope knows and of certain knowledge tolerates it — which is understood of acts simoniacal because prohibited. And this Archidiaconus follows, and it is to be held, although Panormitanus in d. capit. f. feels otherwise; and the Summa Rosella offers a concordance which does not capture the mind of those disagreeing.
