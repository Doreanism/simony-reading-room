Hence, as is plain in *extra eo. and si quaestiones*, if it is in doubt whether the gift was made freely or with a corrupt intention, three things chiefly are to be attended to: namely, the quality of giver and recipient, that is, whether a rich man gives to a poor one or the reverse; the quantity of the gift, namely whether the thing be of great or small value; and the time of the parties and of the donation, namely whether it is conferred under pressing necessity or otherwise. And this is so because on these things presumption is founded — which nonetheless has no place in conscience.

## Sixth

Sixth, it is asked in how many ways simony is committed.

### And I say

And I say that on the side of the thing given it is committed in three ways, corresponding to the threefold “gift,” namely “from the hand,” “from service,” and “from the tongue,” on which see above *gabella §. f.* and below §. ix. f.

### On the side of the parties committing

On the side of those committing it, it is likewise committed in four ways. Sometimes only on the side of the one conferring the spiritual thing — as when he receives a temporal thing without the knowledge of the one on whom he confers the spiritual; sometimes, conversely, only on the side of the one receiving the spiritual — as when he gives something not to the giver but to a friend or counselor of his; sometimes on both sides, namely when the recipient gives something to the giver; sometimes on neither side — as if a friend of the recipient gives something to a friend of the giver without their knowing, as in *q. f. statuimus* and the chapter *si quis a simoniacis*.

### On the side of the mode

On the side of the mode of committing it, it is likewise committed in four ways: by thought, by speech, by deed, and by omission, on which see below §. iii. part v. and ix.

## Seventh

Seventh, it is asked whether it is simony to give a temporal thing — or to receive one — for a spiritual reality essentially, such as eternal life or the remission of sins; and I say that it is not, following Durandus and all others, in accordance with Daniel 4: “Redeem thy sins with alms.” The reason is assigned by St. Thomas: this is “buying” taken broadly, for merit, not in the strict sense — both because \[our sufferings\] are not condign etc., Romans 8, and because merit consists principally not in the external gift, act, or passion, but in the interior affection.

## Eighth

Eighth, it is asked whether it is simony to give something for a spiritual thing “by consequence,” that is, for some sacred or sacramental object; and I say many things.

### First

First, as St. Thomas proves in IV, dist. xxv, q. iii, art. ii, q. f.: since sacred things contain grace, they cannot lawfully be sold or bought — that is, by law, without the vice of simony. Or, as he says at xxiii. q. c. ar. ii., to take money for their grace is the crime of simony, which no custom can excuse. Yet, as is added there, to receive something for the sustenance of those who minister the sacraments of Christ, in accordance with the ordinance of the Church and approved custom, is not simony nor sin, provided it is not taken as the price of a wage but as the stipend of necessity.
