## The seventeenth question

In the seventeenth place it is asked whether it is in any way permitted to make a pact concerning temporal things in exchange for spiritual things, or the converse; and I say that it is, in two cases that come to mind.

First, when a burden is annexed to a temporal thing that is being conveyed, because in the conveyance of his own property anyone may attach whatever pact pleases him (*de condi. appo. verum*). Hence land may be given with a pact that a church be built on it, with a certain rent reserved (*de resti. spo. olim*). Hence one may also grant the license to build the very church under a certain pact, as in c. *quanto* (*de censi.*) and in c. *ex insinuatione* (same title), and likewise in c. *significatum* (*de prebendis*), where a prebend is given with the pact of celebrating daily in such-and-such a church. In the foregoing cases, however, there is no pact properly so called, as *Summa Angelica* supposed, because Thomas says the opposite, as above § 9, part 9, and more fully through Panormitanus in c. *olim*, *de resti. spo.*; rather, it is a donation under a mode or cause, and the acceptance of the donation.

### Secondly

Secondly, when something is exacted by the permission of the law, because to express what the law permits does not induce simony, as Panormitanus notes on the said c. *olim*. And thus it is permitted, in the case of one consecrating or visiting, even though such an act is spiritual, on the part of princes to exact procuration (*extra eo. cum sit romana*; *de censi. cum apostolus*; *de prescri. cum ex officii*; 1 q. 3 *relatum*). This is not to be understood as if human law could dispense in simony, but rather in the presumption of simony — just as where someone does a lawful act, for instance, preaches with a principal intention directed to temporal things such as his sustenance, if a pact is made simony is presumed, which would not be presumed if the law conceded it. From this, then, I say that it is permitted to exact when the law permits. *Summa Angelica* rejects the statement of Thomas in the title (acknowledged through the supplement of Romanus) that it is simony to give a spiritual thing to obtain a temporal debt that is one’s own, though here he is mistaken, because although the law punishes the exaction of one’s own debt, it does not therefore permit its purchase by a spiritual thing given. Indeed, if the law conceded this, the concession would be contrary to divine law and would not be valid, because human law can never grant that a temporal thing be bought by a spiritual one, or the converse; but it can dispense concerning the presumption, as has been said, and concerning the penalties, and concerning certain things — perhaps only by human law — for simoniacs.

## The eighteenth question

In the eighteenth place it is asked what is the law concerning mediators in simony; and I say that, if neither the one administering nor the one receiving a spiritual thing receives or gives or makes a pact for anything, but, without their knowledge, a friend of the receiver of the spiritual thing gives something by a pact arranged with a counselor of the administrator, although those two are not simoniacs, since they know nothing, nevertheless these others are simoniacs (1 q. 1 *statuimus*; and the chapter *si quis a simoniacis*) — concerning which, see as above § 13, part 1.
