And one must also say this in conscience, by reason of the following argument: although the active reception itself into religion or into a canonicate is a spiritual act on the side of its principle, nevertheless the right to sustenance is not something spiritual nor annexed to the spiritual either antecedently or consequently, as is clear from what has been said. Hence for that it is permitted to contract, even with respect to the church, if from the circumstances it appears that the person is moved by this and not by anything else — namely the entry itself. If, however, the monastery were wealthy, then according to all the aforesaid authors it would be simony — but understand this presumptively, just as some nuns have always done and still do, who, swollen with their riches, the wealthier they are the larger the dowries they demand, judging the religious state as though it were the state of a worldly bridegroom. As to the canons cited to the contrary, I say in general that they speak with respect to the presumptions considered in the contentious forum, which do not apply in conscience, as has been said. §. *Lex* — But more particularly, as to the canon *Quoniam* under this title, where it is said that simony is committed under the pretext of poverty: I say that according to Raymond it speaks of when this was said fraudulently, or according to Hostiensis when spiritual things are not offered freely but a pact is made concerning temporal things for the spiritual things — that is, for a spiritual right. As to the canon *Periculoso* under *De statu regularium* in the *Sext*, where, according to the Archdeacon, another opinion than the one he himself follows is established when it says, “we more strictly prohibit that henceforth in monasteries of non-mendicant women’s orders any sisters be received except as many as can be sustained from the goods or revenues of those monasteries without privation; if it be done otherwise, we declare it void” — to this canon, I say, my position is supported: for the reason of this prohibition, as is plainly stated in the text and gloss, is so that they may not, on account of want, be forced to leave the enclosure. Thus the intent of the law is that they should have the means to live. And in truth, if the intention is pure, it is praiseworthy to demand sustenance from those wishing to enter, when the monastery cannot conveniently support a larger number, because the worship of God is thereby extended and the number of his servants is increased.

### Third

According to Saint Thomas, it is also lawful that, on account of the devotion which someone shows toward a monastery by making generous alms, he be more easily received into the monastery; just as it is lawful, conversely, to provoke someone to devotion toward the monastery by temporal benefits, so that he may from this be inclined to enter the monastery — although it is not lawful by pact to give or receive anything for entry into the monastery, as is held in the canon *Quam pio*. So Thomas, who is speaking of an entry by reason of a spiritual right, as is clear from what precedes and from the canon he cites. It would still be permitted, however, to accept things spontaneously offered without a corrupt intention, *q. ii. Sicut episcopum*.
