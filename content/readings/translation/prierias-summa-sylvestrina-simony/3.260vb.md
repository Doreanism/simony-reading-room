*Angelica* ascribes to Tancred and Bernard the view that it is not (*extra eod. v. l.*); but they are speaking with respect to the judicial forum, nor are they speaking of the will to buy, but of that of which more below. As regards God and the conscience of the Fathers, however, simony is committed by the will alone, although the Church does not punish this, as at *c. tua nos eo. ti.* Whether restitution is then necessarily to be made, see above § v. But if we speak of the will, that is, the intention, of receiving or giving something by pact for a spiritual thing, a distinction must be made. For if the intention is secondary — that is, one without which the action would still be done, though not absolutely — it is not simony, even before God; and the reason is that in that case the temporal is not given for the spiritual or vice versa, since with the temporal removed one would nonetheless give the spiritual and vice versa. But if the intention is principal — that is, one without which the action would not be done — \[then\] indistinctly, the *Summa Rosella*, following some other summists, and the *Summa Angelica*, hold that it is simony. Yet this is true only where one would intend to give or receive the temporal directly for the spiritual itself, as its price or wage, as Gehazi did; or also for one’s labor which cannot be hired out (on which see above § ix. parti. ii. and iii.). It is otherwise if for one’s sustenance, as is plain first from the third rule above § ii., secondly from the definition of simony, thirdly because Raymond in *tit. de simo. § quer. f.* holds the same, and similarly his gloss, as will also appear from what is to be said at § viii. and § ix. Hence Archidiaconus says that when one gives the temporal for the spiritual, if one mentions an appraisal or exchange or a principally moving cause — so that, he says, that temporal is for the price of the spiritual thing or is principally given on account of it — the spiritual itself is simony. It is otherwise if one mentions a final or auxiliary cause, as, he says, if he does this for reverence of sacred things, or to preserve a pious custom, or for the sustenance of ministers, and not as a price — provided that all pact and convention cease (*j. q. ii. quapropter*), and that exaction be absent from one unable and unwilling to give, especially before the conferral of the spiritual things. So far from him. He also subjoins that the one who gives a spiritual thing for money, which he would not otherwise give, is a simoniac; but he speaks of him who is bound to administer spirituals, who cannot exact before the giving but only afterward. But he who is not so bound and has no other means whence to be decently sustained, according to Raymond, may hire out his labors in spiritual acts, in the sense that will be said below at parts ii. and iii., § ix. And this is even observed in practice, since religious continually go to funerals and sermons with the hope of sustenance, without which they would not go. Hence the *Summa Rosella* also virtually holds this at the word “simony” iiii. § iii., \[contrary to\] his first statement, as below § ix. parti. iiii. And the *Summa Angelica* follows this against the *Summa Rosella*, and it is undoubtedly true in the case of those who are not bound by office to such acts, and have no other means of sustenance, and have dedicated themselves to God by their labors, not seeking riches but sustenance. In other cases, how the truth stands will be plain from what is to be said. But as regards the Church and human judgment, no one contracts simony by any will whatever, unless something is added.
