## Thirteenth question: whether it is permitted to give or accept money in any way for benefices or ecclesiastical dignitiest money in any manner for ecclesiastical benefices or dignities, and I say many things

### First

If, after the election has already been made by the majority, something is given to someone who is contradicting the election so that, against the will of the one elected, he may desist — even though the election is disturbed — this is simony, and \[the elect\] is bound to resign (X eo. *Mattheus*), even if the contradiction was unjust, according to Innocent (*de elect. significasti*), and all the more so if it was not yet made (X eo. *nobis*). Likewise, if before Peter’s election money is given for it by another without his knowledge — indeed according to Vincentius and Hostiensis and the gloss and Raymond, even when he is contradicting it — it is simoniacal, even if he was otherwise to be elected, according to Directorius (i q. i *placuit* and X eo. *simoniace* and c. *de regularibus* and c. *nobis*) — unless this was done fraudulently to the disadvantage of the elect, so that no advantage may be gained from the fraud (d. c. *nobis*). Peter himself, however, is not properly a simoniac, although he is bound by the aforementioned laws to renounce. It is otherwise if it was given after the election to the one confirming, for confirmation or benediction or consecration, the elect contradicting (X eo. *sicut tuis*) or even being unaware, according to Archidiaconus following Directorius, because it does him no harm unless he has departed from his prohibition, or unless afterward he has consented by paying the money or repaying what was paid (d. c. *sicut*), or unless he were a kinsman (argument from the same place). And likewise it is simony if it is given for the confirmation of the election with the consent of the elect, according to Petrus de Palude — unless harassment redeems \[him\] (which has place in a right already acquired). If therefore something is given to someone lest he disturb the election, a distinction is to be made: for if it is given lest he prevent a juridical election being made — in which he has no right — it is not simony but the redemption of harassment. If, however, it is given lest he prevent this person or that one from being elected, then it is simony, because it is given for a spiritual thing. If, however, the man was already elected, it is not simony but redemption, because the right had already been acquired by him (X eo. *dilecto*). A prince and others who accept money so as to consent to an election or promotion to an ecclesiastical dignity are simoniacs, according to Panormitanus on c. *de hoc* of the same title, and they are bound to make restitution to the church whose dignity was at issue, even though it was not from the goods of the church, as is plain in d. c. *de hoc*.
