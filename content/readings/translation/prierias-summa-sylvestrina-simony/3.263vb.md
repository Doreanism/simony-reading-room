### First

Concerning burial as regards the ground itself, see above and below in part 4. As to the rest, Raymond, Hofredus, and Hostiensis say that nothing should be exacted for such things, but pious customs ought to be observed (X. *De simonia*, c. *Ad nostram*, and c. *Cum in ecclesie*) — namely, the gracious bestowal of spiritual things being first made; otherwise it would be simony (cited c. *Cum in ecclesie*).

### Second

If a church has several cemeteries and a fee is fixed for what sum of money one shall be placed in such-and-such a cemetery, this, according to Hostiensis, is simony (X. *De simonia*, c. *Audivimus*). The same applies if for money a special cross is carried at a funeral (the same c. *Audivimus*). The same view is held by Durandus.

### Third

If clerics have been accustomed to receive beds, cloths, horses, and the like from the dead, and before the body is carried they make a pact for the redemption of such things — according to Hostiensis, since these are temporal goods, this is lawful. But if they were unwilling to bury the body unless security were given them for the redemption of such things, that is simony; whence in such cases the body should first be buried, and afterward action be taken against the heir (X. *De simonia*, c. *Ad apostolicam*).

### Fourth

Concerning the processions which certain collegiate churches make at the funerals of the dead when some money or other temporal thing has been given or promised — which otherwise they would not have made — Archidiaconus, following Spilianus and Raymond, says that if they are bound to such processions by office, then to exact anything is simony (C.1 q.2 c. *Vendentes*). If, however, they are not so bound and a stipend is lacking, then it is not simony, provided they do not intend to receive this as the price of an office, but as recompense of expense. And the *Summa Rosella* agrees, above.

### Fifth

If it be established that a procession be made for money — if indeed the statute is framed in affirmative terms, say, “whoever bequeaths to the college 10 \[units\] shall have a procession, and otherwise they are not bound to it” — it is not simony. But if it be framed in negative terms — as, “no procession shall be made for anyone unless he has bequeathed such-and-such amount” — it is simony, because it removes a duty of piety, namely, that a procession should not be made gratis or for a lesser price (X. *De iureiurando*, c. *Sicut*, first and second). Thomas and Vincent concur. The reason for the difference is that in the first case there is no exaction, since it is not denied to non-legators (argument from *De iureiurando*, c. *Sicut*, etc.). In the second case there is a kind of exaction, since it is presupposed that for less it shall not be done. As regards God, however, there can be simony in the first case on the part of those enacting it but not in the second — and in the second but not in the first — according to intention.

### Sixth

If the chapter of a mother church should enact that no one of its parish shall summon any religious to a funeral unless he summons the whole chapter, which is then to receive some temporal thing — though it is not bound to be summoned — the constitution is simoniacal, because it is negative and against ecclesiastical liberty, as you have from what was said above s.v. *Immunitas*, v. § 2 and in the *Aurea Rosa*, case 58, at length.
