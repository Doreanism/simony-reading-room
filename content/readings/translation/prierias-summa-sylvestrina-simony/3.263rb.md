Qualify this, according to the *Glossa*, in the case where one is not bound to reveal something by reason of office or action or judge. Add, third, that to give money for obtaining a temporal jurisdiction is not simony, according to Panormitanus on c. 1, *Ne praelati vices suas*, and the *Glossa* on c. *Quoniam, Ne praelati vices suas* — which says that it is simony. Panormitanus there understands it of cases where the temporal jurisdiction has a dependence upon an ecclesiastical office, because through such a connection simony is committed (c. *Consulere*, eodem titulo). When, however, the temporal jurisdiction does not have such a dependence, then according to some it is dishonest gain and against the laws, according to Panormitanus on the said c. 1, as in *Authentica, Ut iudices sine quoquo suffragio* and *Authentica, De mandato principum*, § *Illud*. Indeed, according to Hostiensis it is simony to give money so that someone be promoted to be emperor, king, *potestas*, or rector of some city, because jurisdiction is spiritual (C.1 q.3 c. *Ex multis*). Whence to sell a *caltaldionatus* \[gastaldate\] or stewardship is simony (C.1 q.1 c. *Si quis episcopus*, and C.1 q.3 c. *Salvator*), for all power is from God. But the truer opinion is Panormitanus’s: that it is not simony, but dishonest gain — and the texts adduced to the contrary prove nothing except concerning ecclesiastical jurisdiction. Indeed, the truer opinion is that of Sextus \[?\], that it is not even simony, but that something may lawfully be given for obtaining a temporal office (see above s.v. *Dignitas*, § 4 and § 4). This is true if the office is wholly temporal — that is, given by a temporal lord and to be exercised concerning temporal things, as the notariate is given by the emperor and to be exercised in the palace. But if it be wholly ecclesiastical — as when something is given by a prelate concerning the recording of spiritual matters — it is simony, because the jurisdiction by which the prelate grants it is spiritual (unless he were a temporal lord otherwise than through the Church). And the same applies, by the same reasoning, if it be ecclesiastical on the part of the giver who grants it by spiritual jurisdiction, even though he grants it for temporal purposes. But if, conversely, it be temporal on the part of the giver but spiritual on the part of the end — as when a notary of the emperor is taken on by a bishop for the recording of spiritual matters, if something be given so that he be taken on by the bishop, or that he be appointed by the emperor in order to be taken on by the bishop — I think this is not simony, since no jurisdiction is being granted; and to such a one it is permitted to hire out his labour and consequently to buy the position or rank where he is to be employed. See below § 3 of part 4. Whether, however, it is lawful to give anything for a judge’s sentence, see the following §.
