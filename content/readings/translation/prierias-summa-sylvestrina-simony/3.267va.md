## The twentieth question

In the twentieth place it is asked what is the law of simony as to restitution; and I say many things by reason of what has been said above in the same § \[???\] on the fruits of a benefice obtained through simony \[???\].

### The first point

First. Conventional simony, according to all, obliges to restitution of what has been received; but mental simony, if it is in the third \[case\], that is, committed solely from intention and will without a pact, is wiped away by penitence alone and does not oblige to restitution of what was received (same title, *mandato*). Although some, whom Innocent and Hostiensis and Summa Angelica follow, say that this is true in simony which is of positive law, nevertheless others, whom John Andreae and Panormitanus follow, hold better that restitution is required without distinction by the said c. *mandato*, which does not speak of simony that is of positive law — as that Summa Angelica supposes — but of that which is of divine law: that is, by argument from that reception into religion which is to \[???\] spiritualities and \[???\] of princes, just as \[???\] in mental usury, in which intention alone obliges to restitution, as the gloss notes on the said c. *mandato*, and c. *consuluit* (*de usura*) supports this; and it is the common doctrine. The reason is variously assigned by the doctors on the said c. *mandato* and they do not fully satisfy; whence Summa Angelica says that they are ineffective, and says \[???\] that it is so only because the law has declared it — which is not well said, since laws do not declare without reason, being subordinate to moral philosophy. Whence I say that the things which are to be restored by reason of simony are not necessarily to be restored by the rigor of natural or divine law, but only as counsel; because if the giver did not give shamefully, he gave voluntarily and freely, and thus they are no longer owed to him; and similarly if he gave shamefully — whence he neither has nor had certain ownership from the moment they were given, and thus they are not necessarily to be restored, in accordance with what is said above under “Restitution” question 8 § 4. If, however, there is any obligation to restitution, it is from human law, which does not dispose of interior things, or from the disposition of the one possessing them, who can dispose of himself through penitences — otherwise in usuries.
