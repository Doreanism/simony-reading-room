### Ninth

Generally, to take anything for the use of a spiritual power or prelacy is simony; it is otherwise with respect to temporal jurisdiction, as will be stated at the end of this section in response 8. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* 2a 2ae, speaks thus: “The one who commits simony is he whose spiritual power is by office bound to its use in the dispensation of spiritual things, and who also has fixed stipends from the revenues of the Church for his own maintenance; therefore, if he were to take anything for that use, he would not be understood to be hiring out his labour — which by the duty of the office he has undertaken he is bound to expend — but would be understood to be selling the very use of spiritual grace. For this reason it is not lawful to take anything for any dispensation, nor for committing one’s office to deputies, nor for correcting subjects, nor for desisting from correcting them; it is, however, lawful to receive a procuration when one visits — not as the price of correction, but as a stipend due.” Thus Aquinas. And the same applies when a bishop consecrates: yet if the church on account of poverty cannot meet the costs of a procuration, he is to consecrate at his own expense (C.1 q.4 c. *Dum*), according to Bernard and Hostiensis. Add, first, from Archidiaconus, that it is not lawful to accept money for punishing a crime less severely, nor for concealing a crime in an ecclesiastical court, nor for purgation, nor that one may reconcile an unworthily penitent person, nor that one may turn away a worthily penitent person: in all of these cases there is simony, according to Petrus de Palude and X. *De simonia*, c. *Nemo*. Likewise, according to Petrus de Palude and Raymond, it is simony to give anything to a superior so that he should observe justice in a spiritual cause or in ecclesiastical jurisdiction, because these are accessories of spiritual things. And the same according to William, if the matter concerns temporal things but the judging person is ecclesiastical and bound by reason of ecclesiastical jurisdiction to render such justice. If, however, the judge is a layman and judges concerning a temporal matter, it is not simony — though it is a sin. Likewise it is simony to give testimony for money in a spiritual cause, such as a matrimonial cause, a tithe cause, and the like; in other causes it is not simony, but is unjust. From which it follows that, since a judge’s seal is nothing other than the testimony of judges, it does not appear that a judge can demand anything for it — save perhaps the costs of the wax, of the sealer, and of the clerks deputed to this task, according to Durandus in *Speculum* book IV. Indeed, in a spiritual cause founded on ecclesiastical jurisdiction it is simony. To furnish counsel, however, as a jurist, or advocacy as a procurator and advocate — not only in a temporal cause but even in a spiritual one for money — is not simony, nor even a sin, according to Petrus de Palude and Durandus. The reason: since they are not bound by office to render such service, they are not compelled to do a free favour out of their own resources (C.1 q.3 c. *Precario*). Thus he writes. But concerning \[a judge’s\] celebration of a sin — add that it is simony if it be done for money or, according to some, for favour or kinship (cited canon *Nemo*). The reason, according to Innocent and the *Glossa*, is that everyone is bound to judge another’s sin unto punishment (C.23 q.3 c. *Sed*), especially priests. Yet when he does it out of favour, Innocent does not say that he is, strictly speaking, a simoniac, but that he is to be punished as a simoniac. Add also that for pointing out a thief or a fugitive slave a reward may lawfully be taken, *Digest*, *De officio*
