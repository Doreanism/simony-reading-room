If, however, they exact it as a stipend, with no intention of buying or selling, but the intention is directed only to the observance of the custom, it is not simony, especially when one pays voluntarily. In these matters one must carefully beware of what has the appearance of simony or cupidity, according to 1 Thessalonians, last chapter: “From all appearance of evil,” etc. So he. But for an understanding of this kind of custom, note that, according to Panormitanus on *c. sua eo. ti.*, custom can introduce that something be given for a spiritual thing without simony when no exaction has preceded but such a custom has been introduced from the mere liberality and devotion of the people. This is supported by *c. ad apostolica eo. ti.*, where it is said that people are to be compelled to observe a praiseworthy custom. But if a preceding exaction gave rise to the custom, it is not valid, since it has a vicious origin; and so *c. cum in ecclesia* is to be understood, if it is well weighed. But understand this if by custom something is owed to clerics by laymen. Where, however, the custom is that something be exacted from clerics, then according to Panormitanus on *c. iacobus eo. ti.*, what tends to the private advantage of clerics is not valid; but what tends to the favor of divine worship is — understand this if no exaction preceded before the administration of the sacraments. Hence Innocent and John of Lignano say, on the said *c. ad apostolica*, that a custom by which something is given before the reception of the sacraments is corrupt, and is of no force however long-standing. So, granted that no exaction preceded — why does it hold for laymen and not for clerics? The reason for the difference is that laymen seem to have bound themselves under the name of a kind of obligation, by the argument noted on *c. novum genus de deci.* It must also be observed, as St. Thomas indicates in the place cited, art. iv, that under pretext of custom nothing can be exacted by withholding the sacraments, nor on one’s own authority; but, he says, once the spiritual things have first been administered, the established and customary oblations and any other revenues may lawfully be exacted from those who are unwilling but able to pay, with the intervention of superior authority.

### Second

Second. For matrimony, although it is a sacrament, money may lawfully be given and received, according to the Archdeacon following St. Thomas, without simony — not because grace is not there conferred (as is noted, and badly, in *i. q. i. quicquid*), but because it is not a sacrament alone but also an office of nature; and on this account something is given for relieving its burdens and not insofar as it is a sacrament consisting in the dispensation of the Church’s ministers. Hence, according to the law, it is simony to exact something for the blessing of the nuptials, although what is offered spontaneously may be received. *Extra eo. cum in ecclesia.* Nor is it true, as the gloss on *c. sua eo. ti.* says, that something might be requested for it if it were not a sacrament — because, as Panormitanus says there, not only for the sacraments but generally for every ecclesiastical office to be performed, nothing ought to be requested, as in *c. ad nostram eo. ti.* Nevertheless V. V. says that if the priest to whom it pertains is unwilling to grant another the license to bless nuptials except for money, if indeed he does not intend to sell the license but to provide for himself the portion of the oblation that would fall to him, it is not simony.
