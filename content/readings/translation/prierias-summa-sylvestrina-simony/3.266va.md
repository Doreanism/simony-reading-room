Add also that it is the same to offer entreaties to the conferrer of a benefice and to another that he may intercede with the conferrer, because what one does through another \[one does oneself\] (rc. de regu. iur. lib. vi.).

### Fourth

The gift “from service,” as is gathered from St. Thomas in IV, is an act bodily performed for someone’s good, to which the Archdeacon similarly reduces \[the case\] if anyone flatters—although such a one is not bound to renounce a benefice thus acquired, according to Innocent, Bernardus, Hostiensis, whom Summa Rosella follows, against Angelica; concerning which see Adulatio § iv. This gift induces simony, according to St. Thomas in IV, because it is a temporal advantage expected from another and appraisable in money. It is distinguished thus, according to St. Thomas and others: if the service is dishonest, as when one serves a prelate in usuries or commerce or pandering, or if it is devoted to carnal ends, as to the benefit of one’s kindred or one’s own patrimony, it is simony to serve for a benefice or anything spiritual whatever, or to confer this for such service (s. q. iij. Saluator), according to St. Thomas, Durandus, and Richardus. But if, he says in book ii, it is honest, ordered to spiritual things—for instance, to the benefit of the church or the aid of its ministers—from the very devotion of the service one becomes worthy of an ecclesiastical benefice, just as on account of other good works; whence he does not regard it as a gift “from service.” And he adds in book iv that if no pact intervenes, a prelate can without simony give him a benefice, provided he does not have his eye principally on the benefice, but on the dignity of the person; and thus Gregory says in the Register that it is fitting that those who serve in ecclesiastical labors should enjoy ecclesiastical remuneration.
