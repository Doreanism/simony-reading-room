This I take to be true when two conditions concur: first, that the teacher lecture by virtue of the office of a master, in which case the act is spiritual on the part of its principle; second, that a pact or exaction intervene. When these two concur, the teacher is understood — in the Church’s legal presumption — to be selling truth itself, though otherwise perhaps he does not intend it. Thus Thomas says of the spiritual judge in the third book of the *Sentences*. If only the first condition is lacking — for instance, if a learned layman lectures, then, while it may otherwise be unlawful, it has been said to be simoniacal only in the way that it is for other learned persons: namely, if he intends to sell the truth. *A fortiori* the same applies if both conditions are lacking, because then the act is not spiritual on the part of its principle, nor — as far as the Church is concerned — is there a presumption of selling truth. If only the second condition is lacking, namely the pact or exaction, then the same must be said as in the case of the preacher.

### Second

According to Thomas in the third \[book of the *Sentences*\], a judge by his office is bound to render judgement, and likewise everyone is bound, by the fact of being subject to a judgement, to render true testimony. But an advocate is not bound to furnish patronage in a cause, nor is a jurist bound to give counsel. Therefore, as Augustine said to Macedonius, a judge ought not to sell true judgement, nor a witness true testimony, even though an advocate \[may take payment for\] rendering just patronage and a jurist for true counsel — because an advocate does not sell justice, which is spiritual, but his own act; and the same is said of the jurist’s counsel. But a judge, by selling judgement, commits simony if he be a spiritual judge; otherwise it is not simony, but he sins gravely. And the same applies to a witness. Thus Aquinas. Understand this of a witness whether he himself or the cause be spiritual. The same view is held by Alexander of Hales, who also says that patronage and counsel assist a cause externally, but testimony belongs to its substance — whence “facts compel, not advocates.” And so, since to judge is, in a secular judge, a spiritual act only on the part of the end — just as is teaching the liberal arts — it follows that a doctor of arts under obligation to teach is not a simoniac if he accepts something for his teaching. This is true with respect to the judicial presumption; but with respect to God he is a simoniac if he intends to sell truth itself — as when he is unwilling to communicate some elegant and subtle point unless money is given.

### Third

One who gives money for a sentence does not commit simony, according to the *Glossa* on C.1 q.3 c. *Vendentes*, because in profane matters simony is not committed. Qualify this, according to Panormitanus on c. *Cum ab omni* (X. *De simonia, rebus ecclesie*) thus: unless he intends through the sentence to obtain something spiritual or annexed; add: or unless the sentence be from a spiritual person. And in the same way it is simony to corrupt a judge or witness in a spiritual cause, according to Panormitanus on c. *Tua nos*, following Hostiensis, on account of the connection it has with spiritual things — but in a wholly temporal cause it is not. Whence, according to Raymond, it is simony to give anything to a superior so that he should preserve justice in spiritual matters; and the blessed Thomas says the same applies even if the matter be temporal, provided the ecclesiastical person is bound by reason of the jurisdiction granted to him to render such justice. But if the judge be a layman and judge a temporal matter, it will not be simony, but a grave sin. Qualify this: unless a vexation be justly redeemed with respect to a right already required in such a case, since then it will be neither simony nor sin — for instance, when the judge is otherwise unwilling to judge.
