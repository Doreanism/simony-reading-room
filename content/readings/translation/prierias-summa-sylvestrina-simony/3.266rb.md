And as to what he says about stipends, I say that if such a man serves the people, he ought to have a stipend from them; if he does not serve, he ought not to have a stipend, except from God, from whom he has the temporal goods in which he abounds, and he will have eternal goods. And if he does not have temporal goods, he may ask from the church or the bishop sustenance in general, or a simple benefice in particular. And therefore *Summa Angelica*, qualifying the said opinion more strictly, says it is true if he is in need provided he is worthy, and does not intend to advance himself but to do good. But against this it seems that St. Thomas’s argument concerning the cure of souls is not resolved by these \[qualifications\], nor can it be—the argument he sets forth in the second *Quodlibet*, art. 10, q. 1: such a man is presumptuous or proud if he believes himself better than all those over whom he wishes to be set; or he is unjust if he does not so believe; and the argument is founded on Scripture (Hebrews 5): “No one takes the honor to himself, but he who is called by God, as Aaron was.” The things that others bring forward to the contrary do not conclude nor have any appearance \[of force\], for this opinion is not harsh to the humble and frugal and lovers of wisdom—like St. Thomas, who renounced the bulls of the Archbishopric of Naples with the revenues of St. Peter ad Aram on account of humility, frugality, and the quiet of religious life—but \[it is harsh\] only to the ambitious and self-indulgent and to those who do not have the inward delights of the virtues. Nor does promotion to orders require a title with the cure; nor does the toleration of the pope excuse in those things which are prohibited, since they are evils; nor is it improper that the greater part of the beneficed clergy be in an evil state, if it indulges in ambition for the people and in wantonness. Nor is the example of Isaiah to the point, because according to the saints he had seen himself purged by the angel, and did not offer himself for prelacy but for preaching; add that when he heard God saying, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” he understood that he was being sweetly invited by God to the office, to which he offered himself. But if someone offers entreaties for another, a distinction is drawn whether they are spiritual or carnal—that is, founded on kinship or friendship—and whether they are for a worthy or an unworthy person. But the first distinction is not necessary, because, other things being equal, it is not a sin to provide for one’s kindred, according to Durandus (*de coba. cle. et mu. c. j.*). Whence St. Thomas simply says that entreaties for another, if he is unworthy, make the deed simoniacal; but if he is worthy, the deed itself is not simoniacal, although—he says—there can be simony in intention if regard is paid not to the dignity of the person but to human favor; and he means that it is not simony with respect to the church, although there can be \[simony\] with respect to God. But when the entreaties are for an unworthy person, it appears clearly enough, as he says in IV, that nothing else moves \[the petitioner\] to give except favor or the avoidance of danger.
