### Fourth rule

The fourth rule is that it is not simony to give money to redeem vexation in a spiritual matter (extra de simonia dilectus el. j.). Limit this to when it is certain that justice is being done, but not when it is doubtful, according to Thomas II-II ubi supra ar. ult., Peter, and the gloss of Raymond. Hence money can be given so that my prebend may be recovered from an injury done to another, and likewise so that someone may revoke an unjust sentence of excommunication or absolve from it, according to the gloss on c. ad aures eo. ti. Innocent there, however, holds the contrary; but Panormitanus, following John Andreae, says that one may, if from delay in payment grave prejudice threatens, since then vexation is being redeemed — otherwise not, but one’s superior should be approached. And the same applies in cases of suspension and interdict.

### Panormitanus

Panormitanus, however, in c. dilectus filius el. j. eo. ti., comprehending the whole matter of this question, says that in such cases vexation may lawfully be redeemed, both as regards what is possessed and as regards a right to be sought, if it is understood that money is being given, not a spiritual thing; but the one receiving the money unduly is bound to restitution (l. fin. and l. generaliter ff. de calumniatoribus). In spirituals, however, for a right to be sought, it is not lawful to give anything even to remove an impediment of fact (extra de simonia matheus), nor is vexation redeemed thereby, but rather the giver gives so that he may more easily acquire a spiritual right. As for what is already possessed — namely, of a right which one holds and is in fact unjustly molested in — one may give something, as in d. c. dilectus, though the recipient is bound to restore it. But for a right being sought, if anyone vexes justly — as when a bishop wishes justly to depose him, or another justly to accuse him, or pretends a right in the benefice — it is not lawful to give anything, because vexation of fact is not being redeemed (extra de simonia nemo); indeed, not even if he is vexed unjustly and is not in possession of that right but gives in order to obtain a possession unlawfully detained, as Innocent notes in c. ad aures extra eod., and as the gloss holds at xvi. q. vii. decimas, because possession is something spiritual and so does not fall to a layman (de praescriptione causam), or at least is connected to spirituals on account of the administration which follows (j. q. iii. si quis obiecerit). But Panormitanus thinks that one may give when one cannot easily obtain it from a superior and to avoid great prejudice, by analogy with what was said of absolution from excommunication. But still, note according to Hostiensis that, though the giver may redeem without simony, nevertheless the one who returns the spiritual thing he had usurped for money is a simoniac — which is true in itself, though not in the present case, as was said above at decime in fine.
