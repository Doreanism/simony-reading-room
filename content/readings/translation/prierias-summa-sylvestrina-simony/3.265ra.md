### Second

A certain view, which the Archdeacon also reports in his fifth part of the title concerning simony, holds that the one entering a monastery acquires two rights: a spiritual one, by which he has the dignity of clerical status, a voice in chapter, and shares in the goods produced there, and so forth; and a corporeal one, namely that he should be fed and clothed by the monastery. The canons now regularly join these in fact, but by intention they can be separated. This view holds that one may contract for the second right but not for the first, where the monastery’s resources do not suffice for sustenance. This is also the opinion of Saint Thomas. For he says in the *Secunda Secundae*, in the place cited, that for entry into a monastery it is not permitted to accept or demand anything as a price; but if the monastery is poor and not sufficient to support so many persons, it is permitted to grant the entry itself freely yet to accept something for the sustenance of the person to be received, if the monastery’s resources do not suffice for this. So far Thomas. And understand that it is permitted to accept — that is, to demand. Hence in the fourth book he says that then it may be demanded from one who wishes to serve God in that place, not as the price of religion, but so that the monastery may have the means to provide for him. If, however, he can be received without burden to the church, it is simoniacal to demand anything for the reception. So Thomas. And thus it is clear that, while uprightness of intention remains, one may contract for sustenance. This opinion was also held by Ul., saying that in a case of poverty they could have the intention directed to temporal things for sustenance and could express the spiritual things as given freely, while making an agreement about a determined sum of temporal goods — where note the word “offered,” because he does not say “previously bestowed.” The same opinion was held by Rodo., saying that when the monastery is poor it is not simoniacal even to contract in such cases with the gift expressly named and quantified, provided it is not done for the conferring of spiritual things. The same, after Raymond, says that if the one to be received promises a certain annual sum during his lifetime, it is not unlawful. In effect this same view was held by Durandus, who ascribes it to Tancred, Vincent, Raymond, and Hostiensis, according to whom he says the receivers may use these words: “We willingly and freely receive you into our spiritual fellowship, but since our goods do not suffice for us and for you, \[bring\] with you the means by which you may live.” These words in effect and before God amount to saying: “Unless you bring with you the means to live, we will not receive you” — which is to make a pact or to demand. The *Summa angelica* follows this view as well, after the *Summa Pisana* cited above.
