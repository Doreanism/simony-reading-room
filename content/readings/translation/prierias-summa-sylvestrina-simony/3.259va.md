Simony, according to Thomas (II-II, q. 100) and nearly all theologians and jurists in common, is the deliberate will to buy or sell something spiritual or annexed to something spiritual. Panormitanus, on *c. nemo, eodem titulo*, adds “the act having followed,” which was not necessary as regards God, since in a deliberate (that is, considered) will the sin is consummated, but only as regards the judgment of the Church, since the Church does not judge hidden things unless they proceed outwardly.

### Second

Under the name of “buying and selling,” according to Thomas (II-II), is understood every non-gratuitous contract — such as, for example, a settlement, a renunciation, or an exchange (although a prelate may by virtue of his office exchange benefices or prebends, as in *permutatio*, ii, § iiii). Of this same kind is also remission, since according to the *Director* to remit is the same as to give (*s. q. s. si qui sunt*). The Gloss, however, limits this on *c. s. de tef.*, when from the remission a benefit is applied to the party. Paulus also limits it there, when the debt is liquid, and likewise there. Add that under the name of “buying” comes also redemption: whence according to Raymundus, just as spiritual things are not to be sold or bought, so neither are they to be redeemed. This is true; he limits it, however, to one who possessed justly (*extra eo. querelam*), and it is otherwise for one possessing unjustly, as when tithes are redeemed. But that limitation is not necessary, nor is the redemption of tithes the redemption of anything spiritual, but rather of something temporal — as in *decime*, at the end, and § iii, rule iv.

### Third

The “spiritual” itself, according to Thomas (II-II, and in IV, in the place noted), and according to Richardus and Panormitanus on *c. consulere, de simonia*, and Astesanus, is said in three ways. First, essentially — namely grace and the virtues, by which understand all other spiritual or supernatural gifts. Second, causally — as the sacraments, which are the cause of grace and virtues. And third effectually or effectively — as acts proceeding from spiritual grace, sacred office, or any spiritual gift, such as baptizing, celebrating the divine office, working miracles, and the like. These may also be called spiritual by way of cause, inasmuch as they are caused by something spiritual and dispose toward the spiritual.

### Fourth

What is “annexed to the spiritual,” according to Thomas (in IV) and others, is twofold. First, as ordered to the spiritual — such as the right of patronage, which is ordered to presenting clerics to benefices, and sacred vessels ordered to the use of holy things. Add also, generally, everything in which something spiritual is administered, such as a sacred vestment, a church, a cemetery, the relics of the saints, and every sacred object. Although the right of burial is not annexed in this way but in the following way, the cemetery itself is annexed in this first way. Second, as depending on something spiritual — for example, ecclesiastical benefices, which befit only one having clerical office or the right to receive tithes, first-fruits, oblations, the right of burial, and whatever befits clerics by reason of orders, such as ringing the bells, closing the doors of the church, and the like — and also the right of primogeniture.

### Fifth

Under the name of “money,” by which buying and selling are reckoned, is understood, according to Thomas (II-II), everything whose price can be estimated in money, as appears in iii and b.
