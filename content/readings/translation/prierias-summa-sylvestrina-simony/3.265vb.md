### Eighth

If someone is received in religion simoniacally, according to the Archdeacon following Saint Thomas and Raymond, if he himself knew of it he must be expelled without hope of restitution and thrust into a place of stricter rule, *eod. tit. c. Si simonia*; and if no stricter place is found, he must be moved to another monastery of the same rule, at least temporarily, if it cannot conveniently be done permanently, *arg. lxxxi. dist. rf.*, and afterwards he is to be received anew, with his former place changed and a lower one assigned. If, however, he was ignorant of it, it suffices that he renounce his place, and afterwards he can be newly received with his former place changed, *Extra eod. Ex insinuatione*. Understand Raymond — and see — that the foregoing rigor is to be observed even in occult simony, and in the penal forum, even though the crime is not established by inquisition or in due judicial form, *arg. d. c. Quia quod*, which says nothing of accusation or inquisition; but do not understand this of occult simony — i.e., merely mental — but rather of cases that proceed externally by pact or the like, even though otherwise occult, according to Raymond. Yet in favor of religion the bishop could dispense with such persons regarding their order and their place and chapter in religion, the prior agreement being set aside; for since they are to be punished on this account, they ought not from the same fact to obtain a privilege — indeed, he seems to have lost the power of receiving, and those who have not sinned would have acquired it, *arg. De elec. Bone*. Such persons, who have committed simony in such matters, and those who are accomplices, like parents giving dowries, are excommunicated by the *Extravagans* of Urban — although Martin V, according to trustworthy persons (as the Archdeacon reports), declared that it does not extend to dowries given to nuns for entry into a monastery.

## Sixteenth question: what is the law concerning simony which is not committed

not by money given but by some gift — *a manu, a lingua, ab obsequio* — and I say many things.

### First

Money is taken first properly, in coined or numbered form, and so it is not counted among these gifts but is the principle by which such gifts are measured in themselves or in their effect, just as venial sins are. Second, improperly: thus also every measurable thing is comprehended under money improperly, in both senses; and it is so placed in the definition of simony.

### Second

A gift “from the hand,” according to the Archdeacon, is every movable or immovable thing temporally possessed, as is gathered from *f. q. iii. c. Totum*. It induces simony if someone gives or receives a spiritual thing for it, as is clear from the definition of simony. He declares that with respect to God, who sees the intention, this holds however small the gift may be, according to blessed Thomas and Durandus in book IV. But \[ecclesiastical\] judges, who judge as to external matters, do not pronounce simony unless the gift is large, because, as Saint Thomas says in the same place, it is not probable that the mind of a spiritual judge would be bent to do something for a small gift; hence in small gifts given to judges he does not judge that simony is committed. Hence, according to Bonaventure, gifts \[*encenia*\] do not induce simony unless they extend to a substantial quantity.
