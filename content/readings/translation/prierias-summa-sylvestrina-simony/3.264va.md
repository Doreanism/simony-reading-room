### Eighth

One who receives an ecclesiastical benefice simoniacally, according to Archidiaconus following St. Thomas and Raymond and others, is bound to renounce the benefice; and if simony was committed by himself or by another with his knowledge and without his opposition, he is liable for all the fruits, even those that could have been received by a diligent possessor (xiv q. vi *si res aliena* and *de restitutione spoliatorum gravis*) — for in general one who possesses without title is liable for every loss and for every profit received or that could have been received (Dig. *de rei vindicatione* l. *si navis*); but understand this with expenses for the sake of the fruits being deducted, and with fruits and expenses devoted for the sake of the fruits that have been applied to the church’s benefit. And the same seems to apply to one who did not know at first, but after he knew did not at once renounce (argument from X eo. *de simoniace*) — for he is liable for what remains after he knew, but not for what was consumed in good faith (C. *de rei vindicatione* l. *certum*). Yet according to Antonius de Butrio on c. *de regularibus* eo. tit., if such a person is a bishop he will not renounce without papal license, others below \[bishops\] not without the license of their prelates, and exempt persons not without the license of the pope or a legate.

### Ninth

According to Raymond and his gloss, if anyone knows or believes that an order or benefice has been conferred on him principally on account of fleshly services or entreaties interposed by himself or another, a distinction is drawn: for if he was otherwise worthy, he is not bound to resign but must do penance for the corrupt intention (X eo. *mandato*); if however he was unworthy, he is bound to resign, because in that case such entreaties or services take the place of \[simoniacal\] entreaties (s. q. i *sunt nonnulli*, and the agreement, see).

### Tenth

According to Rodericus, to give something to a prelate so that one may not be removed from a priory or benefice is simony — if it is necessary or useful that someone be removed; otherwise not, provided the intention is not corrupt and provided no pact or agreement precedes the collation (i q. ii *quam pio*).

## Fourteenth

Fourteenth, it is asked specifically what the law is concerning simony with regard to leasing the fruits of benefices or otherwise alienating them, and concerning the substitution of prelates’ offices; and I say many things.

### First

If a lease is made by a cleric beyond the proper duration of a lease, it is not valid beyond \[that term\], according to Hostiensis, because a cleric in a benefice is compared to a usufructuary, who in fact holds only for life (xv q. vi *illud*). Innocent says, nevertheless, that if it has been made beyond the lessor’s life in the name of the church and to its advantage, the successor is bound by it (*de solutionibus* c. f., and xii q. ii *hoc habuissemus*); otherwise if it was made in his own name and he did not intend to bind the church. If, however, the lessee or buyer has paid something, according to Hostiensis the successor is bound to ratify it insofar as the money has been turned to the church’s benefit. And although sometimes a rector may lease a church beyond the proper term, nevertheless a canon can never lease a prebend beyond \[the proper term\].
