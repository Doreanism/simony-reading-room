\[Sacred vessels\] are not to be sold by reason of their consecration: that is, if anything additional is demanded for their consecration. Nevertheless, in a case of necessity for the church, they may be sold in respect of what in them is not spiritual, namely the material of gold and silver. In that case, if they are sold to ecclesiastical persons, they may be sold whole. If, however, they are sold to others not for ecclesiastical use, they must first be broken up after a prayer has been said beforehand, nor are sacred things to be handled by any but the ministers of the church. So says Aquinas, and the same is found at X q. ii § *hoc ius*. If, however, some layman should buy a chalice not for profane use but so that the sacrament might at times be reverently confected in it, I do not see that it is necessary that it be broken up or melted down. According to Petrus de Palude, sacred vessels could also be pledged to laymen, and he says the same of relics: that they can be sold or pledged on account of their metal, but not otherwise, “because let no one tear apart the martyrs and let no one make merchandise of them” (C. *de sacrosanctis ecclesiis* l. *nemo*).

### Second

Concerning sacred vestments, since the principle is the same as for vessels, the law is also the same.

### Third

According to St. Thomas in *Sentences* IV, with regard to consecrated ground in burial there is a similar principle as with the return of sacred vessels: for a consecrated cemetery may be sold by reason of the land in a case of necessity for the church, just as a fortified site may be, but not by reason of its consecration, as for example if someone were buried there for money. Abraham did not sin in buying the double cave, because it was not consecrated for burial; or, if it was consecrated, possession of it passed into the jurisdiction \[of the purchaser\] together with the whole field. So Aquinas, with much else aimed at excusing Abraham and condemning Ephron. On which see above under *sepultura* (su. § iv).

### Fourth

As for the right of primogeniture, Jacob did not sin in buying it, according to St. Thomas in *Sentences* IV, because it was owed to him by divine institution, and therefore by his pressing he is understood to have redeemed his own.

### Fifth

Likewise, according to St. Thomas in *Sentences* IV, the right of patronage cannot be sold by itself nor given in fief, although it passes along with an estate if the estate is sold or given in fief, as above under *ius patronatus* § v. And add, according to Panormitanus on c. *querela* *de simonia*, that one who sells an estate together with the right of patronage that he has in it is a simoniac: because the copulative construction makes the conjoined item come along principally and not accessorily; otherwise if he says “I sell the estate with the right of patronage” — yet in conscience attend to the intention. Add also that if a patron has received anything for presenting a candidate, he is a simoniac according to Panormitanus on c. *veniens* (cl. f. *de testibus*); the third matter is good in the case. Further, what is found under *de transactionibus*.

### Sixth

Likewise, according to St. Thomas in *Sentences* IV, the right of receiving tithes follows upon a spiritual office: for it is owed to the ministers of the church, nor can laymen hold such a right, although sometimes for a time the fruits of tithes are conceded to them on account of some necessity of the church. In that case, in the manner in which it can be given to laymen, it can be redeemed from them by the bishop’s authority. And one church cannot buy from another the right of receiving tithes. Thus from him. On which see above under *decima* § x. Say the same concerning oblations and offerings and the right of receiving such things. St. Thomas says nevertheless that if a layman is unwilling to restore the tithe except for money, he commits simony on his part, but the church may legitimately redeem it. But he says the contrary \[elsewhere\] — see above under *decima* § x.
