we may reckon, a little under or altogether, a third part of all the temporal lands. Consider well how many parsonages or vicarages there are in the realm that have at least a plowland \[as much land as one plow can till\] apiece. Then take note of the lands of bishops, abbots, priors, nuns, knights of Saint John’s, cathedral churches, colleges, chantries, and free chapels. For though the house fall into decay and the founder’s endowment be lost, yet they will not let go of the lands. Whatever comes in once may never come out again. They turn it into a free chapel, so that whoever thinks it must come to nothing is mistaken. Besides all this, how many chaplains do gentlemen maintain at their own cost in their houses? How many sing for souls by bequest of testaments? Then there is the proving of testaments and the appraising of goods—the Archbishop of Canterbury’s prerogative. Is that not a great sum throughout the realm in a year? Four offering days, and private tithes. There is no servant but he must pay some part of his wages. None shall receive the body of Christ at Easter—be he ever so poor a beggar, or ever so young a lad or maid—but they must pay something for it. Then mortuaries \[death dues to the parish\], for forgotten tithes (as they say).
