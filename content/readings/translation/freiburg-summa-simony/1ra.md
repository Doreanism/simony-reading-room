# On Simony. Title I.

Since among the crimes of the Church the simoniacal heresy holds first place, as is clear in *1 q. ultima, c. Patet*, so that this most dangerous plague may be better guarded against and rooted out, we must first consider simony itself.

## Question One

Ask, then, what simony is. Answer: Simony is the deliberate will to buy or sell something spiritual or something annexed to a spiritual thing. This is the definition commonly given by both theologians and jurists. For they commonly add “or something annexed to a spiritual thing,” as does Thomas in his commentary on the fourth book of the *Sentences*, distinction 25, and Petrus de Tarantasia, and also Albertus in the same distinction; and Thomas in the *Summa*, q. 100, article 1. And when Raymundus, under the same title, § 1, says that the addition is unnecessary, this is not in conflict, as Ulricus explains in his sixth book, third treatise, c. 19, *§ Et non est contradictio* and the following section, because the conflict is merely verbal. “Spiritual” is used both in a broad sense and in a strict sense. Taken in the broad sense it is further used in four ways: namely, essentially, as grace and the virtues; or by participation, as churches, cemeteries, sacred vessels, and the like — for these participate in consecration, which comes about through a spiritual blessing; or ordinatively, that is, what is ordered toward something spiritual, as tithes and first-fruits and all ecclesiastical benefices are ordered toward this end, that the ministers of the Church, sustained by them, may be free to devote themselves to spiritual things; or as a reward directed toward something spiritual. Spiritual in the third way named is like the presentation which, by reason of the right of patronage, someone seeks for another: it is a way presented for obtaining, through investiture, that benefice to which the candidate is presented. And this indeed is called spiritual inasmuch as authority with respect to a spiritual thing is in some manner spiritual. But inasmuch as that authority is not principal but preparatory to the act of the principal authority, it is something extrinsically added to the principal thing for which it is preliminary. Thus it is called annexed to a spiritual thing. Nevertheless, what is spiritual in the third sense is also called annexed to a spiritual thing, just as that which is ordered to assist something else is annexed to it. But spiritual in the strict sense is only that which is essentially spiritual. And this is twofold: for it is either purely spiritual, as grace, the virtues, gifts, and the like; or it is essentially spiritual but received in a corporeal element, as sacramental grace. And so the aforesaid last three are not spiritual things, but things annexed to spiritual things. Thomas in the *Summa*, article 1, in reply to the sixth argument, deals with this briefly, saying that just as the soul lives by itself while the body lives by its union with the soul, so some things are spiritual in themselves, as the sacraments and other such things, while some are spiritual because they adhere to spiritual things. The lord Hostiensis, book 5, rubric 3, *De symonia, § 1 Quid sit*, at the word “*mihi videtur*,” describes it thus: Simony is the receiving or giving of spiritual things, or of things annexed to spiritual things, with a preceding agreement of promise, condition, manner of service, entreaty, or any temporal consideration.
