And I believe that, since someone has been promoted to sacred orders — given that ordination of this kind requires an ecclesiastical title — he may petition for an ecclesiastical benefice even though he abounds in temporal goods. And the laws which the master adduces to prove the contrary speak according to the state of the primitive Church, when revenues were slender and distribution was made from the resources of the Church as each one had need. And Ulricus agrees with him, §. “secundum est preces.” For no one is compelled to serve as a soldier at his own expense. If, however, he petitions for himself a dignity or the cure of souls out of such great presumption as renders him unworthy of such a benefice, it is simony, because the entreaties are on behalf of an unworthy person.

## Question 15

Which simony is the graver sin. The response, according to Ulricus, §. “quamvis autem in omnibus”: although simony is committed in a gift from the hand, from the tongue, and from service, nevertheless, because a gift from the hand is more properly a price than one from the tongue or from service, therefore that simony is the greater sin.

## Question 16

Whether to sell sacred vessels, sacred vestments, a cemetery, holy water, and the like is simony. The response, according to Ulricus, §. “est tamen differentia” and the three following: all sacramentals ordered to some spiritual effect ought not to be sold, such as holy water, blessed bread, and the like, through which venial sins are remitted. Whence a sacrament cannot be sold even by reason of its matter, as is clear from 1 q. 1 “placuit,” sections 1 and 2. But those things which are spiritual by participation, such as a chalice and a cemetery and the like, before consecration can simply be sold. But after consecration, if they are sold and bought, it is not simony in itself, provided the intention refers only to the temporal thing and not to the spiritual. But if it is simony, this is by reason of the Church’s prohibition; and therefore those things which are prohibited cannot be sold without simony, such as balsam or the oil of chrism and the blessed water of baptism and the like, as in the chapter “predictis placuit” and extra eodem titulo “ad nostras.” It is otherwise with plain water, in which, since it was granted beforehand, there is no simony in its buying or selling — as with a chalice, which can be sold to another church in the same form. But it cannot be sold to a layman or a private person unless it is melted down, 10 q. 1 §. “hoc ius porrectum.” The same judgment applies also to a cemetery, which by reason of the land can be sold and bought, as is clear in the example of Abraham. The same holds for other sacred vessels and vestments. Goffredus and Hostiensis, §. 2 and “Item queritur,” say simply that in any ecclesiastical ministry simony can be committed, extra eodem “ad nostras.” There it is said that for the funerals of the dead and the blessing of those marrying and the like nothing should be exacted; pious customs, however, are to be observed. Raymundus too, §. 6, “on a gift from service,” at the end, and the gloss of Willelmus there, do not seem to disagree from these, if it is rightly understood. Likewise the gloss, §. 16.
