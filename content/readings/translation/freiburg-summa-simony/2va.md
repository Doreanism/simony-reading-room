## Question 13

What if two priests agree between themselves, “You will celebrate the high Mass for me, and I another for you.” The response, according to Willelmus in the gloss there on the section: a spiritual thing is licit, especially when there is a \[good\] cause; and when it is said that this man renders a spiritual service for some spiritual thing, the word “for” does not denote a final cause or a pricing but a supporting aid, since this man cannot sing in place of the other unless the other sings in his place, given that he ought not to sing two Masses.

## Question 14

What is a gift from the tongue, and when is simony committed in it. The response, according to Thomas in the Summa, article 5, argument 3: it must be said that a gift from the tongue means either praise itself, pertaining to human favor and falling under a price, or also entreaties by which human favor is acquired or its opposite avoided — as when the entreaties are armed. Which example he adds in his commentary; and therefore, if someone aims principally at this, he commits simony. And he seems to aim principally at this who grants entreaties on behalf of an unworthy person, whence the act itself is simoniacal; but if entreaties are offered on behalf of a worthy person, the act is not simoniacal — yet simony can lie in the intention, namely when one has regard not to the dignity of the person but to human favor. With these things Ulricus agrees, §. “a gift from the tongue” and the following one. Raymundus, §. 7, “on a gift from the tongue,” distinguishes: for either someone is ordained or chosen through another’s entreaties or through his own. If through another’s, then either they are carnal entreaties and are made on behalf of an unworthy person — then it is simony, 1 q. 1 “de ordinationibus,” for then the entreaties take the place of a price, 1 q. 1 “sunt nonnulli.” But if they are spiritual entreaties and on behalf of a worthy person, simony is not committed, provided that the one ordaining or electing has regard not to the entreaties but to God and the merits of the person, extra “de etate et qualitate” “tuam,” 1 q. 1 “latorem.” With these things Thomas, Petrus, and Ulricus agree, §. “preces autem,” and Hostiensis, §. “si autem.” ¶ But if someone is ordained or chosen through his own entreaties, Huguccio and many others have said that it is simony, however worthy he may be, 7 q. 1 “in scrip.,” 1 q. 1 “ordinationes.” Raymundus says: I, saving a better judgment, distinguish: that one who holds the cure of souls cannot petition for himself a church or dignity or other benefice; and in this I agree with Huguccio, by the laws cited above and extra “de iure patronatus” “per nostras.” But a simple benefice, if he is in need and feels himself worthy, he may petition without fear of the sin of simony, extra eodem titulo “tua nos,” and the argument from “de rescriptis” “ad aures,” extra “de prebendis” “cum secundum.” And in this I agree with the others. With these things Thomas agrees in the Summa, article 5, argument 3, and also in his commentary; and Hostiensis, verbo “lingua”; and Goffredus.
