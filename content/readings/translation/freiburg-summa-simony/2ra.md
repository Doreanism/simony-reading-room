## Question 9

What if money was promised on my behalf without my knowledge, and afterward I paid or repaid it? The answer, according to the gloss of Guilelmus here, the fourteenth, “what shall be done,” is that as for paying the money, it must be said that, although I have sinned gravely — and although in evil deeds ratification has retroactive effect and is equivalent to a mandate — nevertheless the bishop can dispense, since in truth he was not a simoniac in the receiving of the benefice, as Tancredus noted in *Extra*, on simony, *Sicut nobis tuis litteris*; for Bernardus and Hostiensis say or report the same in the gloss in the same place, and do not reject it.

## Question 10

Whether in some cases something may lawfully be given for spiritual things. The answer, according to Raymundus in the same title, under the section “Furthermore there are cases,” is that it must be said that in some cases something is lawfully given for spiritual things — namely, a gift from the hand, a gift from service, and a gift from the tongue. A gift from the hand can be given in five cases. The first: if it is offered freely, not for a spiritual thing, as in 1 q. 1 *Quicquid invisibilis*. For as the gloss of Guilelmus says in the same place, if this preposition “for” denotes a valuation or an exchange, then it is never to be granted that a temporal thing be given for a spiritual thing or vice versa. But if it denotes a final cause — so that the meaning is “he gave money for the sacrament,” that is, on account of veneration for the sacrament or in order to venerate the sacrament — this can lawfully be done and granted. Likewise if it denotes an efficient cause — so that the meaning is “he gave for the sacrament,” that is, out of the devotion he had toward the sacrament — it can lawfully be done and granted. And Ulricus, at chapter 19, agrees well enough with this, likewise concerning this preposition “for.” But if it is doubtful whether the thing was given freely, or out of an agreement, or out of a corrupt intention, three things especially must be attended to, according to Raymundus: the quality of the giver and the receiver — namely, whether by a poor man to a rich one or vice versa, or by a rich man to a wealthy one; the quantity of the gift — namely, whether the thing given is of great or lesser value; and the time of the gift — namely, whether it is conferred under pressing necessity or at another time. According to this a presumption is to be made, either for or against, as in *Extra*, in the same title, *Si questiones*. But these things are to be understood, as Ulricus says here, as all said with respect to the forum of litigation, where a presumption of simony is taken from many factors. In the forum of conscience, however, the intention is what is regarded. Hostiensis says the same here, in the third part, “how it is committed.” The second case, according to Raymundus, is for spiritual works to which one is not bound by office, as in 12 q. 2 *Caritatem*; Hostiensis takes an argument from the fact that a priest may hire out his labors if he has no other means of living. The third: for certain spiritual works that are owed — for instance, when a bishop consecrates a church or visits the diocese, he may demand moderate procurations, as in *Extra*, in the same title, *Cum sit Romana*; 10 q. 2 *Relato*; *Extra*, on prescriptions, *Cum ex officio*; *Extra*, on taxes, in the *Novella* of Innocentius, the chapter *Romana*, in book 6.
