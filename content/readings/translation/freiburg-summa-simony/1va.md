## Question 5

Whether in this matter the will alone, even a will that is not deliberate, makes someone a simoniac. For simony is said to be “the deliberate will to buy,” etc. The gloss of Guillelmus explains this as “a will that advances toward an exterior act,” and Ulricus and others say the same in their writings on Peter. The response, according to Raymundus and the *Concordia* of the title \[on simony\], § 2, “Simony is,” etc., and according to the other jurists commonly, must be this: that certain things are forbidden which are simoniacal, such as to sell or buy sacred things, and in brief, whatever things were simoniacal in the Old and New Testaments — in these the will alone suffices. But certain things are simoniacal which are forbidden, namely, because they are simoniacal only by a constitution of the Church — as when someone resigns a church on the condition or pact that it be given to his nephew or to another. In such cases the will alone does not suffice, unless it proceeds into act, as in *extra, de officio delegati, Ex parte* \[part one\]. Goffredus says the same under the same title, § “It is committed, however.” And Petrus says so in his writing on Innocentius, in the gloss on the chapter *Tua nos*, under the same title. This gloss also says: Note that, since Gehazi was a simoniac and yet is not read to have made any pact, anyone who said that there is no simony without an express pact would be in error. As to what Raymundus says here, “unless it proceeds into act,” understand it according to the gloss here: into act, I do not mean the act of resigning, but the act of making a pact. Hence Ulricus, § 3, “In the aforesaid,” says that in these matters one does not sin by the will alone, nor even by the will proceeding into act simply. But if the will proceeds into act in that manner according to which the thing is forbidden — namely, under a condition and a pact — then it is simony. For if without this someone resigns a benefice with the intention that the one who is worthy should receive it, there is no sin. This gloss agrees with these things. Among the number of those things which are simoniacal because they are forbidden, as Goffredus says, is to sell a vicariate, a castellany, a stewardship, an advocacy, and the like, as in *extra*, under the same title, *Ad nostra*; 1 q. 1, *Si quis episcopus*; and q. 2, *Salvator*. Ulricus says the same.

## Question 6

How an exchange of ecclesiastical benefices may be made. The response, according to Ulricus, § “I have excepted, however”: it must be said that either there is an exchange of benefices only, or of possessions only, or of both — as when benefices unequal in their possessions are exchanged, and therefore the one more abundant asks that a certain sum of money be added to him, so that they may thus be able to arrive at an equality of exchange. The first cannot be done by the authority or the pact of the parties without simony. But the bishop, if he sees that it would be useful, can by virtue of his office transfer the persons and make the exchange. Hence the parties also ought not to confer with one another about this. But each, or one of them, may disclose to the bishop his desire and the reason or usefulness of his exchange. Some nevertheless say, as Guillelmus does in the gloss here, that the parties may without sin intimate their wishes to one another. But the first is safer, as Ulricus says. Innocentius IV also, in the gloss on *extra, de rerum permutatione*, on the chapter *Quaesitum*, says that it is not lawful for the parties to make a pact and treat about this among themselves. He also says in the same place that such words may be addressed to the bishop: “Since I cannot safely remain in my church or make progress” — namely, on account of a difference of language or for some other just cause — “I beg that you provide for another from my prebend or church, and provide for me in another.” We also believe that he may say to him that his church would better suit so-and-so and that so-and-so’s would better suit him, by the argument of *extra, de praebendis, Inter cetera*. Hostiensis also agrees with this in the gloss in the same place. It continues in Ulricus: An exchange of possessions, however, is not in itself prohibited, as Pope Clement III says in *extra, de rerum permutatione, Ad quaestiones*, because there a temporal thing is given for a temporal thing.
