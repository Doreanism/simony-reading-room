# Causa I, Question I

*(Continuing the preceding canon, from p. 511.)*

> \[is to be condemned\]; but the holiness of the sacraments is by no means to be violated in any perverse person. For it is established that this \[holiness\], whether in perverse and wicked men inside the Church or in those outside, remains unpolluted and inviolable; and that they are said to pollute these things only insofar as it lies in their power, while the things themselves remain unpolluted: in the good they remain unto reward, in the evil they remain unto judgment. *And below.* §. How are those Catholics, who trust that the promises of God can be impeded from being fulfilled by no human crimes — and who attend his sacraments with such due veneration that, even if they have been handled by the unworthy, they show that, while those persons are condemned by their own persuasion, the sacraments themselves remain undefiled in their holiness — how then can such persons fail to glorify God?

*α. ¶*Quod accepit*. Algerus likewise has this, although the original reads slightly differently.*

*β. ¶*Neque vllo modo*. These §§, down to the word* violabitur*, have been added to the original; from these too this chapter has been enlarged in other places.*

*γ. ¶*Vel argentum*. In the printed codices of St. Augustine there follows* sed aes*, but in two Vatican manuscripts,* vel as*.*

> 12. From these words of Augustine it is clear that in all persons — whether apostates, heretics, or the condemned — true and holy sacraments remain, so far as they are in themselves: but, unless they are aided by an indulgence, they redound to the damnation of the usurper, whether of the one who has them, gives them, or receives them. What good is it, then, that they are true and holy, when they destroy their usurpers b just the same as if they were evil and noxious? Crassus c thirsted for gold, and he drank gold — true gold, just as it would have been true poison. ¶Likewise gold d is just as truly gold in the chest of a thief as in the treasury of a king. But by a thief, since it is held unlawfully, it is unlawfully given or received; by a king it is lawfully held, lawfully given and received: and therefore the thief himself and the accomplice in the theft (unless perhaps he purge himself by acknowledging the theft), as f Gelasius declares in writing to the Emperor Anastasius, will both justly and worthily be condemned. ¶But it is objected —

**Ex his.** CASE. Gratian infers from the foregoing chapter that true sacraments exist among the heretics so far as they are in themselves, but they bring damnation to those who give, receive, or possess them, unless they return — just as true gold proved harmful to Crassus.

**a. ¶Ex his.** He infers from the preceding chapter that the sacraments are without distinction true among the heretics, down to §. *opponitur*, and there he raises an objection. He presents Augustine as he himself understands Augustine to speak, down to §. *sed ne*, where he offers one solution; down to §. *quamuis*, where he offers another, sound and catholic solution, in proof of which he adduces four examples that I shall note in the text.

**b. ¶Perimant.** That is, *those things* — namely, the abuse of them — as *Io. de Fant.* says, since these are Augustine’s words, down to §. *opponitur*; Hugo holds the contrary view: rather, they are Gratian’s words, as appears here in the marginal note. *Below, in the same place.*

**c. ¶Crassus.** This dictator was a contemporary of Julius Caesar and Pompey, and was killed by the Parthians with molten gold.

**ITEM AVRVM.** CASE. Say here that gold is gold both in the thief’s chest and in the king’s chest, but it is unlawfully given by the thief, and therefore the thief and the accomplice will be charged with the harm they have caused. And in the same place, from *ex his* down to the chapter *per Esaiam*, as H. says.

**d. ¶Aurum.** He who shares in it is held liable. *ff. de com. fur. proinde.* There is, however, a difference between the thief and the accomplice; for the accomplice cannot be manifest \[i.e., caught in the act\]. *ff. de fur. is qui opem.*

**e. ¶Furti.** It seems, then, that one is bound to purge oneself with respect to such knowledge. Argument: *insti. de obli. q. ex delic.* §. *sed hac actio. nes. C. de fur. ciuile. arg. 1. q. 4. quia praesulatus.* The contrary view is found in the corresponding place. And this is the argument that a person both may and must denounce a thief. *Arg. ext. de fur. quicumque positis.* Argument to the contrary: *si. de cod, ob tur. caus. ide.* and *si ob stuprum §. ult.* and *ff. de praescri. uer. l. solet.* For in both places it is said that I may lawfully accept money a from the master to point out a runaway slave. *Io. Sol.* In this case it seems possible to say that, according to the canons, it is not lawful.

**f. ¶Vt perhibet.** Here it appears that these are rather Gratian’s words than Augustine’s.

**OPPONITVR.** CASE. Here Gratian objects 5 to what was said above, namely, that heretics cannot confer orders, even though they can baptize. For even a deposed priest, although he can baptize, cannot consecrate; nor can one who has been deposed confer orders, even though he can baptize. But to this objection he offers a twofold solution. The first begins at \[*Sed ne*\]: that those who are received into the Church by the final imposition of hands retain its power, and those who depart from the Church \[retain it\], by similarity to the character of matrimony, which after divorce either is not retained, or otherwise if outside the Church. Gratian offers another solution, which begins at \[*Quamuis*\], where he says that the sacred rites of heretics are true, provided that they are celebrated according to the form of the Church, but they have no effect when ill ministered or ill received. He proves this by four arguments. First, by the true Eucharist given to the traitor. Second, by \[the case where\] the true \[ministry\] in his own country had no effect, because faith was lacking there. Third, by the twofold transfiguration of Christ, one of which did not profit unbelievers. Fourth, because the foolish virgins had virginity just as much as the wise. Finally he concludes that the sacred rites of the heretics are true. in \[?\]
