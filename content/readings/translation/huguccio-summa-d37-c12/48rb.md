# On D. 37, c. 12. *De quibusdam locis*

Behold, suppose \[a master\] is unwilling to give a cleric permission to attend the schools unless a fee is paid. The cleric gives him the fee. Is this simony? I answer: it is — yet at least it is shameful gain and a mortal sin, since he is taking a price for what he was bound to give freely. Argument from D. xc, C. i q. iii *Videtur*, and C. ix q. ii *Quia si rectores*. And likewise it is shameful gain and a mortal sin if anyone accepts money in order to grant someone the license to teach, as used to be done at Paris. For the chancellor would not permit anyone to direct schools there unless a price had been paid — a corruption which another of \[the popes\] rebuked, and under pain of anathema forbade ever to be done again, as in *Extra* \[X 5.5.3\] *Quanto Gallicana* and the immediately preceding \[X 5.5.1\] *Quoniam ecclesia Dei*. Perhaps in such a case simony was indeed being committed, where the master was supposed to be teaching theology.

Behold, suppose a master gives a cleric leave to attend the schools, but will not give him leave under his own blessing unless a price is paid. Is this simony? I draw a distinction: if you grant that by law he was bound to keep him in his schools, then simony is committed on the bishop’s side, and not on the cleric’s. Argument from C. i q. iii *Si quis*, and C. i q. ii *Quod pro*. But if by law he was not bound to keep him in the schools, then simony is committed on both sides.
