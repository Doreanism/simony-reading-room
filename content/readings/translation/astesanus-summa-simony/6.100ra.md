But Bernardus says: hence the pope is not permitted to take money for any spiritual thing, just as no other man may, indeed he sins all the more gravely the higher the office he holds. For although the goods of the church belong to him as principal dispenser, they do not belong to him as lord and possessor. And so Thomas says that if he were to receive money for any spiritual thing, whether from the goods of the church or from other goods, he is not free of the vice of simony.

## Next, we must consider the third question, namely in how many and in what ways simony is committed.

Concerning this it must be known that simony is committed by a threefold gift, as Gregorius says, on account of which Isaiah 33 says: Blessed is he who shakes his hands free of every gift. For there is the gift of the hand, namely money; the gift of the tongue, namely favor; and the gift of service, namely servitude continually rendered, *1, q. 1, sunt nonnulli*. And it is sometimes contracted on one side only, that is, of the giver or of the receiver; sometimes on both sides; sometimes on neither, as when, with both parties unaware, a friend of the one to be ordained gives or promises something to the bishop’s counselor, *1, q. 1, statuimus*, and *c. si qui a simoniacis*.

### Beyond the threefold gift mentioned above, the Cantor of Paris posited a fourth, namely the gift of blood,

whence he said that to confer a church or prebend on someone in consideration of consanguinity is simoniacal. But to others, such as Alexander de Hales and Thomas, it seems that this is indeed a grave sin, but not simoniacal, because nothing is given or received there as a price, which is what is required for simony. Nevertheless, if any prelate were to give some ecclesiastical benefice to someone with this pact or intention that he provide for his own kinsmen, he would be a simoniac. Of the three gifts mentioned above, then, we must speak in order.

### And first concerning the gift of the hand. It must be noted that to give or receive this gift

for spiritual things, or even for things annexed to spiritual things, always entails simony, unless it be so small that it ought not to entail it, the disposition of the giver being considered along with the cause, the time, and the quality of the person, as in *c. ad questiones* — which is true if no pact preceded; otherwise the contrary, as *extra eodem, tue*, and *1, q. 1, placuit* and *nullus*. And judges especially must attend to this, because whatever men do, intention judges all, that is, with respect to the giver, *extra de electione, cum ex officio*. Hence Laurentius says: in matters of gifts, attend to these four things: who you are giving, or who you are seeking from, what cause is being sought, who — that is, poor or rich, what thing, a price great or small; and a fifth may be added, namely whether he confers it under pressing necessity or at another time.
