§ But could any remedy be applied to this matter? I reply that yes, according to Hostiensis: namely, that the nuns receive girls freely and spiritually. And even if they are so poor that they cannot provide for them in necessities, let them proceed against the father to compel him to give support, to which he is bound, as will be said below in the treatise on matrimony; or, if he is dead, let them proceed against the father’s heir, as in c. *De sanctis* *Episcopis* §. *Nullam*, penult. col. 9. Suppose someone, fearing to suffer a refusal, says to the abbot, “If you would receive me, I would give you my land.” The abbot, having taken counsel with the brethren, receives him \[in ignorance of the law\], and \[the man\] in whose hearing the abbot spoke this in chapter — afterwards, before he takes the habit, gives all his goods, or part of them, to the monastery: is this simony, and may such a man remain there? Rosso replies that whether he was received with a view to that promise or not, there is simony at least on the part of the offerer, since he interposed the condition of giving temporal things for his reception, as the canon implies (X eod. *Nobis*). Nor may the man remain in this monastery, nor may he reclaim the goods given, since he is in a base cause; he is, however, bound to transfer to another monastery, if by that reception he was bound to religious life — for example, if he gave himself over and was received as a monk and brother, since this is a kind of profession (argument from X *De regularibus et transeuntibus ad religionem* *Porrectus*). § What if the condition of having such things was expressly made on the part of the receiver, and the one to be received remained altogether silent, intending not to give but to enter on God’s account: may he remain there? Rosso replies that he may not, just as in the preceding case, since his silence — when he did not refuse the unlawful pact — must be reckoned as consent, even in the penitential forum, if he kept silent for this reason: that he feared a refusal if he spoke. § What if a condition is interposed on the part of the one to be received or on the part of the abbot, but, before he is received and before he enters, both repent of the condition imposed, and revoke it, and he asks to be received on God’s account alone, to which they consent: is this lawful? Rosso replies that it is, provided that the intention is not corrupt. But if he had already been received and had entered the monastery before the revocation of such a condition, by that very fact he would have made himself unworthy of that monastery.
