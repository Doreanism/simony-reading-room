### What if it were established in some church that no procession should be held at anyone’s funeral unless he bequeathed a fixed sum of money?

Raymundus and Thomas reply that such a statute would be illicit, because it would exclude the office of piety — that is, if the procession were not held for someone present either gratis or for a smaller sum (argument from Extra, de iureiurando, “Sicut”, and i.7.z.). But if it were established that for all who bequeath a fixed sum such an honor would be performed, when otherwise there was no obligation to do so, this is neither simony nor illicit, provided they intend to have it only by reason of the labor (argument from Extra, “Cum sit”). The first arrangement entails the expectation of an exaction; the second entails the expectation of a gratuitous recompense.

### Whether it is lawful to give money for celebrating an anniversary.

Richardus replies that it is not lawful to make a pact for giving money for this; yet if it is given out of devotion, the church is bound to celebrate for those for whom it is given, in proportion as it is more obligated by the benefaction. Innocentius, however, in *Ne praelati vices suas*, in book VI, on the chapter *Cum enormis*, says that it is meritorious to establish certain rents in some church so that someone’s anniversary may be performed there. And this can be established by those who have the power to make anniversaries (Extra, de praebendis, “Significati”), but not by the donor himself, since it is not lawful for him to attach spiritual and temporal conditions, like the redeemers of captives (and i.q.z. “Aurum”). But Hostiensis, on the same chapter *Cum enormis*, says that this does not please him; and that, while imposing this burden on the rents, it would be safer to say: “I leave to such-and-such a church annually 10 pounds upon such-and-such a house, to be divided only among those present at my anniversary, and I ask the prelate or chapter or convent of the said church to perform my anniversary as has been said.”

### What if a canon attends the hours principally for the sake of the hourly pence — does he commit simony by this?

Iohannes Andreae replies, in book VI, *De clericis non residentibus*, following Hostiensis (who notes this at Extra, de verborum significatione, “Cum olim”), that such a one does not commit simony, because no express pact intervenes; yet he sins gravely and makes satisfaction by penance (Extra, eodem, “Tua nos”, and the last chapter).

### What if a cleric keeps vigil over the dead for money and recites the Psalter?

Hostiensis replies that if he made a pact that for ten pence he would read the Psalter, it is simony; but if the pact was that he would keep vigil over the dead, it is not simony, even though it is tacitly understood that he must read it (argument from Extra, de praebendis, “Significatum”).
