If, however, no pact intervenes, then a distinction is drawn according to Hoff., Hostiensis, and Berengarius on the chapter cum essent: either I serve you with this hope as a secondary motive — namely, that you may confer a benefice on me, while I serve principally for free — and then I do not sin (argument from D. 1, quid proderit); or I serve principally so that you may confer a benefice on me, and \[serve\] gratis only secondarily, and then I sin — though in such a case it is enough for me to make satisfaction by penance, as was said above. According to this distinction the passage Extra, eodem, tua nos is to be understood, according to Hoff., and the laws which seem mutually contradictory are reconciled. Otherwise it can be distinguished according to Raymundus, Hoff., and Hostiensis as follows: the service is either corporal or spiritual; if it is corporal and honorable, it does not induce \[the\] vice, provided no pact intervenes — as if someone serves by going to Rome, by acting as advocate, by transacting his business. And this is implied a contrario in C. 1, q. 1, sunt nonnulli, where it speaks of “a gift from service when service is rendered improperly.” And understand this where a worthy person renders service to a worthy person, as has been said; if, however, a worthy person renders service to an unworthy one, or an unworthy person to a worthy one, it is not free of the vice of simony — as if he acts to the advantage of his \[the prelate’s\] kinsmen, or does something for him, this is a “gift from service,” and it is simoniacal according to Thomas. If, however, the service is spiritual and a pact intervenes, it induces the vice — unless it is annexed to the benefice or personatus, as when this burden is imposed on a particular prebend, that whoever holds it shall say Masses of the Blessed Virgin (Extra, de prebendis, significasti). And understand, according to Rodericus, that a benefice ought not to be given on account of corporal and honorable service; rather, it is given to the one rendering service whose merits have been made known through such service, because, as the Cantor of Paris said, “courts are the proving-grounds of clerics.”

### ¶ But suppose

But suppose that one priest is to say the High Mass and another the Low Mass, and they agree among themselves that one will say the Mass for the other on a reciprocal basis; is this lawful for them? I answer that it is, because when this one says \[Mass\] he renders a spiritual service for another spiritual \[service\]; the word “for” here does not denote pricing but support, since the one cannot sing for the other unless that one also sings for him.
