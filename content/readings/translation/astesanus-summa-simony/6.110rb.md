I respond that nothing is. For he received it simoniacally and stealthily, and so did not prescribe. And although it was simony on the part of the bishop, it was not on the part of the monastery, which was redeeming its own right — which it was free to do: 1 q. 1 *Nullus*.

### What of confraternities so constituted that no one is received into them unless he promises or even swears that he will give ten denarii each year?

Raymundus responds that if they are made for an honest cause, such as for feeding the poor, redeeming captives, and the like, there is no simony in this; rather, they are lawful and praiseworthy, and whoever opposes them is to be anathematized: dist. 42 *Si quis respicit*, and argument from 16 q. 7 *Sane*. I do not, however, approve the oath, because of the danger of perjury. According to Hostiensis, however, such a statute under such a negative form is not lawful, because it excludes piety; but in an affirmative form it would be lawful, namely, “whoever shall be received shall give such-and-such an amount.”

### What of a presbyter who sells the office of carrying holy water through his parish?

Hostiensis responds that if it is the custom that the presbyter may retain such an office, and exercise it by himself or by someone of his household and receive its proceeds, it is not simony — unless he commits it to farm, as those who give churches to farm. Otherwise it would be simony, taking simony broadly, and at the least basely.

### What if someone, under the pretext of religion, asks a presbyter that he may collect money in his parish, on condition that this presbyter have a fixed share: would he commit simony in this?

Thomas responds in a certain *Quodlibetal Question* that since the presbyter’s persuasion is that it is something spiritual, and people believe him because he is a presbyter, in such a case there is simony with respect to the presbyter, and sacrilege with respect to those giving to him, because there is a defrauding of alms contrary to the intention of the givers; and he holds both bound to restitution.

### What if someone sells the office of closing the church doors, or of ringing the bells, and the like?

Hostiensis responds that there is simony there, taking the name of simony broadly, since such offices are spiritual broadly speaking. If, however, that office has been given to someone gratis, and it is the custom of the place that he may put another in his place, and lease, sell, give to farm, or even give it as dowry to his daughter for the lifetime of those given, it is not simony. So Hostiensis. Hence concerning the office of closing the church doors it is said at 1 q. 2 c. 1, that it is not the office of a sacred gift, even if joined to it. And the gloss there says that it is then simony to buy any administration of the church, when it is annexed to something spiritual, or has customarily been performed by a cleric.
