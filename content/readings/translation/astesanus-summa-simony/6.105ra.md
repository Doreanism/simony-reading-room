What if someone simply offers his goods to a church or monastery without any pact, but with this intention—namely, that through temporal goods he may obtain spiritual ones, being unwilling to give them otherwise, nor would the clergy or monks otherwise receive him: will such a reception be simoniacal, and must what was thus accepted be restored? I answer that both he and they are simoniacs, because simony is committed by the will alone, as was said above near the beginning of this title, and according to Raymundus. Nor is the one so received bound to renounce, nor are those receiving him in this way bound to restore to anyone what was thus accepted, because this mental simony is otherwise expiated by worthy penance. *X 5.3.[mandato]*. ### Note, according to some, that money received with injury to many ought to be restored to the one who, after the giver, had the strongest claim to the thing given. And so if some entice another through avarice and fraud—so that he enters the monastery in order that they may have great wealth from him—although it was received to the injury of the monastery, nevertheless because the heirs are defrauded in this matter (who had the strongest claim after him), it ought to be restored to them; thus they understand that text *C.20 q.2 [constituit]*. ### On this point note that a prelate who extorts something from the church through simony must restore double. *X 5.3.[audivimus]*. But one who extorts through extortion (*concussio*) must restore fourfold. *C.1 q.1 [concussionis]*. Now to “extort” (*concutere*) is said of one who, under pretext of an office committed to him, extorts something from an unwilling party by threat. *C.22 q.1 [militat]*. The above has been said concerning the penalty of clerics. Laymen, however, ought to be excommunicated. *C.1 q.1 [reperiuntur]; [si quis episcopus]*.

There follows the sixth principal question: dispensation, and through whom it may be applied in matters of simony. Concerning this a distinction must be made: whether one is a simoniacally promoted simoniac, or simoniacally promoted but not a simoniac, and whether he commits simony in the order or in the benefice. Simoniacally promoted but not a simoniac is one who, when someone gives or promises money on his behalf without his knowledge—and who afterwards, when it comes to his notice, does not ratify it—is ordained or beneficed. Simoniacally promoted and a simoniac is one who, with himself or another acting on his behalf knowing of it, or afterwards ratifying it, is ordained or beneficed. With one simoniacally promoted, then, who is a simoniac in the order, the bishop cannot dispense. *X 5.3 [nobis]* in fin., but only the pope. *X 5.3 [si quis]*, in the text and the gloss, and *X de renunciatione [post transactionem]* in gloss 6.
