Thus Alexander \[of Hales\].

### But to whom shall the money paid by the buyer of the right of patronage be applied?

He replies (*1 q. 1, “Non est putanda”*, in the gloss, and *Extra, de iurepatronatus, “Quia clerici”*, in the gloss) that it shall be applied to the church to which an injury has been done by such a purchase (argument from *Extra* on this point). And so the church will be the patron of itself.

### But what if that same church had bought the right of patronage from its own patron — with whom shall that right remain, the patron or the church?

It seems that it does not remain with the church which bought it (argument from *Extra, de iurepatronatus, “Illo”*), since the sale did not stand: because a spiritual thing or one annexed to a spiritual thing cannot be sold (*Extra, eodem, “Querelam”*); and so the money will remain with the patron, restored to him. But the contrary may be gathered from the chapter cited above, where it is said that the buyer should be despoiled, not the seller. But if he had wished to despoil the seller, he would have spoken of him as of another. Or say that both ought to be deprived. But the gloss on the chapter “*Clerici*” notes this. And the last opinion is approved by Innocentius.

### Whether it is lawful to sell consecrated vessels — for example chalices, patens, and the like.

They are consecrated by reason of their form, not by reason of their matter; and therefore it is permitted to sell them by reason of their matter, without regard to their consecration — provided, however, that they not be sold by a secular person except after first being reduced to a mass. They may, however, be sold to other churches even in the same form (*19. q. 2, “Hoc ius porrectum”*). It is not so, however, with consecrated oil and chrism, because these are consecrated by reason of their matter.

### But what can or ought to be done with the wood of a consecrated church if it is destroyed?

I reply, as the apostle says (*19. q. 3*): the wood ought either to be burned in fire, or converted to the use of another church, or set aside for the benefit of the brothers in the monastery — and this not indifferently, but, as is contained according to the gloss, in religious places, such as the kitchen, the dormitory, the chapter house, and the like; not, however, in planting vineyards, nor may they be converted to the use of laymen, as is said in the same chapter “*Ligna*”, and *19. q. 3, “Quae semel”*.

### Whether it is lawful to sell ground for the use of burial.

It seems that it is not, because Effron is reproached for the fact that he sold the field in which there was a double cave, in which the dead were buried (Genesis 23). I reply: it can be said, commending what was said above (namely at the beginning of the titulus on burials), to the contrary argument — according to Alexander \[of Hales\] — that with that field, when sold, the cave passed; and this could be done without sin. Some, however, like Praepositinus, say that even that cave could be sold and bought without sin, because it had not been blessed or consecrated for burial. And according to either of these solutions, for that very reason the name was changed for Effron himself — namely, that afterwards he was called \[differently\], outside the promise.
