Raymundus, however, holds that if my enemy gave a gift to a bishop to my prejudice, and I knew of it, and some bishop summoned me to a benefice, and I protested to myself that he would not have summoned me except on account of that gift, I ought not to accept. For if someone, without my knowing or expressly speaking, secretly gave or promised money to a bishop unwilling to weigh my election or to his associates, the answer is that it does me no harm, as in *Extra*, e[odem titulo], c. *Nobis*, and c. *Sic*. It is otherwise if it is done with my knowledge and consent: for then I am said to interpose my will. And therefore, even if it is done in secret, I am bound to renounce \[the benefice\] if I wish to save my soul, *Extra*, e[odem titulo], *Matheus*. And what was said in the first case — that it does me no harm — is to be understood as true unless I afterward consent by paying the money promised, or by repaying what was paid, as in c. *Sicut*.

### But suppose I ratify what someone has given on my behalf, though I do not pay it: do I incur simony. On this point Bernardus there notes that some — like Tancredus — say that I do not incur simony, but that I sin gravely, argument from *Extra*, e[odem titulo], *Tua*, and in c. *Dictum*, where it is said by the paying of money.

### But suppose I have paid or repaid the money: who can dispense with me. Raymundus answers that the bishop can, since I was not a simoniac in the receiving of the benefice, as Tancredus notes. Bernardus also notes, on c. *Dictum*, *Sicut*, that what was said above concerning the first case — namely, that it does me no harm — is to be understood, according to Innocentius, when I have already been elected, and money is given to the confirmors without my knowledge and over my protest. But if I had not yet been elected, even though I protested, money given to the electors would harm me, since by money they would elect one whom I hate — unless the giver did this in his own interest, as in c. *Nobis fuit*. Innocentius also notes, on c. *Tanta*, that if someone obtains an order or a benefice through another’s giving money, but not to the ordainer, there is no simony, if he would have obtained it even without the money, argument from 1 q. 6 § *Si*. But if he himself gave money to the ordainer, it would be simony, even if he would have been ordained without money, argument from 1 q. 2 *Quamvis pio*, and 7 q. 6 c. 1.

### What if a beneficiary, on the matter of a provision to be made for him by the apostolic see in some church, receives an annual pension from it, or another benefice, in exchange for renouncing the first benefices he had obtained. This case is set out in *Extra*, de rescriptis, *Ad audientiam*. According to Innocentius there in the gloss, and Bernardus and Hostiensis, there is no simony there. And Hostiensis says there that, if the renunciation is made by a pact between the parties for some temporal thing, there is simony.
