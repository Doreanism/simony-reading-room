Hostiensis, however, says that the foregoing distinction must be so strictly observed that, if one of its members fails, the witness is rejected. And Bernardus also seems to feel this in the first gloss. And so Hostiensis does not admit criminous witnesses without supports even in the crime of simony, even against a regular of inferior grade. Nor does he admit those who testify not out of zeal for justice but with the kindling of malice — like capital enemies and conspirators (as in the chapter “Per tuas. i.”, §. “Nos vero”). Nor does he admit the aforesaid if proceedings are brought for deposition from grade. Nor likewise if testimony is given of a public crime to the effect that someone be removed from his administration, since then proceedings are by the rigor of the law. Likewise he does not admit them if a secular is accused, or a regular of higher grade such as a bishop, or one of inferior grade of good opinion, or one who cannot be removed without scandal. Others say that this decretal “Per tuas” is corrected by Gregorius (Extra, de testibus, “Testimonium”). But Hostiensis considers this absurd, since the decretal itself is highly reasonable. Therefore he says that the decretal of Gregorius is to be understood according to the foregoing distinction. What about an associate without an intermediary in simony — will he be admitted against the author of simony? I reply that if such a one is associate only of the crime and not of the gift, he will be admitted against him: even if proceedings are merely civil. Not, however, if proceedings are criminal. But an associate both of the crime and of the gift will not be admitted, even if proceedings are merely civil (book VI, de testibus, “Mediatoris”). And according to Iohannes Andreae this chapter is to be understood of an intermediary not amended of his crime; otherwise there would be something special in this case (from eodem titulo, “Testimonium”). Or it can be said that it is to be understood of one amended, and it is indeed special that the intermediary is admitted. ### But how shall an associate or partaker in the crime exclude \[the testimony\]? For if the accused says, “You cannot testify against me, since you were my associate in the same crime,” he will be condemned as a confessed party (Extra, de confessis, the first chapter). But if he says, “I did not do what is imputed to me; if I had done it, you could not be a witness, as one laboring under the same vice,” in this way he is lying, and the law does not teach lying (22. q. 4, “Quaepeccat”). According to Gregorius Nazianzenus, however — with whom Hostiensis also agrees — he can say: “I except against you, since I declare that you committed the crime concerning which you wish to accuse me or to testify; adding that you ought not to be heard, and that I offer myself to prove this.” For if the judge does not admit such an exception, he will appeal justly (as in Extra, de appellationibus, “Interposita”).
