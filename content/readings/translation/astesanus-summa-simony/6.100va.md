Some, however, with whom Raymundus agrees, distinguish: either one gives some temporal thing for a spiritual thing, that is, principally on account of the spiritual thing, and thus it is simony, *1, q. 1, eos in fine*, and *1, q. 7, si quis prebendas*. Or not principally, such that it would otherwise be given, and thus it is not simony, *ex c. licet questiones*, and *c. dilectus filius a, in fine*. Of which opinion Hostiensis says that it seems harsh, because it would rarely or never occur. For if some bishop of Paris is enthroned, the archdeacon of Sens has been accustomed to give him a half-mark of gold; but when otherwise would it be given to him, except when he is enthroned? Hence he believes that if it is done spontaneously without a pact, it is not simony; he also believes that if some temporal thing is given, not as a debt but as a customary gift, it is not simony, according to Hostiensis.

### It must be known, however, that not every pact entails simony.

For a possession can be given so that an oratory may be built on it, as *extra de rescriptis spontanea, olim*. And thus by a definite pact license to build it can be given, *extra de censibus, conquerente*, and *extra eodem, ex insinuato*; for such pacts are not reckoned spiritual, as in *extra de prebendis, significatum*. And in the conveyance of his own thing anyone can attach a pact in whatever manner he pleases, *extra de conditionibus appositis, nullum*.

### A case is when something is given to some priest for spiritual works,

because he is otherwise not bound to them, *12, q. 2, charitatem*. And this is true if he has not whence to live from another source, as in *1, q. 1, iudices*. And the strongest argument, *12, q. 1, illi qui*. Hence he can hire out his services for a time, as the rented-out priests \[*presbyteri educticii*\] do — or, because this sounds bad, let them be called vicars or auxiliaries, as in *extra ne prelati vices, c. quoniam frequenter* — provided, as has been said, according to Hostiensis and Bostiensis, that he direct his principal intention not toward the money but toward obtaining a stipend. But if he is bound to those works by his office — for example, because he is wedded and titled to a church — he ought to receive nothing even under the pretext of expenses, *de consecratione, di. 2, sufficit*; otherwise he would commit simony, as *1, q. 3, vendentes*. The parishioners, however, are to be compelled by the superior to pay what praiseworthy custom has introduced, *extra eodem, ad apostolicam, in fine*. And likewise to give offerings, *16, q. 1, statuimus*. If, however, he is not bound and has other expenses, he must either chant freely or cease; and if he does this for money, either there will be simony there or avarice, as Hostiensis and Raymundus say.
