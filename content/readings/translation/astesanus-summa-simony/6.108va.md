What then of the bishop who refused to give his cleric license to go to the schools unless money was given to him? Raymundus, with Laurentius, answers that this is not simony, speaking strictly of simony, but base gain, by argument from *i. q. \[3\]* “Venantes.” Robertus, however, believes that if he gives such a license for money for the five-year period conceded by the law, it is simony, *Extra* eodem *De magistris* “Super specula.” But if he gives it for another period, and has principal regard to releasing him for a time or in perpetuity from a spiritual office which he owes to the church, it is simony. If, however, \[the regard is\] to making a stay in study, it is base gain, not simony. And he believes that in the first case the one who gave may reclaim what he gave, because he is not in turpitude, since he was redeeming his own right.

Ninthly, there follows the consideration of certain doubtful matters concerning simony, so that some things omitted somewhere above may here be in some measure supplied. And first there shall be set down some doubts concerning the sacraments and certain sacramentals; second, concerning dignities and benefices; third, concerning certain spiritual works; fourth, concerning certain things annexed to spirituals. Concerning the first, then, several questions are raised.

And the first: whether it is simony if money is given for matrimony. I answer: matrimony is a sacrament consisting in the dispensation of the ministers of the church, and so it does not fall under sale; whence one who receives money exacted for the blessing of the spouses commits simony. It is also an office of nature, and so it is permitted to enter into a pact concerning it and to give and receive a price, just as for other civil and corporal offices.
