What about those who have separate cemeteries — that is, one in which no one is buried unless he is a canon or brother, and another that is common — and where it is fixed at how much an outsider may be buried in the cemetery of the brothers or canons?

I reply that it is simony (*Extra, eodem, “Audivimus”*, and the chapter “*Sicut*”), because nothing ought to be received either for the ground or for the office.

### What if the larger cross is not carried unless for a fixed price?

Hostiensis replies that this is a sign of avarice and induces the vice of simony (*Extra, eodem, “Audivimus”*).

### What if a pact is made about redeeming the bier for a fixed price before the body is brought to the church?

I reply that the bier is something temporal and therefore can be sold, redeemed, donated, and exchanged from the beginning and after the fact.

### What if the clerics refuse to bury the body unless a price is first set for redeeming the bier?

Hostiensis replies that this is a species of evil, since a pact is made about a temporal matter in respect to a spiritual one (*Extra, de rerum permutatione*, the last chapter).

### What if, after the body has been carried to the church, the friends wish to recover the bier and forcibly carry it back to their house?

Hostiensis replies that nonetheless the body must be buried gratis and honorably. But if it is the general or quasi-general custom of the church that the bier be retained by the clerics, the friends can be compelled by ecclesiastical censure to restore the bier, since such a custom is praiseworthy. And in this case and similar ones, the chapter “*Ad apostolicam*” can be understood.

### Whether to do justice for money or its equivalent is simoniacal.

I reply, according to Richardus and Raymundus, that it is so when speaking of cases where ecclesiastical justice is to be rendered — as it is given to a bishop or others for rendering judgment on spiritual matters. Rodericus likewise believes the same with respect to temporal matters, if the person is ecclesiastical, since he is bound to render judgment by reason of ecclesiastical jurisdiction. The case is otherwise if he is a lay judge, although otherwise he sins gravely (from this *i.q.z. “Vendentes”*, in the text and gloss). Yet to some, as Alexander \[of Hales\] said, it seems that even in the case of a secular judge to sell a just judgment is simony, because this is an act of virtue, and thus in some respect it is spiritual.

### Whether to conceal someone’s sin for money is simoniacal, when nonetheless it pertains to one’s office to reveal it.

He replies that it is. From *eodem, “Nemo”*, where it is said that it is simony to conceal a public sin of a subject from the prelate, not only when money or temporal gain intervenes, but also out of respect for the person, kinship, or affinity. Hence, if a priest does not notify the bishop of an incorrigible person sinning publicly, on account of the aforesaid he commits simony. And this is a spiritual matter, because the revelation of a sin — to which he was bound by office — is set under a price.
