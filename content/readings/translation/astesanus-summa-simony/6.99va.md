\[things spiritual\] by way of causation and disposition, as the sacraments, which are in a certain manner the cause of grace and the virtues. And by way of effect, as those things which belong to someone from some spiritual gift or office, such as to prophesy, to work miracles, or to celebrate the divine office. Now what is annexed to the spiritual—that is, by relation to the Holy Spirit—is so called in many ways. In one way, because in it something spiritual is administered, as sacred vessels, the church building, the cemetery. In another way, because it depends upon or follows from the spiritual, as to hold a church or an ecclesiastical dignity, a canonry or prebend, or any other ecclesiastical benefice; or the right of receiving the fruits arising from the aforesaid; or the right of receiving tithes, first-fruits, or oblations. For all these are annexed to the clerical order, since they belong to clerics alone. In a third way, because it is antecedently ordered to spiritual things, as the right of patronage, which is the power of presenting to an ecclesiastical office. One might also, in a broad sense, call what is annexed to the spiritual itself “spiritual.” The spiritual in the first sense can neither by law nor in fact be sold. But the spiritual in the second and third senses, and likewise what is annexed to the spiritual in any sense, cannot be sold by law, but only in fact—matters which will be set forth more fully below. And just as the spiritual in the aforesaid senses cannot be sold and bought, so neither can it be redeemed or resigned for money, as in *Extra*, On this, *Querelam*, and *Causa* 1, *q*. 1, *Nullus episcopus*. 2. It cannot, I say, be redeemed from him, namely, who has the right of receiving \[it\]; otherwise it can be redeemed, as tithes are redeemed from the hand of soldiers unjustly retaining them. For in all the aforesaid every promise, every condition, every pact for temporal advantage induces simony, as in *Extra*, *De pactis*, last chapter, and *Causa* 1, *q*. 2, *Quam pio*. A lawful condition, however, and one which is according to law, may be appended, as in *Extra*, *De electione*, *Significasti*.

Now the spiritual, by relation to the human spirit, is so called in two ways. In one way by essence, as the philosophical sciences, which are acquired by human investigation and teaching. In another way by cause, as the teaching of the aforesaid sciences. The sciences can neither by law nor in fact be sold. Whence Proverbs 17: “What does it profit a foolish man to have riches, since he cannot buy wisdom?” But teaching can by law and in fact be sold, of which we shall speak below. The things annexed to this spiritual, however—such as schools and books—can be sold both in fact and by common law, unless the Church has assigned the place of the school to the teacher.
