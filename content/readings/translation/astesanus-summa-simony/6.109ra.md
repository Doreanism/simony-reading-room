### Whether it is simoniacal to sell holy water or blessed bread.

Alexander of Hales and Rodingus answer that it is, because in receiving such things the remission of venial sins is conferred.

### Whether a slave who buys himself in order to be ordained, or, once ordained, to serve in his order, commits simony.

Bartholomeus answers, *di. 54*, that he does not, because there no transaction takes place over the order, over the freedom, or over the service itself. Yet a transaction can be made over the use \[of the slave\]. 26 q. 1 *Raptoribus*. Nor is there any more simony there than if someone bought a piece of land for the purpose of building a church.

### Whether simony is committed if something is given so that someone may not be ordained, or not elected, or so that an election may not be confirmed.

I answer that it is. 1 q. 1 § *Qui aut*, and c. *Quando ad finem questionis*; argument from c. *Nemo*. And the same holds if anyone, for money or for the sake of consanguinity or familiarity with any person, conceals the public sin of some prelate. The same applies if, by favoritism or partiality, he reconciles a less-than-worthy penitent, or, out of malice, fails to reconcile a worthy one — as in c. *Nemo*, in the text and the gloss. And according to Innocentius there in the gloss, one who conceals \[a sin\] through favor toward another is called a simoniac by way of analogy, since he is to be punished as a simoniac.

### What of those who by violence or favor do not allow churches to be put in order.

We decree that these are to be judged as sacrilegious, 1 q. 1 *Fit autem quidam*. And one should also understand, according to Hoffredus and commonly according to all, that they are no less to be judged as simoniacs — which is to be understood, he holds, when violence is brought to bear by virtue of a pact; otherwise not.

Concerning dignities and benefices, however, other doubts are also raised — namely, whether before promotion any security may be required, that is, a bare promise, without the vice of simony. I answer that it may not, and much less may a formal security be required — namely, by oath, by pledge, or by surety. But afterward it may, 1 q. 2 *Quam pio*, provided no pact has preceded. Other matters are treated at 8 q. 3 *Artald*. Afterward, however, the security that is approved by ancient custom is to be given, di. 63 *Quod sancta*. The superior, however, both before and after confirmation, can require the due security. *Extra*, de elec., *Significasti*. For the authority of the superior generally takes away the vice. *Extra*, de pac., *Cum pridem*.
