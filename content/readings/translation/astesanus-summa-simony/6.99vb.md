The “Gehazitic” \[seller\], however, is so called from Gehazi, the servant of Elisha, who sold the grace of healing to Naaman the Syrian, 4 Kings 5, *Causa* 1, *q*. 1, *Qui studet*. From which it appears that the seller of spiritual things is properly called Gehazitic, while the buyer is properly called simoniac. Custom, however, has prevailed that both are called simoniac—and this fittingly. For Simon wished to buy the gift of the Holy Spirit with money for this very end, that he might afterwards sell it to others; whence in him there was, as it were according to his intention, the vice of both buyer and seller.

There follows the consideration of the second point, namely, whether simony is a sin. I respond that it is, and indeed a mortal sin and very grave. Whence in *Causa* 1, *q*. 1, *Qui studet*, it is called a capital crime. For as was said in book 2, an act is evil by its kind from the fact that it is directed upon an undue matter. Now an undue matter for buying and selling is a spiritual thing. The reason for this is threefold. The first is that a spiritual thing cannot be compensated by any earthly price, as is said of wisdom in Proverbs 3: “She is more precious than all riches, and all the things that are desired are not to be compared with her.” And therefore Peter said to Simon, Acts 8: “May your money be with you in perdition, since you have thought that the gift of God may be possessed by money.” The second reason is that that alone is the due matter of sale of which the seller is the owner. But the prelate of the church is not the owner of spiritual things, but the dispenser. As is said in 1 Corinthians 4: “Let a man so account of us as ministers of Christ and dispensers of the mysteries of God.” The third reason is that selling is repugnant to the origin of spiritual things, which proceed from the gratuitous will of God. Whence Matthew 10 says: “Freely you have received, freely give.” And therefore one who in selling and buying spiritual things shows irreverence to God and to the three divine persons sins by the sin of irreligion. It is, however, compared to the sin of heresy, *Causa* 1, *q*. 1, *Qui studet*, and *Causa* 1, *q*. last, last chapter—not because it is heresy properly, but by way of similitude, because the simoniac so acts as if he held something contrary to the faith, namely, while he strives to buy and sell spiritual things for a price.

Whether in those things which induce simony the authority of the pope would excuse from simony. Bernard responds, in *Extra*, *De pactis*, *Cum predictis*, that in those things which are by their own nature simoniacal in the Old Testament—as to sell the sacraments and other such spiritual things—the authority of the pope does not excuse.
