There follows the fourth point: namely, by what penalty simoniacs are struck Concerning this, note thus, according to Hostiensis, that simoniacs are punished both by law and by judge. By law they are punished *ipso facto*: for whoever has been ordained simoniacally is *ipso facto* suspended, even however hidden it may be — according to Raymundus, Hostiensis, Hoff., and Bernardus (*extra eo. accusant.* and Innocentius on *c. ex e. tanta*). And as regards himself, namely, that he should not occupy himself with the execution of his order; and as regards others, namely, that no one should communicate with him in the execution of the order, whether his sin be public or hidden (*extra eo. tanta*). There it is also said, in aggravation of the crime of simony, that every sinner can celebrate Mass except a simoniac. “Every sinner,” I say — understand: not cut off, tolerated, penitent — can \[celebrate\] etc. Thus it ought to be supplied, according to Hoff., Hostiensis, and Bernardus in the same place. By what he says, “not cut off” — namely, from the church through a sentence of excommunication of judge or canon — he removes the objection concerning excommunicates and the suspended, who are excluded from participation in the sacraments; but by what he says, “penitent,” he removes the objection concerning him who is in mortal sin, who is suspended as regards himself (*1. q. 1. sacerdotes*). This, however, has no place, as Hoff. says, in enormous \[crimes\] or even in middling ones that are notorious by law or in fact: for crimes of this kind, even after performed penance, impede the executor of the order, both as regards himself and as regards others (*extra de temp. ordi. c. fl.* and *de renuc. nisi cum pridem*). Whence Hoff. supplies thus: “Every sinner not cut off, tolerated, penitent, can celebrate Mass — except a simoniac, and except others abandoning enormous crimes even occultly, or middling ones notoriously.” Otherwise also you will expound that passage where Bernardus’s gloss is placed. Nor does what is said in *c. ex tue fraternitatis* stand against the foregoing, where it is said that it is not safe to restrain a simoniac in his order from his office: for that is to be understood when the crime is hidden — so that the prelate does not know it except through professions \[of confession\] — and therefore he cannot prohibit him from performing his office (*extra de offi. ordi. si sacerdotes*). Damasus, however, said that a hidden simoniac in his order is suspended only as regards himself, but a notorious one as regards himself and as regards others; and that *c. ex tue* speaks of the hidden, and *c. tanta* of the notorious. But according to Hostiensis, it is better to say with the other doctors what was said before — namely, that even the hidden \[simoniac\] is suspended as regards others, because such a one has a stolen character — that is, in a broad sense, because he did not enter through the door, according to what John says: “He who enters not” etc.; and *i. q. i. ordinationes*; and the order, having arisen, has therefore fallen, as in *i. q. i.*
