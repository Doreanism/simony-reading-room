Likewise, what about a servant of religious who refuses to serve them unless they promise him that they will receive him at least after a three-year period or some other definite term, and they so promise him: is there simony in such cases? Rosella replies that in the last case such a promise is simoniacal. In the other cases set forth above, however, he does not believe that simony is committed, nor is such an agreement or arrangement unlawful, because it is properly as if he were saying: “We will receive you at such-and-such a time, provided that in the meantime you make yourself fit to be received and useful to the monastery by learning such-and-such a craft, or by gaining knowledge of such-and-such a subject.”

### What of him to whom a promise has been made of being received at a definite term under the aforesaid condition: when the term arrives and the condition has been fulfilled, is he bound to enter, since they are prepared to receive him?

Rosella says that if he made no vow nor obliged himself to them to enter, but accepted the promise of a term made to him wishing to provide for himself in the future, he is not bound to enter according to the judgment of the penitential forum. In the forum of God’s causes, however, he will be judged according to presumptions and the things alleged and proven. But also the bishop who receives a promise or oath from one to be ordained that he will not trouble him about asking for a title, and the presenter who accepts a similar pact, are punished. For each of them — namely the bishop in the ordination and the presenter in the presentation — is bound to assign a title to the one ordained, [*Extra, de prebendis, “Episcopus”*]. Therefore each is punished, for the presenter is by the law itself suspended for three years from the execution of orders, while the ordainer and the one ordained are suspended without a fixed time from the conferral and execution of the order thus received, until they merit the favor of the Apostolic See, [*Extra c. “Si quis ordinaverit”*]. Thus the *Glossa*. And it is to be understood, according to Hostiensis on *Extra “Decretalis,”* in the penitential and not the judicial forum — as if he were saying: let the ordainer and the ordained and the presenter beware of interposing and accepting such a pact, because although it is hidden and cannot be proven, they nevertheless suffer this penalty by the very fact through the canon, and so are suspended by the law itself.

But to remit is the same as to give, [*Extra, de testibus, c. “Veniens”*]: but this man — namely the presenter or ordainer — has remitted a right that belonged to him, namely that the presenters or the ordainer should make provision for him. But it seems that he ought not merely to be suspended but also deposed, as in [*Extra, eodem, c. “B.”*]. Henricus replies, on *Extra c. “Si quis,”* that such a person remits nothing, since nothing is yet owed to him, but renounces the right that he would acquire if he were presented in the due manner.
