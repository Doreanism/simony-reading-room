In the manner described, Raymundus, Hostiensis, and Bostiensis distinguish. Hostiensis, however, says that to a priest, whether he is in need or not, money can be given for his expenses, but not as gifts for the priesthood, that is, for the priestly office which he discharges, just as Hieronymus says, that grape-clusters, loaves, and a vessel of honey, if they were given to a prophet, are rather to be reckoned as for his expenses than as gifts for prophecy, 1, q. 1, iudices. Therefore when an officiating priest receives money for officiating for a time, he speaks for the church, and \[the money\] points to a cause sine qua non, or to an auxiliary cause, and not to a valuation or estimation of the office, or even of the labor he undertakes; because that labor is so annexed to that office, as some have wished to say, that it ought not to be sold or hired out, just as the office itself ought not to be — because whoever sells either of those, sells the other to himself. Hence in every case mentioned above he ought to discharge the office freely. And as Augustinus says, the apostles received bread freely from those to whom they preached freely, according to Hostiensis.

### It must therefore be noted that when it is said that some temporal thing is given for a spiritual thing,

this preposition “for” can denote a valuation or estimation, and thus it ought not to be conceded; it can also denote a final or efficient cause, and thus in either way it can be conceded, so that the sense is: he gives money for a Mass, that is, on account of veneration of the Mass — and then it denotes the end; or “for a Mass,” that is, for the celebration of the Mass, which moves him to give the money — and this denotes the efficient cause. It can also denote auxiliary support, and is also reduced to the efficient cause, as when one says: he supplies the priest with a book and chalice for celebrating the Mass; or, he gives money to the priest, and thus it can also be conceded. It must also be noted that temporal things are given to those who minister spiritual things and are received by them as a stipend of necessity, and according to approved custom, not as the price of a recompense for the spiritual things, because then simony would be committed by both givers and receivers — notwithstanding any custom, because custom does not prejudice natural or divine law, which prohibits simony, as 1 Timothy 5, on which the Gloss of Anselmus says: let him receive his stipend of dispensation from the Lord himself, the recompense for eternal life.
