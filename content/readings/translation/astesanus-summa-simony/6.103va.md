The argument is, according to Bernardus, C. 1 q. 2 § *Si quis propter hoc*. Thomas, however, said in his *Summa* that for entry into a monastery one may not receive or demand anything as a price, but for the support of the person to be received it is permissible to receive something if the resources of the monastery do not suffice for this. Likewise it is also lawful that someone be more readily received into a monastery on account of the devotion he has shown by giving large alms to it — just as, conversely, it is lawful to draw someone toward devotion to a monastery by temporal benefits, so that he may thereby be inclined to enter. But it is not lawful, by pact, to give or to take anything for entrance into a monastery (C. 1 q. 2 § *Pio*). Rosso, however, says that even to bargain in such matters, when the monastery is in need, is not simoniacal — provided it is not done for spiritual things to be conferred or received, but for expenses to be made or recompensed; whence he believes that it is permissible to specify the names and the amount of what is offered. The stronger opinion is the last, since it agrees better with the words both of the canons and of the doctors, as is plain to anyone who considers the matter. Nor is the foregoing contradicted by X eod. *Quam simoniaca*, where it says, “those who, under the pretext of poverty, wish to cloak their vice.” For this, according to Hugo and Hostiensis, is to be understood of cases where it is not so cautiously expressed but rather the receivers make a pact for a definite payment, and when they have promised in this way they then try to excuse themselves under pretext of poverty — but such a pretext does not spare an offender (X *De divortiis* *Ex litteris*). According to Raymundus and Bernardus, however, this is to be understood when one says it fraudulently. Hence those entering should be advised that they intend to give principally on God’s account, and so as not to be a burden to the house (argument from *Optimum*, C. 16 q. 6 *De lapsis*). Nor is it an objection that necessity arising from extreme hunger excuses even from theft (*De cons.* D. 5 *Discipulos*); for in such cases all things are common, but if the necessity is not so great, it does not entirely excuse (X *De furtis*, *Si quis per necessitatem*).

What of a monastery where no pact is imposed, but it is fixed how much an entering girl must give? I reply, according to Hostiensis, that such a fixed assessment is reproved (X eod. *Sicut pro certo* and c. *Intantum* at the end). Therefore, if a pact is interposed for the observance of such a custom, it is simoniacal (X eod. *Veniens* and c. *Intantum* §. *Cum vero expressius*). It is the same if she is received freely \[gratis\] and afterwards compelled to observe the custom (X eod. *Audivimus* and c. *Iacobus*). If, however, all agreement and all coercion ceasing, what is customary is given freely, simony is not committed as regards the church, but it is committed as regards the giver \[the guilty party\] — and this is expressly stated in X eod., the final c., and is held there.
