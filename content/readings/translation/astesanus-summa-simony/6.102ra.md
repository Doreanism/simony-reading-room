Landulphus, however, and Bregl. and Halo. said that, where nothing is given, simony is not committed (i. q. 2. *quam pio*). But Hostiensis understands this to apply when the kinsman is worthy, since the prelate is bound to provide for such a one rather than for a stranger (argument from extra de cohabi. cle. c. i.). If, however, he is unworthy, then \[the rule\] applies, as was said above. If, on the other hand, the prelate is moved by another’s prayers — and these carnal — then either the man is promoted to a prelacy or to a simple benefice. If to a prelacy, the prayers are unjust, especially when the petitioner is a layman whose face may be feared (extra de elec. *Osius. quisquis*), unless perhaps the prelate, paying no regard to his prayers, institutes him by virtue of his own office because the necessity or utility of the church requires it (extra de cra. 7 quali. *curam*). If, however, to a simple benefice, then either the man for whom petition is made is unworthy — and then simony is committed (as in c. *de ordinationibus*, 14. q. 1, and c. *nonnulli*) — or he is worthy, and then it is not committed, provided the prelate is not principally following the prayers (i. q. i. *quibusdam*, and the argument in c. *tuam*, 15). Ho. Raymundus, however, and Bernardus (extra de eta. *tuam*) agree on the foregoing distinction with Hostiensis and Hoff., except that on the first member — namely, when someone promotes by his own prayers — they distinguish thus: that one cannot petition for himself a church, dignity, or benefice having attached cure of souls (argument from 8. q. i. *in scripturis*, and i. q. i. *ordinationes*, and extra de iure patro. *per nostras*); but a simple benefice, if he is in need and judges himself worthy, he can request without fear of simony or pact (extra c. *ad aures*). If, however, he is unworthy, or even if not in need, he sins, as is said in the same place, and i. q. 2. *clericos*. And he calls a “simple benefice” that one which is without cure of souls or dignity. Robertus, however, says that this opinion seems harsh, whence he believes that anyone may petition on his own behalf unless he is unworthy of a benefice having attached cure of souls — by the example of Isaiah, who says, “Behold, here am I; send me.” He likewise believes that, since one who has been promoted to sacred orders \[in such a way that\] this kind of ordination requires an ecclesiastical title, he may petition an ecclesiastical benefice, even though he abound in temporal goods. The legal authorities that Raymundus adduces are speaking of the state of the primitive church, when revenues were slender and were distributed from the church’s resources according to each one’s need.
