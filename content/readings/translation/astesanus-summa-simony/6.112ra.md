It remains to consider (di. x. q. i. and last) namely, who are admitted to accuse a simoniac and to testify against him. It must be known, then, that all are admitted to accuse a simoniac, even the criminous and infamous, provided they are not enemies or conspirators. And this if the accused is a cleric of ill repute. The case is otherwise if he is a cleric of good repute (*Extra, de testibus, “Tanta”*, and *2. q. i. “In primis”*). When, however, the accuser does not prove the case, many witnesses are admitted for accusing who are excluded from giving testimony. Hence there is a difference between this and that (*Extra, eodem, “Licet”, §. “Proximo”*, at the end). Who, however, may be admitted to testify is taught us by that decretal, *Extra, eodem, “Per tuas. i.”*, which some, for greater clarity, distinguish in many ways. First, concerning the manner of proceeding: whether the case of simony is being treated criminally or civilly; and if civilly, whether by strict law or by equity. Second, they distinguish concerning the persons against whom one proceeds: whether they are religious or seculars; and if religious, whether of inferior grade or of higher dignity; and whether those same are of good opinion or ill repute, and whether they can be punished easily, or cannot be condemned without scandal. Third, they distinguish concerning the persons of the witnesses: namely, whether they are honest, or amended of their crime, or persevere in it, and whether they testify out of charity or out of hatred. Fourth, also, because crimes are objected against witnesses: whether the same or greater, or equal or lesser. Fifth, whether there are also other supports that aid the witnesses. Recall those five modes as to one side, by saying: that criminous witnesses, and especially those amended of their crime — supported by other supports — and Hostiensis believes it necessary that they be supported by other supports if they are criminous and not amended, otherwise not — and especially those not partakers in the crime, testifying out of duty, with zeal for justice, against whom lesser crimes are objected (such as theft and adultery, which are less than simony) — admit a regular of inferior grade gravely defamed, who can easily be removed without scandal. And this when the matter is treated civilly and according to the temperance of equity: not so that he be deposed from his order, but so that he be removed from his administration.
