### ¶ Concerning the gift “from the tongue” (munus a lingua)

Concerning the gift “from the tongue” it must be known that this gift, which falls under a price, is either praise itself pertaining to human favor, or entreaties by which human favor is acquired, or commendation. And therefore if anyone aims principally at this, he commits simony, because the contrary \[disposition\] is forbidden. And the same applies if anyone should aim principally that prayers on behalf of an unworthy person — namely \[unworthy\] on account of some crime or the like — be granted hearing in simony of this sort. If, however, \[the prayers are\] for a worthy person, it is not simoniacal, because there is a due cause for which something spiritual should be given to him — unless perhaps the very deed itself is simoniacal, in which case there would be simony in intention. Yet someone may simply attend to human favor rather than to a person’s worthiness, and then he commits simony if he grants the benefice to someone on account of consanguinity, when he would not otherwise have granted it — because “if anyone, by an unjust love of the heart, or by base prayers” etc. (C. 1, q. 1, si quis). Nor does the argument from the chapter nemo and the chapter cum stand against this, for if the favor of a third party constitutes a gift of favor, much more so the favor of the one who is being ordained (C. 1, q. 1, sunt nonnulli; this is noted in Extra, ut ec. bene, ut nostrum).

### ¶ According to Thomas

According to Thomas such a collation is carnal and unlawful, but not simoniacal, because nothing is received. But why is less simony committed by entreating on behalf of an unworthy person than by giving money? Bartholomeus answers (C. 1, q. 1, quibusdam) that in entreating one gives nothing and receives nothing, and so no commerce is to be understood there. But on behalf of an unworthy person one sins more gravely. Or, according to Alexander \[of\] Hales, prayers are ordered to making known a person’s worthiness, while money is ordered to moving the will of the conferrer in a disordered way. Concerning this a distinction can be drawn according to Hoff. and Hostiensis as follows: either someone is moved by his own entreaties or by another’s; if by his own, then either to a prelacy — and he is conferred \[the office\] through election, and then he commits simony, because such a person appears ambitious and unworthy (Extra, de elec., qualiter; and C. 1, q. 1, sicut). Or to a simple benefice, and then he does not commit \[simony\], provided he is worthy (Extra, eodem, tua nos at the beginning).
