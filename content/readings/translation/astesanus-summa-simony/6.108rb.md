### What if the one who has the power of granting the license to teach is himself reading \[lecturing\] in person: may he deny the license to another?

Hostiensis answers that he may not, even if the other is highly fit, by argument from *Extra, De officio archipresbyteri*, last c. For he still impedes both the teacher and the learner. For such a one might perhaps hear another whom he would not hear from him. The love for the master, the manner of teaching, and eloquence have great effect in matters of this sort. Bernardus on c. “Prohibeas” holds the same view.

### To this end it is enjoined that the statute concerning masters of theology to be appointed in each metropolitan see be inviolably observed.

And lest any be able to excuse themselves on account of the scarcity of masters, it has been decreed that some teachable men—that is, according to Bernardus, men suited for teaching—be designated by the prelates of churches for the study of theology, from whom they may afterwards make doctors; and if their own benefices do not suffice, what is necessary should be supplied to them by the aforesaid \[prelates\], namely the priests and chapters. All of this in *Extra* eodem “Super specula.” And according to Bernardus in the same place, the said teachable men ought to be elected by the chapters. And by the force of this constitution, as it seems, the prelates and chapters can be compelled to supply what is lacking to them—yet we see none of these things observed today. It is also enjoined today that at the Roman curia, Paris, Bologna, Oxford, and Salamanca, two Catholic men skilled in the Hebrew, Greek, Arabic, and Chaldean tongues are to be maintained, who shall teach others these languages, book v, c. 1, *De magistris*.
