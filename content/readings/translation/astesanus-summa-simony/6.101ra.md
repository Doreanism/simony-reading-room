But why is this not simony, since these things are spiritual? I answer that almsgiving is given to the poor on God’s behalf, who is the Lord of spiritual things; therefore simony is not committed with him, as it is with prelates, who are not lords but stewards of those things. Likewise the aforesaid acts fall under the order of merit and atone for venial sins before God through the currency of love and works of charity; this is not so with spiritual things which are conferred through the ministers of the Church. Hence corporal almsgiving is not the price of eternal life, except insofar as it is commanded by charity.

The fourth case is when something is given to redeem oneself from harassment (*Extra, eodem, dilectus filius i.*). Hence if a bishop excommunicates me unjustly, or threatens to take from me a benefice that I justly possess, I may lawfully redeem my right against him by giving him money so that he may revoke the unjust sentence or cease from this injury (argument from *Extra, de magistris, prohibemus*). Nor does the passage *Extra, eodem, querelas* stand against this, where both buying and redeeming seem to be forbidden, because there, according to Hostiensis, “to redeem” is explained as “to resign by means of money.” Nor does that passage *Extra, eodem, nemo* stand against it, because, according to Hostiensis, there it is not the harassment that is being redeemed but absolution that is being bought. Or, again according to Hostiensis and Iohannes (*Extra, eodem, dilectus i.*), it can be said that if someone justly excommunicates a person who acquired a benefice unjustly, then in either case \[simony\] is committed (*Extra, eodem, nemo*, and *C. 1, q. 1, nullus episcopus*). The second redemption of a spiritual thing, therefore, is permitted to the one unjustly harassed who redeems himself, but it is forbidden to the one harassing and to the one who receives \[the money\] in exchange for ceasing the harassment. If, however, he has excommunicated me justly, it is not lawful for me to give him money for the benefit of absolution, nor lawful for him to accept it — although it is lawful as a punishment for sin. The one absolving must take care, however, that in this he does not serve cupidity more than correction. Rodericus, however, seems to think that it is also simony if anything is given for the revocation of an unjust sentence; hence according to him the one unjustly excommunicated should complain before a superior. But the position stated above is held in common. Therefore nothing is to be given to the judge directly for absolution; the costs, however, which his adversary incurred on account of the contumacy of the accused, or which the judge incurred when proceeding *ex officio*, the accused must himself reimburse before being absolved (*Extra, ut lite non contestata, quoniam, paragraph in aliis*; and *de sententia excommunicationis, sacro*; and *de appellationibus, cum speciali*). If, however, he has been unjustly excommunicated, he ought to be absolved without any burden, as in the chapter *sacro*. Thus Berengarius, on *Extra, eodem, ad aures*.
