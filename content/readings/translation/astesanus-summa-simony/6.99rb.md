Hostiensis offers yet another reason, namely that a monk has nothing of his own, so that whoever might restore the price to him, the price would always be the monastery’s; whereas restitution is made to one who has given usury, because he has property of his own. Hostiensis again objects to the same description, because it implies that without studious cupidity to buy or sell things spiritual, or things annexed to the spiritual, simony is not committed — which is false, as will appear below. On account of these things, then, Hostiensis describes simony thus: Simony is the receiving or giving of things spiritual or of things annexed to the spiritual, where there has preceded a pact of promise, of condition, of mode, of service, of price, or of any temporal consideration whatever. This description, however, will be made clear from what is said below. Yet the foregoing description \[i.e., the standard one\] can be sustained by saying that “studious will” there means a will progressing to an external act, according to the Gloss on *i. q. i., Qui studet*. It can also be said that, since the perfection of sin lies in choice — although a person is sometimes inclined to it through weakness or ignorance — therefore simony is called a “studious will,” that it may be understood as a choosing will, not a surreptitious one. By the name of buying and selling there is understood every contract that is not gratuitous; whence even dignities, prebends, or other primary ecclesiastical benefices cannot be exchanged on one’s own authority without danger of simony, nor even compromised about (*Ex de trans. supra eod.*). A bishop, however, may do this for a necessary or useful cause (*Extra de rerum permu., Quesiti*). Now a “compromise” (*transactio*) is a non-gratuitous pact concerning a doubtful matter when litigation has begun but is not yet ended, as Roffredus says, that those are simoniacs who bargain among themselves in this manner: “You shall do what lies in your power that I obtain your benefice, and I shall do likewise for you regarding mine.” They may, however, intimate their wishes to one another by saying, “I would more gladly be in your church,” and conversely, and signify the same to the bishop. ### Further, for the elucidation of the foregoing description, it must be seen what is there called “spiritual,” and what is called “annexed to the spiritual.” Concerning which it must be known that “spiritual” can be understood in two ways, namely by comparison to the Holy Spirit, and by comparison to the human spirit. “Spiritual” understood in the first way can be spoken of in three ways: namely, by essence, as grace and the gratuitous virtues;
