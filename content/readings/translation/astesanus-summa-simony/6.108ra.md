### Whether anything may be demanded for a license to teach.

I answer that it may not, yet there is no simony there, according to Goffredus and Hostiensis. For nothing spiritual or annexed to a spiritual thing is bought or sold, but rather there is the crime of extortion. The crime of extortion occurs when someone, under pretext of his office, extorts from an unwilling party what he ought not to receive, *xxiii. q. i.* “A militare.” Hence one who has so demanded is to be stripped of dignity, office, and benefice, *Extra* eodem c. 1 and c. 6 at the end. For the license to teach ought to be granted gratis to anyone fit by the one who has the power, whether by right, by privilege, or by custom; or, if no custom appears, it seems to belong jointly to the bishop and chapter, *Extra* eodem “Quia” § “Nullum.” It is otherwise if he is not fit, since then refusal is just, by argument from the same place and c. 1. But also if anyone has delayed to set masters in suitable places, the superior shall supply the negligence of the inferior, *Extra* eodem “Prohibeas.” Although there seems to be turpitude on the part both of the giver and of the demander, so that the condition of the possessor is the stronger and the condition of the receiver the worse, *xiiii. q. v.* “Non sane,” yet in this case relief is given to the one who promises so that the promise be remitted, and to the one who pays so that what was paid be returned, *Extra* eodem “Prohibeas,” and this in odium of the one demanding. Or it may be said with Goffredus that there is no turpitude on the part of the one promising or paying. For since he is fit, which indeed is required *Extra* eodem c. 1, and is being impeded or not permitted what is his by right, it is lawful for him to redeem his vexation, *Extra, De simonia*, “Dilectus,” and Bernardus notes the same on c. “Prohibeas.”
