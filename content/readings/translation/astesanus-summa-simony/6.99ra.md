And if it is objected that usury is committed by intention alone (*Extra de usur., Consuluit*), the reply is that this is not true in the judicial forum, as is clear from c. *De usur. In civitate*; rather, in the penitential forum the usurer is induced to restore what he has thus received, as c. *Consuluit* says. If it is asked why here in the penitential forum the abbot is not likewise induced to restore, and the monk to renounce, just as the usurer is induced to restore, I answer that a distinction must be drawn. Some things are forbidden because they are simoniacal, such as buying or selling sacraments. And, in brief, all things that were simoniacal in the Old and New Testaments — for these the will alone suffices to introduce the sin, whether it remains within its own bounds or proceeds further to the act of buying or selling, or even of bargaining. But it does not suffice to introduce irregularity unless it proceeds to the act of bargaining. Other things, however, are simoniacal because they are forbidden by the Church — for instance, when someone renounces his church on this condition or by this pact, that it be given to whomever pleases him; and in such cases the will alone does no harm, but the act of bargaining is required to introduce the sin (*Extra de offi. ele., Ex parte*; and *De renun., De multa*). For one does not sin who wishes to renounce, or even actually renounces, a benefice for the use of another, provided that other be worthy, so long as no pact intervenes. Hostiensis, on those things which are simoniacal because of the Church’s prohibition, gives the example of the sale of a benefice, saying that this was not forbidden in the Old Testament; today, therefore, it is simoniacal because forbidden. Hence today it is simoniacal to sell or trade a stewardship (*gastaldia*), an *yconomatum*, an advowson, and the like (*Extra* eod., *Ad nostram*; *i. q. i., Si quis*; and q. iii, *Salvator*). And in these the will alone does no harm, but an act is required as regards the Church, according to Hostiensis. The aforesaid distinction concerning what is simoniacal is, however, set forth commonly by both modern and ancient doctors, and is set forth by Henricus \[of Susa\] (*Extra de offi. ele., Ex parte*) — that is, by Hostiensis. Replying, then, to the question proposed, he says that in the same manner that is observed concerning usury, which is condemned by both Testaments, so likewise will it be observed concerning simoniacs in the first sense described. And in this way can be understood that text *i. q. i., Qui studet*, and c. *Reperiunt*, and the like. Concerning simoniacs in the second sense described, however, *Extra* eod. c. ult. is speaking.
