But if a right has been acquired for him, it is lawful — not indeed by way of \[purchasing\] the election, but by way of redeeming himself from the unjust harassment (*Extra, eodem, dilectus filius i.*). What if someone who clearly had no right at all in the election, before the election, were to impede it from taking place — could the chapter redeem itself from his harassment? Rodericus answers that it could, namely if he were impeding the election from happening at all; if he were impeding the election from falling on this or that particular person, then perhaps the chapter could give nothing to the one impeding, just as neither could the person whose election he was impeding.

The fifth case concerns consecration and visitation. For if a bishop consecrates a church, or visits a diocese, he may receive a moderate procuration (*Extra, de censibus, cum apostolus*; and *Extra, eodem, cum sit romana*; and *de prescriptionibus, cum ex officii*; and *C. 10, q. 3, relatum*). And nonetheless he seems to do this gratis (argument from *Extra, de vita et honestate clericorum, cum ab omni*). You should not, however, understand this to mean that anything is exacted for the consecration itself — otherwise it would be against *C. 1, q. 1, statuimus* — but rather for the labor of the journey (*C. 12, q. 2, charitate*), even if the church is in the city (*Extra, de censibus, venerabilis*), according to Hostiensis. According to Hostiensis in the *Summa*, however, since such labor is spiritual — labor which the prelate is bound to undertake of necessity and by virtue of the office enjoined upon him — it is better to say that this proceeds from custom (*Extra, eodem, ad apostolicam*) and from equity, namely that the one who sows spiritual things should reap also temporal things (as is clear from many examples, *C. 12, q. 2, paragraph quia ergo*); or rather, this arises from the connection \[between the spiritual and temporal\], as in *Extra, de censibus, cum venerabilis*. According to Berengarius and Hostiensis in the gloss on *Extra, c. cum sit*, the aforesaid procuration is permitted by law as quasi-licit, because no sin is committed under the authority of the law (*C. 23, q. 4, quod peccat*). And it must be done according to the resources of the church, like other levies (*Extra, de censibus, sopite*). Indeed, even if the church cannot afford it because of poverty, the bishop shall consecrate the church at his own expense (*C. 1, q. 1, placuit*).

But why is procuration given to the bishop for the consecration of a church and not for the ordination of clerics, since there too there is labor? I answer that the bishop can ordain in his own church and can easily have his expenses at his own house, but it is not so when he consecrates or visits another church.
