For in the Preacher’s case it is one and the same thing to ask or receive expenses gratis—namely, for God’s sake alone—and to ask or receive them as a debt, that is, by the Lord’s Right; for they are owed by the debt of charity. Hence on that text of 1 Timothy 5, *Let the presbyters who rule well be held worthy of double honor,* the Gloss says, *It is a matter of necessity for the Preacher to accept that whence he lives, and of charity to provide it.* The bread, therefore, owed to Preachers, since it is owed out of charity, is gratuitous, and ought to be called gratuitous. Hence Augustinus in the same book, chapter 7: *The Apostles, just as the Lord appointed, living from the Gospel, ate gratuitous bread from those to whom they preached gratuitous grace.* Now the Preachers’ bread is called gratuitous, although it is owed out of charity, because it is received without bodily labor, as Augustinus says in the same book, chapter 3, in these words: *That, working only spiritually*—namely, by preaching—*they may eat bread gratuitous from bodily labor.* See, then, that the Preachers’ bread is gratuitous, and is called gratuitous in the Scriptures, although it is owed by the Lord’s Right. It remains, therefore, that the Preacher who has been sent, when he asks for or receives expenses from his hearers, although he accepts them only for God’s sake, yet does not accept them by way of begging, but by way of power; and in so taking he uses the Apostolic power. Falsely, therefore, do such Preachers speak when they say that they receive expenses from their hearers not by way of power \[*potestative*\] but by way of begging \[*mendicative*\], on the grounds that they ask for them for God’s sake.
